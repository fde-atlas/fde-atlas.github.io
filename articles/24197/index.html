Summary

This Japanese article presents a practical framework for system handovers, aimed at forward deployed engineers. It argues a handover is a transfer of responsibility, not of documents: it is complete only when the successor can independently run operations, make a small change, and has become the contact point for inquiries. The author proposes a four-layer inventory (system, operations, business knowledge, stakeholders) and a five-stage process (inventory, planning, knowledge transfer, parallel running, full transfer). It also lists five essential document items, factors determining handover duration, how FDE handovers differ from SIer and internal IT ones, and common failure patterns with countermeasures.

Key points

  • Completion is judged by the successor independently handling incidents and minor changes, not by documents delivered.
  • Handover targets span four layers: system, operations, business knowledge, and stakeholders.
  • A five-stage process runs from inventory through parallel running to full transfer of authority and contact routes.
  • Among five required document items, a decision log explaining why specs exist is the most valuable.
  • FDE handovers must also transfer business judgment and customer trust, designed for customer self-sufficiency from day one.

What is a system handover? What counts as a completed handover?

A system handover is not finished just by handing over documents. It is complete when the successor can operate, change, and make decisions on their own.

A system handover is the process of transferring operational responsibility for a system from the predecessor to the successor (or another team, or the customer) when there is a personnel transfer, resignation, contract end, or organizational change. If you treat the handover as "done" merely by passing over documents and accounts, inquiries will bounce back to the predecessor when the first incident or change request arrives. At FDE Soken, we view handover as a "transfer of responsibility," with knowledge transfer positioned as one of the means to achieve it.

Think of the handover scope in "4 layers"

Divide the handover scope into 4 layers: system, operations, business knowledge, and stakeholders. This reduces omissions and makes it easier to judge whether the handover is complete.

LayerMain contentsEasily overlooked items
System layerArchitecture diagrams, repositories, infrastructure, permissions, external integrationsKeys and tokens tied to personal accounts, manually changed settings
Operations layerScheduled jobs, monitoring, incident response procedures, release proceduresLow-frequency tasks such as annual or quarterly work
Business knowledge layerBusiness flows, data meanings, background of specificationsThe reasoning behind decisions such as "why this specification exists"
Stakeholder layerCustomer contacts, vendors, internal approversImplicit routes such as who to consult with to get things approved

The system layer is visible, so it is easy to turn into documentation. On the other hand, the business knowledge and stakeholder layers often exist only in the predecessor's head. If the handover ends with these layers left behind, the successor ends up in a state of "can touch it but cannot make decisions."

Judge completion by "whether the successor could handle it independently"

Completion is not judged by "whether the documents are complete." It is judged by whether the successor could handle incident response and minor modifications on their own.

In practice, we find it manageable to consider the handover complete when the following 3 conditions are met.

  • The successor completed one full cycle of routine operations (scheduled jobs and monitoring) without the predecessor's assistance
  • The successor independently completed one small change from design through release
  • Inquiries from customers and related departments now come directly to the successor rather than the predecessor

The third point is easily overlooked. Even if the technical transfer is complete, responsibility has not shifted unless the destination for inquiries has been switched.

What is the procedure for a system handover? What are the 5 stages at a glance?

Proceeding with a system handover in 5 stages—"inventory → planning → knowledge transfer → parallel run → full handover"—helps suppress omissions and rework.

StagePurposeMain tasksExit condition
1. InventoryFinalize the handover scopeList assets, tasks, and stakeholders for each of the 4 layersPredecessor and successor agree on the target list
2. PlanningDecide the timeline and completion criteriaSet the parallel-run period, priorities, and completion criteriaSchedule and criteria shared with stakeholders
3. Knowledge transferReach a state of "knowing"Documentation, walkthroughs, Q&ASuccessor can explain the overall picture in their own words
4. Parallel runReach a state of "being able to do it"Successor as lead, predecessor as reviewerSuccessor independently completes routine operations and minor modifications
5. Full handoverSwitch responsibilityReorganize permissions, change the inquiry contact point, post-handover follow-upInquiry routes to the predecessor are closed

Stages 1 and 2: Decide "what and by when" through inventory and planning

In the first two stages, agree in writing on the handover scope and completion criteria. Skipping this leads to later disputes over whether the handover is finished.

During the inventory, check not only what the predecessor can recall but also job scheduler registrations, cloud billing statements, and monitoring alert notification destinations. Tasks the predecessor has forgotten are often discovered from such actual data. In planning, decide the completion criteria before the timeline. With criteria in place, you can decide what to prioritize and cut when things fall behind.

Stages 3 and 4: Verify "being able to do it" through knowledge transfer and the parallel run

Knowledge transfer creates a state of "knowing," and the parallel run creates a state of "being able to do it." Skipping the parallel run makes the handover likely to end as a formality.

In knowledge transfer, a "reverse walkthrough," where the successor operates the screen while explaining back, is effective, rather than having the predecessor only explain. During the parallel-run period, switch roles early: the successor becomes the lead and the predecessor moves to a reviewer role. If the predecessor keeps doing the hands-on work, the parallel-run period will pass with the successor remaining a spectator.

Stage 5: In the full handover, reliably switch the contact point and permissions

In the full handover, explicitly switch the inquiry contact point and permissions, and set a deadline for post-handover follow-up to draw a clear line.

At this stage, collectively remove the predecessor's permissions, change shared account passwords, and notify stakeholders of the change in responsibility. In the case of a personnel transfer, providing a time-limited follow-up channel, such as "answering questions for a certain period after the handover," lowers the successor's psychological burden. In the case of resignation or contract end, assume follow-up is not possible and strengthen stages 3 and 4.

What should handover documents contain? What are the minimum required items?

The minimum required items for handover documents are 5: "overall picture, operating procedures, known issues, contacts, and decision background." Prioritize ease of updating over comprehensiveness.

The 5 minimum required items

For each of the 5 items, writing backward from "situations where the successor would struggle in the first month" produces a practical document while keeping the volume manageable.

ItemWhat to writeTypical situations where the successor struggles
Overall pictureArchitecture diagram, data flow, external integration pointsNot knowing the scope of impact during an incident
Operational proceduresRoutine tasks, releases, first-response to incidentsNot knowing the order of month-end processing
Known issuesUnresolved bugs, technical debt, workaroundsTreating known issues as new incidents
ContactsCustomer, vendor, and internal contacts and their rolesNot knowing who to ask
Rationale for decisionsWhy major specifications and designs were chosenNot being able to judge whether a specification can be changed

A decision log recording "why we did it this way" is the most effective

Among the 5 items, the rationale for decisions has the highest value. If the successor understands why a specification exists, they can judge for themselves whether it can be changed.

By reading the code and configuration, you can learn "what is being done." But "why it was done that way" is, in most cases, recorded nowhere. Even a single sentence like "we chose this time to match the customer's accounting close" or "we added this check in response to a past incident" reduces the risk of the successor accidentally breaking a specification. From hands-on experience, writing out 10 decision log entries tends to reduce post-handover inquiries more than carefully drawing detailed architecture diagrams.

Don't over-document; put it where it gets updated

If handover documentation is too extensive, it won't be read and won't be updated. The realistic approach is to keep it short and place it where the successor opens it daily.

Rather than creating one large dedicated document all at once, splitting it across places you normally use, such as the repository's README or an operations wiki, keeps it updated even after the handover. Writing the last updated date and the updater at the top of the document lets the successor judge whether the information is stale.

How should the handover period be decided? What conditions vary with the setup and scale?

Don't decide the handover period based on a number of days. Work backward from whether the major business cycles, such as monthly processing, can be completed once in parallel operation.

For example, for a system with monthly closing processes, schedule the parallel period so that at least one closing is included. For quarterly or annual tasks that won't come around during the parallel period, keep past execution logs and screen recordings in addition to procedure documents.

5 conditions that affect the period

The period lengthens or shortens based on 5 conditions: the successor's experience, system scale, the predecessor's continued presence, the state of documentation, and customer involvement.

ConditionState that allows a shorter periodState that calls for a longer period
Successor's experienceHas experience with the same tech stack and industryNew to both the technology and the business domain
System scaleSingle system, few integration pointsMany external integrations, multiple environments
Predecessor's presenceTransferred internally and available for questions after the transitionRetired or contract ended, unreachable after the transition
State of documentationOperational procedures and decision logs are in placeRelying on the predecessor's memory
Customer involvementInternal system with few stakeholdersCustomer-embedded setup where building relationships with stakeholders is also needed

Here are examples of how to think about each condition. If the successor is a less experienced engineer, weight parallel operation more heavily than knowledge transfer. If the customer includes overseas locations and communication in English is required, arrange early meet-and-greets with stakeholders in addition to the technical handover. With large enterprise customers, there are many approvers and related departments, so budgeting extra time for mapping out the stakeholder landscape prevents last-minute panic.

When the predecessor is unavailable or leaving soon, prioritize "reproduction"

If the predecessor has little time left, prioritize having the successor reproduce the work by hand over listening to explanations.

This is because time spent only listening to explanations tends to lose its value once the predecessor is gone. In a limited period, have the successor work hands-on while the predecessor supplements from the side, and record the session for later reference. For anything that cannot be completed, explicitly list it as an "untransferred items list," report it to management, and re-prioritize.

How does this differ from handovers at SIers or in-house IT departments? What perspectives are unique to FDEs?

In an FDE handover, you must transfer not only the system but also the business judgment at the customer site and the trust of stakeholders. This is what distinguishes it from SIers and in-house IT departments.

An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role known as a working style popularized by Palantir, who embeds at the customer's site and handles everything from problem definition through implementation and operations. Therefore, the handover content includes "how the customer's business is understood and how much judgment authority has been delegated."

Comparing handovers: SIer vs. in-house IT vs. FDE

The differences among the three mainly appear in three points: the basis of the handover, the recipient, and the focus. FDE Soken compares them along these three axes.

PerspectiveSIerIn-house ITFDE
Basis of handoverContract, deliverables, maintenance scopeInternal regulations, assigned responsibilitiesAgreement with the customer and trust built on-site
Main handover recipientMaintenance vendor, customer's IT departmentInternal successorAnother FDE, or the customer's own staff
FocusCompleteness of deliverables and specification documentsContinuity of operationsContext of business judgment and stakeholder relationships
Impact of failureMisalignment over the maintenance scopeOperational mistakes, knowledge silosLoss of customer trust, halt of the improvement cycle

SIer handovers have a clearly defined contractual scope, but implicit knowledge outside that scope tends to fall through the cracks. FDE handovers tend to have a vaguer scope, but if the business context is conveyed, the successor can more easily continue improving things.

An FDE-specific perspective: design the "handover to the customer" from day one

Since the ultimate handover recipient for an FDE is often the customer itself, design the handover with internalization in mind from the very first day.

Ideally, an FDE's involvement ends once the customer can run things on their own. Rather than writing the handover documentation all at once at the end, write the operational procedures together with the customer's staff partway through the project and share the decision log; this makes the final transition lighter. From the practical perspective of the FDE Soken editorial team (VACAN), deciding early and by name "which customer person will take over which judgments" reduces confusion in the final phase. However, depending on the customer's structure and staffing, internalization may not be realistic, in which case consider combining it with a transition to a maintenance vendor.

Cases where an FDE-style handover is not a good fit

For small-scale systems with little room for business judgment, a heavy FDE-style handover can be overkill.

For standardized operational systems with fixed specifications, or projects where the maintenance scope is strictly defined by contract, SIer-style deliverable-centered handovers are more efficient. Rather than asking which approach is correct, it is realistic to choose based on whether the system "keeps changing" and "involves judgment calls."

What are the cases where system handovers tend to fail?

Most failures come down to three things: "skipping side-by-side operation," "not documenting tacit knowledge," and "not defining completion criteria."

Typical failure patterns and preventive measures

By understanding failure patterns together with the stage at which they occur and the preventive measures, you can take action at the planning stage.

Failure patternStage where it tends to occurPreventive measure
Skipping side-by-side operation and handing over with documents onlyStage 4Run through the main business cycles at least once via side-by-side operation
The rationale behind decisions is not recordedStage 3Make the decision log mandatory as one of the five items
No completion criteria, so the handover drags onStage 2Agree on "the successor completing it independently" as the criterion
Personal account permissions remainStage 5Create a permission list at the inventory stage and verify it at transfer time
Infrequent tasks get missedStage 1Identify tasks from job registrations, billing details, and past logs
Notification to the customer about the change of contact person is delayedStage 5Schedule a meeting with the customer during the side-by-side operation period

Verification questions to detect a "thought-I-handed-it-over" situation

Whether the handover is truly complete can be checked by asking the successor three questions. Any item where they struggle to answer is within the scope not yet transferred.

  • If a failure occurs tomorrow morning, what do you check first, and who do you contact?
  • If a customer requests a specification change, which specifications can be changed freely, and which should be handled with care?
  • What tasks will occur for the first time next month or next fiscal year?

These three questions are meant to be asked while the predecessor is still on board. From on-the-ground experience, even when successors can answer technical questions, many get stuck on the second question, which shows that this is where the decision log should fill the gap. A handover will never be perfect in one pass. If the scope not yet transferred is made explicit and shared with stakeholders, that too can be considered one valid form of "completion."
