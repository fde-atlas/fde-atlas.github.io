The FDE Frontier: Designing Forward Deployed Teams That Compound

Michael Couch | couch.cx · 2026-10-26 · 9 min read

Summary

Michael Couch argues that companies copied Palantir's FDE job title but not the operating system behind it, producing staff augmentation, SWAT teams, or center-of-excellence theater that never compounds. He proposes designing FDE work as a triad: a domain operator who owns the outcome, an FDE who builds the running system in the operator's environment, and a platform owner providing identity, model routing, logging, and golden paths. He suggests one platform pod per eight to twelve FDEs, residencies of 90–180 days with written exit conditions, and deliverables like production harnesses, evals, runbooks, and kill switches. Governance should travel with the FDE as defaults, and success should be measured by time to first production path, reuse, operator lift, and platform pull rather than use-case counts.

Key points

  • Copying the FDE title without org design yields theater, not compounding results.
  • Design unit is a triad: operator, FDE, platform owner.
  • Suggested ratio: one platform pod per 8–12 FDEs.
  • Residencies run 90–180 days with written exit conditions.
  • Measure production paths, reuse, operator lift, and platform pull.

Palantir named the job. The rest of the industry copied the title. Almost nobody copied the operating system. That is the frontier now: not whether your company has Forward Deployed Engineers, but whether you designed an FDE team that can survive contact with a real enterprise, P&L owners, regulators, legacy systems, and a product org that still believes the backlog is the business.

The last two years made the copy-paste inevitable. Labs, clouds, and consultancies all discovered the same gap: models that can reason, and companies that cannot absorb them. The FDE became the human adapter. Sit with the customer. Build in their stack. Leave something running. Then the enterprise did what enterprises do, it hired the label into a center of excellence, put it under IT, measured utilization, and wondered why nothing compounded.

SCQA (at a glance)

Situation: Implementation is cheap, domain context is not, and the old product org is too far from the work. Complication: Enterprises staff "FDE teams" as SWAT, staff-aug, or CoE theater, proximity without ownership. Question: How do you design a forward deployed function that compounds instead of touring? Answer: Embed against outcomes, ship harness not slides, ratio FDE to platform to operator, and treat governance as something they carry, not a gate they wait on.

What FDE actually is (and is not)

A Forward Deployed Engineer is not a sales engineer with a nicer laptop. They are not a contractor you park in a war room until the RFP is won. They are a builder who lives in the domain long enough to change how work runs, then leaves behind a system the operator can keep.

Palantir's original insight was brutal and correct: the hard problem is not the platform. The hard problem is the last mile of reality. Schemas that lie. Approvals that live in email. The plant that still faxes. The brand team whose "source of truth" is a SharePoint nobody owns. You cannot product-manage that from a central backlog. You have to stand in it.

That is the same physics I wrote about in The Corporate Impotence Cycle and The End of the Monolithic Product Org. Authority without proximity produces summaries. Central product orgs produce averages. FDE is the structural answer: build capacity at the edge, with a platform behind it, the inverse of a transformation programme that flies in, decks the room, and leaves.

Why this is the frontier now

Two constraints flipped at once. First, implementation stopped being scarce. A serious operator with an agentic IDE ships in days what used to be a capital request. Second, the things that remain scarce, taste, domain fidelity, permission to change the workflow, still live with the people who do the job. Labs noticed. They staffed deployment engineers because the model does not install itself into a bank, a network, or a holding company.

Enterprises noticed the title and missed the ratio. They hired five "FDEs" into a digital CoE, pointed them at twenty business units, and asked for a roadmap. That is not forward deployed. That is a touring band. The frontier is org design: how many, where they sit, what they are allowed to leave behind, and how the center stays a platform instead of becoming another queue.

AI did not create FDE. It made the cost of not having it indefensible. When the harness matters more than the horse, someone has to wire the harness into the actual workflow. That someone is FDE, or it is shadow IT with a better GitHub.

The unit of design is the triad, not the hero

Do not design a team of lone wolves. Design a triad that can finish a loop:

  • Domain operator, owns the P&L or the workflow. Names the outcome. Stays after the FDE leaves. Without this person, you are building for a ghost.
  • Forward deployed engineer, translates the workflow into a running system. Writes the glue, the evals, the permissions, the boring integration. Lives in the operator's tools, not in a parallel sandbox that never ships.
  • Platform / harness owner, sits in the center. Provides identity, model routes, logging, policy-as-code, golden paths. Does not take tickets for "please automate my team." Makes the FDE faster and safer.

That is a micro-team with a job. One to three people, not a two-pizza theatre. The FDE is the hinge. If you staff only FDEs, you get mercenaries. If you staff only platform, you get a catalog nobody adopts. If you staff only operators, you get slideware and a graveyard of pilots.

A workable starting ratio inside a large enterprise: one platform pod per eight to twelve FDEs, and one FDE per serious domain residency, not per "initiative." If an FDE is covering four business units, they are a visitor. Visitors do not change operating models.

Residency, not rotation-as-a-service

The useful tour is a residency: ninety to a hundred and eighty days against a named outcome, with a written exit condition. "Invoice exceptions no longer require a human for the happy path." "The brand team ships a locked campaign pack without opening a ticket." "The analyst's weekly pack is a system, not a Sunday night."

Rotation is fine after the harness exists. Rotation as the default is how you train people to start and never finish. The anti-pattern is the internal consultancy: FDEs hop, the operator never learns the system, and the platform team inherits twelve half-built snowflakes.

Exit is part of the design. The FDE should leave behind: a running workflow, an owner, evals that fail loud, and a path to extend without calling the original human. That is how this connects to the fractional digital employee, you are not selling hours. You are installing a capability that keeps working.

What they ship

If the artifact is a deck, it was not an FDE engagement. If the artifact is a prototype on a laptop that dies when the FDE goes on leave, it was theatre. The ship list is unglamorous and specific:

  • A harness in production, interface, memory, permissions, the actual system of record, not a demo against CSV.
  • Skills and runbooks the operator can compose, filesystem-addressable, versioned, not tribal knowledge in a Slack DM. Same instinct as skills over agent armies.
  • Evals on the workflow, golden cases from real exceptions, not a vibe check on Friday.
  • Observability the center can see, traces, cost, failure modes, who approved what. If it cannot be audited, it cannot scale in an enterprise.
  • A kill switch and a human path, the operator can stop it, override it, and explain it to a regulator or a client.

This is why FDE sits next to agentic infrastructure rather than next to innovation theatre. A real lab is a foundry, access, harness, witness, so change-makers can try in the light. FDE installs the last mile. If your FDE team reports into a lab that only explores, you have already decided they will not own production.

Where they sit on the org chart

Put them too close to sales and they become pre-sales. Put them too close to a central product org and they become a faster ticket queue. Put them only in the business with no platform and they become shadow IT with better models.

The design that works: solid line to a deployment or domain-engineering lead who is measured on outcomes in production, dotted line into the business unit for the residency, and a hard contract with the platform team for golden paths. Compensation should look more like product and less like billable utilization. If you bonus FDEs on hours in meetings, you will get meetings.

Career path is not optional. The job burns people out if the only promotion is "more accounts." Sequence it: residency FDE → lead who designs two or three triads → platform or domain owner who productizes what kept working. Some will rotate back into a business unit as the technical operator. That is a feature. You just trained the IC the org actually needed.

Governance they carry, not governance they wait on

The CoE instinct is to freeze FDE behind review boards. That recreates the monolith. The adult version is the same split I argued for in the product-org essay: non-negotiables in the pipeline, preferences out of the forum.

Data residency, identity, model routing, logging, secrets, eval gates, those travel with the FDE as defaults. They should not be able to ship a workflow that bypasses them even if they want to. Everything else, which exception to automate first, what the UI looks like, whether the weekly pack is a Slack message or a page, belongs to the triad.

If every residency needs a steering committee to choose a use case, you do not have an FDE function. You have a transformation office with engineers. Use cases should be chosen by the operator who feels the pain, inside a menu the platform has already made safe.

How you know it is working

Stop reporting "AI use cases in flight." Measure the loop:

  • Time to first production path, not demo. A real operator using it on a real case.
  • Reuse, how many later residencies started from a skill, a connector, or an eval the last one left. If reuse is zero, you are funding artisanal snowflakes.
  • Operator lift, hours returned, exception rate, cycle time. Named in the exit condition before the residency starts.
  • FDE leverage, residencies completed per FDE per year without quality collapse. If the number only goes up by shortening the tour, you are cheating.
  • Platform pull, how often FDEs use the golden path versus forking. Forks are signal. If everything is a fork, the platform is a brochure.

This is closer to how you would run a product than how you would run a professional-services bench. That is the point. FDE is product at the edge.

The failure modes (you already have them)

Staff aug in a hoodie. FDEs billed against a programme, swapping in and out, no exit condition. You bought extra hands. You did not change the operating model.

The SWAT team. A heroic squad that lands on fires, screenshots the win, and leaves the operator with a tool they cannot extend. Heroics do not compound.

CoE theatre. A center that owns the narrative, the vendor relationships, and the quarterly showcase. The work happens in PowerPoint because production still belongs to someone else.

Platform as bottleneck. Every residency waits on a central backlog for identity, data, or model access. You rebuilt the monolithic product org and called it enablement.

No operator in the triad. IT "sponsors" the work. The business watches. Six months later nobody uses it because nobody who does the job was in the room when the workflow was encoded.

If you recognize your company in more than one of those, you do not need more FDEs. You need a redesign.

How to stand it up without a two-year operating model

Start narrower than the strategy deck wants. Pick two residencies in domains that already feel the pain and have an operator who will stay. Staff each as a triad. Give the platform pod one job: make those two residencies boring to ship, auth, logs, model route, eval harness. Time-box 120 days. Write the exit condition in a paragraph, not a RACI.

After the first two, productize whatever got copied. That becomes the beginning of a skill library, not a slide about synergy. Then add FDEs only as fast as you have operators and platform pull. Hiring FDEs ahead of operators is how CoEs bloat.

Vendors will offer you their FDEs. Sometimes that is how you learn the motion. It is not a substitute for owning the triad. If the only people who can change the workflow leave when the statement of work ends, you rented a demo.

The frontier is not a new job title. It is whether the enterprise can put build capacity next to the work, leave a harness, and refuse to confuse proximity with a tour. Design the triad. Measure the loop. Let the center be a platform. Everything else is a CoE with better merch.
