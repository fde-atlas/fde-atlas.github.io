Palantir Forward Deployed Engineer: The Role and How to Prepare for the Interview

Prepare.sh · 2026-11-04 · 6 min read

Summary

This Prepare.sh guide explains Palantir's Forward Deployed Software Engineer (FDSE) role, internally called Delta, and how to prepare for its interviews. As of November 4, 2026, Palantir's job board listed 65 titles starting with Forward Deployed, mostly government-related, some requiring security clearances or citizenship. Posted base pay ranges from $135,000–$145,000 for new grads to $200,000 for government roles, with travel from 25% to 75%. The interview centers on decomposition—turning vague problems like detecting money laundering into concrete solutions—plus debugging, coding, system design, and behavioral rounds. The author stresses FDSEs write production code, are not consultants, and must show they can connect data to value.

Key points

  • Palantir originated the FDE title; internally FDSEs are called Deltas.
  • 65 Forward Deployed titles were listed in November 2026, mostly government roles.
  • New grad pay is $135k–$145k; government roles reach $200k.
  • The key interview round is decomposition: break vague problems into working solutions.
  • FDSEs write production code and join on-call; framing the job as consulting hurts interviews.

Palantir is where the forward deployed engineer title started. Its version, the Forward Deployed Software Engineer (FDSE), is "a software engineer who embeds directly with our customers to configure Palantir's existing software platforms to solve their toughest problems." Internally, FDSEs are called Deltas.

This page covers what's specific to the FDSE path: which role you're actually applying for, what the job requires, and how to prepare. For the full company process, rounds and timeline, see our Palantir interview guide.

One title, many jobs

Palantir's job board had 65 titles beginning "Forward Deployed" on November 4, 2026. Most are government-related.

That grouping is our reading of the titles and postings. They split by specialty too:

Family Examples
Software Engineer (44 titles) US Government, UK Government, Intel, Federal Health and Civilian, Tactical Edge, Edge Autonomous Systems, Japan, Korea, Germany
AI Engineer US, UK
Infrastructure Engineer US and UK Government
Reliability, Security US, UK; Security is US Government
Other Mixed Reality, Enablement Engineer (Customer Success)

Check eligibility first, because some of these rule you out regardless of skill:

  • "Active Full Scope Poly level US security clearance" (Intel)
  • "Active Top Secret Clearance with eligibility and willingness to obtain SCI." (Korea and Japan Forward Deployed)
  • "Must be a US citizen or green card holder" (Reliability)
  • "Sole British National" (Infrastructure, UK)

Early career is a real route here, unusually for this title. 14 titles are New Grad and 13 are internships. The US FDSE posting asks for "1+ years of relevant, post-college work experience", and the New Grad commercial posting asks you to be "graduating in December 2026 or Spring 2027."

Palantir also describes relocation-style deployments. One engineer wrote: "I deployed to Brazil on two weeks' notice." That's from 2019, so ask how it works today.

What the job is

Palantir's own framing: a Dev's focus is "one capability, many customers", and a Delta's is "one customer, many capabilities". And "A common misconception is that a Delta is a consultant." You write code that runs in production. The Infrastructure posting mentions "Participate in a support on-call schedule."

A posting gives a good sense of the starting point: "Projects often start with a nebulous question like 'Why are we losing customers?'" The AI Engineer posting says the "responsibilities look similar to those of a hands-on AI startup CTO", and asks you to "contribute learnings from the field back to the Palantir AIP product suite".

You'd also be doing what the day in the life post describes: "learn to work with systems, architectures, and codebases you are not familiar with".

What's known about the interview

First-party. Palantir says the process is tailored: "The number and format of interviews that you'll go through will be personalized to your skillset". "Everyone starts with one or two phone interviews", each "typically lasting 20 to 45 minutes". For early career, it says the process varies by role (FDSE or SWE). It doesn't publish a round list for FDSE.

Candidate reports, each a single account:

  • A London applicant wrote in 2022 that "The Virtual Onsite round had 3 1hr interviews scheduled consecutively in a 3-hour window: Decomposition, Coding and Learning", and that for both SWE and FDSE, "they'd interview me for both and decide at the end".
  • An FDSE intern candidate in London in 2019 reported "3 technical interviews: debugging, coding and system design", with problems including a broken BFS.

Formats change, and those are years old, so ask your recruiter. Some applications ask for written answers or a video response. One UK posting says of its application questions, "We read them."

Decomposition

This is what the role is about. Palantir describes the skill: "'Technical decomp.' Our job is to take a high-level problem, 'decompose' or break it down, and design a solution." Our Palantir guide covers the round in general. Here's a worked example using a line from one of its posts.

"How can we more effectively identify instances of money laundering?"

This appears in a Palantir posting as an example of a customer's problem. It's a practice prompt, not a reported interview question.

Don't start with a model. Palantir's advice: "articulate the alternatives and trade-offs, but don't forget to be pragmatic enough to be able to arrive at some concrete approach. Deliver a functioning idea first, then expand it afterwards."

Ask who's deciding what. Is this for investigators working a case queue? How many alerts do they get, and how many are false positives? What does "more effectively" mean: fewer missed cases or fewer wasted hours?

Find the data. Transactions, account and customer records, past investigations and their outcomes. Are they in several systems? Who owns them? Which can be joined, and how clean is the customer ID?

Propose a first version. "Join transactions and accounts into a single view of each customer. Show investigators the existing alerts ranked by simple rules: new accounts with large transfers, or links to accounts already flagged. Don't replace their process yet. Measure how many of the top 50 become real cases, compared with the current queue."

Then expand. Network features, a model trained on past outcomes, audit logs for regulators, and explanations for each flag, because an investigator needs to defend the decision.

Follow-ups to expect. Permissions, since who can see what matters a lot here. What if past labels are biased? What's your plan if the data isn't joinable? One candidate's report says the interviewer set up the data as a given ("I was told to assume I had a database containing certain records"), so listen for what you can assume.

Palantir's phrase from another post is the test to apply: "What you need to show is that you can connect data to value."

Working in someone else's code

Candidate reports mention learning and debugging rounds. Palantir has a page on working inside existing systems that's worth reading: "It's common to look at a codebase and think, 'This is bad. This should be rewritten.'" and then "Take some time to reject that urge."

To practice, take an unfamiliar open source project, find a bug and fix it, narrating as you go. Read before changing, form a hypothesis, test it, and keep the fix small. The day in the life post asks a similar question: "How do I investigate a production software outage, identify a root cause of the issue, deploy a fix".

Our Palantir question hub has hands-on practice, such as diagnosing an Nginx CPU bottleneck and extracting a schema from a Parquet file. Also see the system design guide, since the 2019 report included a system design round.

Behavioral questions

Palantir says it asks about "how you've executed, challenged yourself, motivated others, and made judgments" and "where you've failed in the past". Another post says: "We want to hear about an actual failure."

Anjor Kanekar, who hired for FDE roles at Palantir, published questions he used:

  • "How did you get from A→B→C and why? How was A→ B different than B→C , what would have happened if you went straight from A→ C?"
  • "What is the hardest thing you have done/worked on/learned so far? Why was this hard for you?"
  • "What was the hardest failure you have dealt with? Did you see it coming? How did you salvage the situation? Who did you ask for help?"
  • "Tell me about a time when you proactively did something outside your day to day responsibility."

Expect "Why FDSE and not SWE?" One new grad candidate reported being asked "why a specific role? Dev or FDE?" Have an honest answer about wanting to work with users, and ask yourself whether it's true. Two Hacker News commenters also report interviews going badly after framing the work as consulting, so talk about what you built.

FDSE or SWE

Palantir's early talent page shows that candidates choose. Think about it in terms of your week: one Delta describes some weeks spent developing and reviewing code and others "scoping the future of a project with a client". If you'd enjoy that variety and can handle "Directing focus is challenging at times", FDSE is a fit. If you'd rather go deep on one system for years, look at the SWE route.
