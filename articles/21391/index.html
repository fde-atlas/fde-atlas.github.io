As efforts to use AI in corporate operations intensify, a new engineering role called "FDE" (Forward Deployed Engineer) is drawing attention. From a vendor's perspective, the shortest explanation is "an engineer stationed at the customer company to support AI adoption," but its definition and interpretation vary among companies and individuals.

Oracle Japan recently explained its "Oracle-style FDE," so I will introduce its key points and, taking this opportunity, offer three proposals to companies considering using FDEs.

According to Gartner, an FDE is "a software engineer, or a development model, in which engineers from a vendor providing software products embed themselves at the customer company's site and co-develop AI-based services or software tailored to the customer's specific challenges." As noted earlier, definitions and interpretations vary, but this article assumes Gartner's definition.

I had been hoping to cover FDEs in this series when the opportunity arose. Then, at "Oracle AI Forum," an event Oracle Japan held in Tokyo on October 6, 2026, and at an accompanying briefing for media and analysts, the company explained its new organization "Applied Engineering," which supports customers' AI implementation as the Oracle-style FDE. I will first introduce its content and then consider FDEs based on the information I gathered there.

At this event, I directly posed two questions to the company's top management and the executives of two user companies: "Shouldn't FDEs be compensated based on results?" and "Shouldn't user companies themselves cultivate FDEs?" I will present their answers in the latter half. First, let's look at what the Oracle-style FDE is.

Oracle Japan began its Applied Engineering efforts in June 2026 (the start of its fiscal year 2027). Kentaro Yoshikawa (Corporate Officer, Cloud Business General Manager, AI Platform General Manager) and Ken Inoue (Director, Cloud Business General Manager, AI Business Strategy General Manager) explained the initiative at a briefing held alongside the event under the theme "Data Platform Strategy in the AI Era."

According to their explanation, the official name is "Applied Customer Engineering (ACE)," with Applied Engineering as the abbreviation. Inoue, who leads this organization, said its distinguishing feature is "combining three elements—business understanding, technical implementation, and headquarters connection—into a single team." Business understanding refers to "understanding business context," technical implementation to "a prototype development team well-versed in Oracle technologies," and headquarters connection to "reflecting feedback into products through collaboration with (US) headquarters" (Figure 1).

Regarding AI adoption, the company aims to "support building mechanisms that maintain and develop AI-ready applications, data platforms, and organizations in order to elevate the business assets customers have accumulated into AI-ready assets" (Inoue) (Figure 2).

Although Oracle Japan introduced Applied Engineering as a new organization, Inoue said its roots lie in the "DX (Digital Transformation) Promotion Office" established six years ago, which began operating as a development team supporting customers' DX. It later began using AI within DX, and has now become a new organization taking on the role of the Oracle-style FDE. User companies likely have similar histories. Both AI and FDEs should be understood within the broader flow of DX promotion.

However, Inoue also stated, "ACE accompanies customers beyond PoC (proof of concept) until an actual prototype is running. Through a feedback loop with the development team at US headquarters, we deliver products of even higher quality. Furthermore, we actively support new businesses that customers are particularly focused on. We want to pursue these activities unique to ACE." The comment conveys pride in ACE rather than in the Oracle-style FDE.

Meanwhile, after the keynote speeches at the event—delivered by Tomomitsu Misawa (Director, Corporate Executive, President) of Oracle Japan, and by user companies of Oracle's AI solutions, namely Hiroshi Tange (Corporate Officer, Head of AI & Digital Office, General Manager of Information Systems) of Yazaki Corporation and Yusuke Kobayashi (Senior Managing Executive Officer, CIO (Chief Information Officer) & CAIDO (Chief AI and Data Officer) of the Commerce & Marketing Company, Vice Director of Commerce & Marketing Technology) of Rakuten Group—held a press conference, so I asked each of them about the ideal form of FDEs, which Oracle Japan is pursuing through Applied Engineering.

I first asked Misawa: "FDEs are attracting attention, but for users, it is hard to predict costs, including AI usage, so many hesitate to use FDEs. Also, we would be troubled by 'pseudo-FDEs.' To eliminate these issues, I believe FDEs should be compensated based on results—what do you think?" Beyond his position as top management of Oracle Japan, which has begun Oracle-style FDE activities, I wanted to hear how he, with his long experience in the IT industry, views FDEs. He responded as follows.

"Our Applied Engineering's role is to work alongside customers on-site as a development team so they can make full use of Oracle products. Another major role is to feed customers' various requests about our products back to development at US headquarters so we can provide even more useful products. Therefore, we do not currently intend to charge customers for Applied Engineering's activities alone. However, from the perspective of what is generally called an FDE, if the role is to grow going forward, we will likely need to build a framework, including results-based compensation, in consultation with customers."

I also asked Tange and Kobayashi, from the users' perspective: "Since FDEs ideally need deep familiarity with each company's business, some argue that user companies themselves should cultivate them. What do you think?" They responded as follows.

"FDE is generally described as 'an engineer who knows the business well and is also well-versed in AI,' but I see a major missing element. That element is data preparation, which is essential for applying AI to business operations. To enable AI to be used effectively across various business processes, this work is indispensable first. Much of an FDE's initial work is 'making data usable.' Rather than focusing on the FDE itself, companies need to recognize that this work is a fundamental prerequisite for AI adoption. From that perspective, FDEs who also handle data preparation should be actively developed in-house. However, in-house talent alone will not be enough. There is even a possibility that vendors will run short as well. I recognize this as a very serious concern." (Tange)

"At Rakuten Group, we are actively pursuing in-house development of FDEs. FDE is sometimes perceived as a form of consulting, but fundamentally, I see it as a question of 'how the role of the developer should change in the AI era.' As a company, we want to pursue that. That said, at present, the FDEs who can support, for example, new business initiatives are limited, and Oracle Japan's Applied Engineering team is also working alongside us. Going forward, as AI adoption accelerates and demand for FDEs grows, a talent shortage is clearly foreseeable, so prompt action will be required." (Kobayashi)

In sum, it was an extremely valuable exchange at this press briefing. I would like to thank the three of them.

Based on this reporting, I would like to close by offering three proposals to companies considering adopting FDEs.

The first is to use FDEs on a success-fee basis. The reason is as I described in my exchange with Misawa during the briefing.

The second is to prepare your data first before pursuing AI adoption. The reason is as I described above in my exchange with Tange during the briefing.

The third is to develop FDEs within your own organization. The reason is as I described above in my exchanges with Tange and Kobayashi during the briefing.

I will continue to report on these three points and share any new developments as they emerge.
