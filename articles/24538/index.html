01 From Model Dividends to Implementation Dividends

Original title: 01 从模型红利到落地红利

FDE中国社区 · 2026 · 3 min read

Summary

This opening chapter of an FDE course series argues that the bottleneck in enterprise AI is shifting from model capability to deployment: integration with real workflows, data, permissions, evaluation, and operations. The author distinguishes a “model dividend” from a “delivery dividend,” showing via a failed manufacturing Q&A demo that demos prove a model can answer, while delivery requires adoption under real permissions, data, accountability, and exceptions. It lists six common causes of stalled pilots, outlines six questions FDEs use to structure AI projects, and provides an action framework, exercises, and a checklist.

Key points

  • Enterprise AI competition is shifting from model capability to deployment capability.
  • Deployment complexity, not model complexity, defines the FDE role's value.
  • Six pilot failure causes: unclear goals, data, permissions, evals, integration, operations.
  • A manufacturing demo case shows the gap between demo and delivery.
  • Includes a six-question framework, exercises, and a checklist for readers.

Chapter Objectives

Understand why the FDE role has emerged: the main bottleneck of enterprise AI is shifting from model capability to business deployment, process transformation, system integration, evaluation and governance, and user adoption.

Why It Matters

Over the past few years, enterprises focused on large model capabilities, prompts, Copilots, and agent demos. Once projects reach production, the questions become:

  • How does the model enter real business processes?
  • How are data, permissions, tools, and systems connected?
  • How do users come to trust and adopt the outputs?
  • How are failures evaluated, tracked, and rolled back?
  • How does the project generate measurable ROI?

Large models don't lack demo capability; what enterprises lack are people who can put model capability into practice on the ground.

Core Concepts

Model Dividend

Model capabilities improve rapidly, allowing enterprises to build demos for Q&A, summarization, extraction, generation, and tool calling at low cost.

Deployment Dividend

Real competition shifts to who can integrate AI into processes, systems, permissions, data, and operational mechanisms, and continuously deliver business results.

Deployment Layer

The deployment layer connects models to the enterprise frontline, covering scenario discovery, technical scoping, integration, evaluation, launch, training, and ongoing operations.

The Demo → Delivery Gap

StageFocusTypical Risks
DemoModel capability, interaction quality, concept feasibilityManually prepared inputs, idealized data, no permission constraints
DeliveryReal data, permissions, users, failure casesIntegration, evaluation, operations, lack of organizational accountability

The core value of the FDE is turning a demo into a deliverable capability.

API Economy → Deployment Economy

  • The API economy solves "capability is callable."
  • The deployment economy solves "capability is usable, controllable, and operable."

Enterprises won't pay just for tokens; they pay for deliverable results, labor savings, and risk reduction. Deployment complexity—not model complexity—determines the value of the FDE role.

Six Reasons Pilots Fail

Enterprise AI pilots often stall after the demo stage. Common causes:

  • Unclear requirements: No clear goals or acceptance criteria.
  • Disconnected data: Data is scattered across documents, systems, IM, and people's heads.
  • Unclear permissions: Who can view, who can execute, and who is accountable is undefined.
  • Missing evaluation: No golden dataset, regression testing, or failure case management.
  • Integration difficulties: Cannot connect to core systems and daily workflows.
  • Operational gaps: After the demo, no one maintains prompts, knowledge bases, tools, or user feedback.

Opening Case: The Demo That "Looked Like a Success"

A manufacturing company built an equipment maintenance Q&A demo in two weeks. During the demo, the system could return SOP passages based on fault descriptions. Management thought it worked well, and IT believed "the model capability was already sufficient." But once it entered the pilot, frontline engineers quickly stopped using it: SOP versions differed across plants, night-shift staff lacked permission to view certain maintenance records, the old documents the system cited had expired, and real maintenance decisions also required checking spare parts inventory and equipment downtime schedules.

This case shows that the gap between demo and delivery in AI projects cannot be solved by "tweaking another prompt." A demo proves the model can answer; delivery proves the system can be adopted amid real permissions, real data, real accountability, and real exceptions.

This is exactly where the FDE comes in: translating "what the model can do" into "how the business frontline is willing to use it, who is responsible for confirming, how to roll back when things go wrong, and what counts as effective."

The FDE Working Method

An FDE needs to break an AI project down into six questions:

  • Which business process is worth changing?
  • Which data and tools are available?
  • Which actions require human review?
  • How to design the minimal verifiable pipeline?
  • How to prove effectiveness?
  • How to sustain operations after launch?

Action Framework for This Chapter

You can use the table below to judge whether an AI idea has entered the "deployment dividend" stage:

QuestionDemo-Style AnswerDelivery-Style Answer
Scenario"Build an assistant""Help after-sales agents handle policy-related follow-up questions"
Data"Upload a knowledge base""FAQs, SOPs, and order status filtered by role"
Output"Answer questions""Generate a draft; the agent confirms before sending"
Evaluation"Looks good""Classification accuracy, citation accuracy, adoption rate"
Risk"The model will try to be correct""Payouts, complaints, and sensitive customers must be escalated to humans"
Operations"We'll see after launch""Weekly review of failure cases and knowledge base versions"

After reading this chapter, you should at least be able to rewrite any "AI idea" into a verifiable business task.

Hands-On Exercise

Choose an enterprise process you know well and answer:

  • Who currently completes this process?
  • Which step is the most time-consuming or error-prone?
  • Where is AI most likely to intervene?
  • If the output is wrong, who is responsible for confirming?
  • How do you judge whether it's worth launching?

Deliverables

  • A one-sentence business scenario.
  • A brief description of the current process.
  • Three deployment obstacles.
  • Three verifiable metrics.

Common Pitfalls

  • Mistaking "can answer" for "can go live."
  • Focusing only on model performance, not on whether users adopt it.
  • Ignoring permissions, auditing, exceptions, and rollback.
  • Building a complex agent first, then looking for a business problem.

Checklist

  • Can you explain the gap between model capability and production systems?
  • Can you identify non-model reasons for project failure?
  • Can you describe AI's value using business metrics?
  • Do you know why the FDE has become a key role in the enterprise AI deployment layer?
