Deepgram FDE Role: Onsite Time and 60/90 Days

FDE Times · 2026-11-08 · 6 min read

Summary

The article examines Deepgram's Strategic Accounts FDE posting, where each engineer serves exactly one strategic customer with no time split across other accounts or teams. Onsite time is normally once or twice a month but can reach 80% during peak phases. Milestones are explicit: ship to production by day 60, then fully own the work—monitoring, debugging, and optimizing—by day 90. Junior hires get senior review before shipping; senior postings shift to handling live deployment issues and broader influence. The author advises candidates to demonstrate real production accountability, not just demos.

Key points

  • Each FDE serves one strategic customer at a time, no split time.
  • Onsite visits run once or twice monthly, up to 80% at peak.
  • Ship to production by day 60; own it fully by day 90.
  • Juniors get senior review; seniors handle escalations and live issues.
  • FDE work sits on one Applied Engineer ladder from Associate to Principal.

In brief

  • Deepgram's Strategic Accounts posting assigns each FDE one strategic customer at a time and does not allow time to be split with other accounts or teams.
  • Normally engineers visit the customer once or twice a month, but at peak onsite time can reach 80%; the senior postings mention only occasional customer travel.
  • Day 60 is the milestone for shipping to production; day 90 is for owning your work in full, meaning monitoring, debugging and optimising what you shipped.

Deepgram’s job posting for a Forward Deployed Engineer in Strategic Accounts contains one very blunt sentence: while deployed, the pod’s account is your only job, with no time split across other accounts or teams. Each engineer is attached to one strategic customer at a time.

That sounds like a privilege: room to focus, little context-switching. But read on through the sections on onsite time, the day-60 and day-90 milestones and responsibility for production, and it becomes clear that the focus comes with a specific price.

If you work as an FDE or want to become one, Deepgram’s posting is worth reading closely because it describes one kind of FDE quite precisely: deeply embedded with the customer, backed by a team, and judged by what is running in the customer’s systems.

Focus at the individual level, not the team level

The pod model is not Deepgram’s invention. Perspective’s playbook on the FDE function describes a pod as one FDE plus a product manager and a data or platform engineer, jointly owning one strategic account.

The same playbook notes that, at team level, an FDE function can still run several engagements at once. So the absolute focus sits with the individual: the company has many customers, but each engineer has only one.

The consequence, if one reasons from that structure, is that an FDE has little room to hide. With a portfolio of ten customers, one slow integration can sink among the others. With a single customer and no split time, a production incident easily comes back to the engineer sitting there.

From once a month to as much as 80%

The Strategic Accounts posting sets a baseline onsite rhythm: you will be at the customer’s site at least once or twice a month. But during peak phases of an engagement, onsite time can reach 80%.

Take a five-day working week: 80% means four days in the customer’s office, leaving one day for syncing with the pod, reading internal documentation and work that needs uninterrupted concentration.

By contrast, once or twice a month is just a few days out of roughly twenty working days. In the same role, the rhythm of life can change completely from one phase to the next.

Compared with the senior postings, the gap is even starker. The Senior FDE posting in Enterprise mentions only occasional customer travel, supporting sales deals and key onsite working sessions. The Senior FDE Strategic Accounts posting in Calgary likewise says only occasional customer travel.

On one side, up to 80% of the time at the customer; on the other, occasional trips. The gap may come from seniority, from the phase of the engagement each posting describes, or simply from how the postings were written, since they do not explain.

Why would a company want its engineers at the customer so much? CRV, in its analysis of the FDE role, argues that being on site brings hidden constraints to the surface. If you apply, do not guess: ask directly what the onsite ratio is in normal times and at peak.

Ship by day 60, own by day 90

The milestones in the posting are counted in days, not in onsite weeks. By day 60, the engineer must have shipped to production. By day 90, the expectation is to own their work in full, end to end.

“Own” has a concrete meaning here. The posting on 4dayweek.io says you will monitor, debug and optimise what you have shipped in production. The work does not stop at the merge.

Consider a hypothetical: the customer is a large call centre that wants to add speech-to-text to its call-handling pipeline. By day 60, the speech-to-text pipeline has to be running on real calls.

From then until day 90, when latency rises at peak hours or recognition goes wrong for a group of calls, the first person who has to open the logs is you.

This is where the one-customer-at-a-time model makes accountability heavier. There is no second account to share your attention, so what you ship for that customer is almost the whole of what you are judged on. CRV captures exactly this: FDEs ship code straight into the customer’s live environment and feed the lessons back into the core product.

Perspective’s playbook adds something about the period after handover: the FDE stays alongside the customer, still in the customer’s Slack, and is now accountable for something running for real. Production is not the end of the engagement; it is where the hardest part begins.

Newcomers get a safety net; seniors are the safety net

The Strategic Accounts posting does not leave newcomers on their own. The role is hired under the title Applied Engineer, on a single career ladder from Associate to Principal, and a senior FDE reviews work before it ships.

The senior postings reverse that role. The Calgary posting gives the senior engineer full ownership of the implementation, handling issues with live deployments and acting as the escalation point for complex technical incidents when needed. The Enterprise posting states plainly that your influence will extend beyond any single customer.

Put the three postings in one table and a trajectory emerges:

Aspect FDE, Strategic Accounts Senior FDE, Strategic Accounts (Calgary) Senior FDE, Enterprise
Customer attachment One strategic customer at a time, no split time Full ownership of the customer implementation Influence beyond any single customer
Onsite time Once or twice a month, up to 80% at peak Occasional customer travel Occasional travel, key onsite sessions
Production Ship by day 60, own the work by day 90 Handle issues with live deployments Not stated in posting
Who backs whom Senior FDE reviews before shipping Acts as escalation point Not stated in posting

The most reasonable reading: newcomers learn the trade by putting all their effort into one customer, under the review of those ahead of them. At senior level, the work shifts to being a support for others and carrying what has been learned to more customers, with less time on site.

For anyone worried that FDE work is a dead end, the detail about the Applied Engineer ladder is a signal worth noting. Work at the customer counts on the same ladder from Associate to Principal, rather than being split off into a separate track.

Tell the story after the ship, not just the demo

If you are aiming for the kind of FDE role Deepgram describes, your CV and interviews need to prove one thing: that you have been accountable for something that ran for real, not just that you built it. The day-90 milestone in the posting is exactly that test.

On a CV, a bullet such as “built a POC integrating a speech API” is far weaker than one that says the system ran for real users, how you monitored it and which incident you debugged. In interviews, the best story is a time you found a root cause in production and fixed it while users were still waiting.

When it is your turn to ask questions, ask about exactly what Deepgram’s posting spells out: how much onsite time at peak, what must be delivered by day 60, who reviews your code. Vague answers suggest that the price of focus will be yours to bear alone.

The one-customer-at-a-time model does not reward people who are good at slides or good at coordination. It rewards people willing to put their name on a running system, and that is a skill you can start practising today on the service you already work on.
