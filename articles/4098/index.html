How FDE teams work with product and engineering (without broken telephone)

Tandem · 2026-08-18 · 8 min read

Summary

Tandem, drawing on interviews with nine FDE programs, examines how forward deployed engineers can feed field knowledge back into product and engineering. It uses Palantir's model—rough field builds later generalized into product features—as the canonical loop, noting Nabeel Qureshi's point that 95% of enterprise data problems are access, cleaning, and joining. The loop breaks at three points: signal degradation across function handoffs, documentation diverging from actual system state, and the unpaid feedback tax on FDEs (up to 3 hours of prep per customer meeting). The authors argue coding agents will compress the build step, making field ground truth more valuable, not less. The fix is structural: assemble account records automatically from calls, Slack, email, and CRM, mine patterns across accounts, and route workarounds to product as artifacts, measured by traceability, latency, recurrence coverage, and re-explain rate.

Key points

  • Palantir's loop treats field builds as product discovery, generalizing recurring patterns into features.
  • The loop breaks via broken telephone, state divergence, and the feedback tax on FDEs.
  • 95% of enterprise data problems are access, cleaning, and joining—knowledge only the field holds.
  • As agents compress the build step, the bottleneck shifts to goals and evals, raising field signal's value.
  • The fix: auto-assembled account records, cross-account pattern mining, and four measurable loop metrics.

TL;DR: Forward deployed engineers are the highest-bandwidth feedback channel a product org can have, and most companies waste it. Palantir built its product on the loop, field builds becoming product features, and Nabeel Qureshi's account notes that 95% of enterprise data problems are access, cleaning, and joining, knowledge that only exists in the field. The loop breaks on context: one FDE we interviewed described sales, marketing, and implementation as a broken telephone running on outdated documentation. Teams that fix the shared context layer close the loop, the same layer that doubled account aggregation activation at Qonto from 8% to 16%.

The forward deployed engineer sits closer to the truth about your product than anyone else in the company. They watch real users fail at real workflows inside real environments, and they build the workarounds with their own hands. Then, at most companies, that knowledge evaporates, because there is no channel wide enough to carry it back to product and engineering.

This piece is about that channel: how the loop is supposed to work, the three places it breaks, what the automation wave changes about it, and the fix that does not involve another weekly sync. It draws on our research interviews with nine FDE programs plus the public record from the companies that invented the model. If you are new to the role itself, start with what a forward deployed engineer is and come back.

The loop that built Palantir

The canonical version of FDE-to-product feedback is Palantir's, often described as gravel roads becoming paved highways. Field engineers build fast, rough, customer-specific solutions. The core engineering team studies those builds, finds the patterns recurring across customers, and paves them into product features. The custom work is not a services cost to be minimized. It is the product discovery mechanism.

Palantir's own description of the role is built around that embedding, and Qureshi's insider account explains why the field knowledge is irreplaceable: 95% of enterprise data problems involve access, cleaning, and joining data, not analysis, and you only learn that by standing inside the customer's mess. Even the skeptics agree on the mechanism. Marty Cagan's critique of the FDE model worries about companies drifting into custom development, and his own resolution is the same loop: the model works when field learnings flow back into a generalized product instead of accumulating as bespoke code.

The teams in our research that run this well make the loop explicit. A computer-vision platform formalized it as pioneer-and-replicate: FDEs prove a pattern at one customer, implementation engineers spread it, and the recurring patterns get product attention. Their FDE team even owns internal tooling development, building systems that ingest meeting recordings, Slack, and email so field knowledge lands somewhere durable. The org design half of this is covered in our FDE team structure guide. This piece is about the information half.

Where the loop breaks: three failure points

Broken telephone between functions. The sharpest description we heard came from an FDE running four accounts a day: sales, marketing, and implementation each communicate differently, all working from outdated documentation, so the field signal degrades at every hop. By the time an FDE's workaround reaches a product manager, it is an anecdote with the load-bearing details stripped out. The sales-to-implementation handoff is the first hop where the signal corrupts, and the corruption compounds from there.

State divergence. The same engineer named the deeper version: docs do not get updated when code changes, and clients pivot mid-engagement, so the written record describes a system that no longer exists. Product teams reading that record are learning from fiction. His personal fix was a private Postgres database of account truth, which worked for exactly one person, the same pattern behind every DIY project brain we have studied.

The feedback tax nobody pays. Writing up field learnings is real work that competes with delivery, and delivery always wins. Our interviews put status reporting and follow-up writing among the top drains on FDE time, up to 3 hours of prep per customer meeting before any synthesis for product even starts. Asking FDEs to also file structured product feedback adds a tax on exactly the people with the least slack, which is why feedback processes announced in kickoffs die by the third sprint.

  • Broken telephone. What product receives: an anecdote, two hops old. What the field actually knew: the workflow, the user, the exact failing step.
  • State divergence. What product receives: docs describing last quarter's build. What the field actually knew: the current workaround and why it exists.
  • Feedback tax. What product receives: silence, or a stale quarterly deck. What the field actually knew: patterns across every active account.

What the automation wave changes about the loop

There is a live argument about where FDE work is heading, and it bears directly on feedback. One framing now common among AI infrastructure founders breaks the FDE job into three steps: define the business problem, codify it into an eval rubric and environment, and iterate until the agentic workflow solves it. The argument is that the third step, historically hundreds of hours of bespoke software per customer, is the one being automated by better models and coding agents, leaving goal definition and evals as the durable human work.

He is right about the compression, and our field research pushes back on the premise. Codifying the business problem assumes the problem is legible, and the engineers we interviewed say the opposite: the stated requirement is rarely the real one, and the people on the ground rarely have the full picture. You cannot write the eval before you have reconstructed the account. Steps one and two are context problems, not modeling problems, which means that as the build step compresses, the bottleneck moves to exactly the work the feedback loop depends on: knowing, with evidence, what is actually happening inside every customer. Nothing about client-environment access friction, measured in weeks to months in our interviews, or change management compresses because a model improved. The dividing line for the role holds: who types the code is changing, and who owns the goal and whether it worked is not.

For product teams, the implication is direct. The FDE signal is about to get more valuable, not less, because it will increasingly consist of the one thing models cannot generate: ground truth about customer reality.

How to tell the loop is working

Treat the loop as a system with numbers, not a vibe. Four measures survive contact with real teams. First, traceability: the share of roadmap items that cite field evidence, an account, a call, a recurring pattern, rather than internal intuition. Second, latency: elapsed time from a field observation to a product ticket, which should be days once capture is structural. Third, recurrence coverage: how many active accounts each shipped fix maps to, since patterns across accounts are the point. Fourth, the re-explain rate: how often product asks an FDE to repeat context that should already be on record. When the first three rise and the fourth falls, the loop is closed. When only meeting counts rise, it is not.

Closing the loop without another meeting

The fix that recurred across our research is structural. Stop asking humans to carry the signal, and capture it where it already exists.

  • Make account state a byproduct, not a chore. Field truth already lives in calls, Slack, email, tickets, and the CRM. An account brain assembles it from those sources into a current, cited record per account, so nobody writes up what was already said.
  • Mine patterns across accounts, not anecdotes within them. Product wants to know what recurs. With sourced account records, the question which accounts hit this same limitation gets answered with evidence instead of a hallway poll. This is the portfolio view we describe in RAG status that isn't theater, pointed at product instead of at delivery risk.
  • Route workarounds to product as artifacts. A workaround with its context attached, the account, the call where the need surfaced, the promise it protects, is a roadmap input. A workaround as tribal knowledge is a future incident.
  • Keep the human on the goal. Agents can draft the synthesis and the updates, with approval before anything ships. The FDE's irreplaceable contribution is judging what matters, which is the same scoping muscle the role selects for.

The payoff shows up on both sides of the loop. Product learns from the field at the speed the field learns, and FDEs stop paying the feedback tax. If your product roadmap is running on two-hop anecdotes, see what a sourced view of your accounts surfaces in a demo.

FAQs

How should FDE teams work with product and engineering?

Through an explicit loop: field builds are treated as product discovery, recurring patterns get generalized into the product, and the context behind each workaround travels with it. Palantir institutionalized this as field solutions becoming product features, and the model works only when the information flow is structural rather than meeting-based.

Why does FDE feedback rarely reach the product roadmap?

Three compounding failures: the signal degrades across function handoffs, documentation diverges from reality as code changes and clients pivot, and writing up learnings taxes the people with the least slack. Fixing the meeting cadence addresses none of these. Fixing the shared context layer addresses all three.

Will coding agents make the FDE feedback loop less important?

The opposite. As agents compress the build step, the durable work becomes defining goals and evals, and both depend on accurate account context, which is precisely what the feedback loop carries. Field ground truth is the input models cannot generate.

What tools support an FDE-to-product feedback loop?

The core requirement is automatic capture from where truth already lives: calls, Slack, email, tickets, CRM. Teams in our research tried personal databases and per-client repositories, which work for one person and fail at retrieval scale. A shared account brain with cross-account pattern queries is the durable version.

Key terms glossary

Feedback loop (FDE-to-product): The channel through which field learnings from customer deployments become product decisions. The mechanism behind Palantir's model and the main justification for FDE cost beyond individual accounts.

Gravel road to paved highway: Building rough customer-specific solutions in the field, then generalizing recurring patterns into product features. Field work as product discovery.

Broken telephone: Signal degradation as account knowledge hops between sales, implementation, FDE, and product, each working from different and outdated records.

State divergence: The widening gap between documentation and reality when systems and customer needs change faster than docs. The reason product teams often learn from fiction.

Eval rubric: A codified definition of what a successful agentic workflow must do for a given business problem. In the automation-era framing of FDE work, writing correct evals is the durable human task, and it requires reconstructed account context.

Account brain: A shared record of each account assembled from calls, Slack, email, CRM, and tickets, with sources attached. The structural fix for feedback loss between field and product.
