How to Write Proposals Using Generative AI: FDE-Style Structure, Prompts, and Verification Steps | FDE Research Institute

Summary

This FDE Soken article explains how forward deployed engineers can use generative AI to write proposals while keeping humans in charge of judgment, facts, and commitments. It lays out a five-step workflow, a six-element prompt template (role, reader, purpose, input, output format, prohibitions), chapter-by-chapter generation, and a three-layer verification covering facts, logic, and reader fit. It also suggests having AI play devil's advocate in a separate session, and warns about confidential data handling and that submitters bear full responsibility for AI-drafted content.

Key points

  • Humans own judgment, facts, and promises; AI handles drafting and structure checks.
  • A one-page issue memo largely determines proposal quality.
  • Prompt prohibitions are the most effective guard against fabricated numbers.
  • Generate chapter by chapter, feeding confirmed text into the next prompt.
  • Run devil's advocate reviews in a fresh session to avoid AI self-approval.

What is the basic workflow for writing proposals with generative AI?

When writing proposals with generative AI, humans decide the issues, AI creates the draft, and humans verify it at the end. This three-stage process is the basic approach.

If you have AI write the entire proposal, you tend to end up with a document that sounds plausible but doesn't resonate with the customer. Why is the customer struggling? Why now? Why your company? Only someone who has spoken directly with the customer can write about these three things. On the other hand, generative AI excels at creating structural drafts, organizing wording, and checking for omissions. Deciding in advance what humans handle and what you delegate to AI significantly reduces rework.

How do you decide the division of roles between humans and AI?

Assign judgments, facts, and commitments to humans, and structuring, writing, and completeness checks to AI. This division is the safest and most efficient for proposals.

ProcessPrimary ownerHow to use generative AIWhat humans must always verify
Identifying challengesHumanSummarizing interview notes, suggesting candidate issuesDistinguishing what the customer actually said from speculation
Creating the outlineAI + HumanProducing multiple draft chapter structuresWhether it reads in the order decision-makers expect
Drafting the bodyAIGenerating chapter by chapterProper nouns, figures, and assumptions
Estimates, team, scheduleHumanFormatting tables onlyBasis for effort, costs, and deadlines
Final reviewHuman + AIUsing AI as an objector and omission spotterWording that constitutes contractual commitments

It's best not to include estimates and team structure in the process where AI "thinks things up." Generative AI can produce unfounded effort estimates and plausible-sounding costs. Numbers written in a proposal are taken as commitments to the customer as-is.

How do you organize the proposal-writing steps in order?

Following five steps—organizing inputs, structure, chapter-by-chapter generation, verification, and polishing—keeps quality consistent.

  • Gather inputs: Collect interview notes, the RFP, the customer's public materials, and past proposals from your company (excluding confidential information).
  • Summarize the issues on one page: A human writes out "customer challenges," "definition of success," "constraints," and "decision-makers" as bullet points.
  • Generate the outline: Provide the issue memo, have AI produce two or three chapter structure patterns, then a human selects one.
  • Generate the body chapter by chapter: Don't have AI produce the full text at once; repeat generation and review at the chapter level.
  • Verify and polish: Do fact-checking, objection checks, and wording consistency, then finalize the formatting.

From hands-on experience, the quality of the "one-page issues" in step 2 almost entirely determines the final result. If you skip this and hand the RFP straight to AI, you tend to end up with a proposal that's just a paraphrase of the RFP.

How do you create prompts for having generative AI write proposals?

Include six elements in your prompt: "role, reader, purpose, inputs, output format, and prohibitions." This greatly reduces output inconsistency.

With just "write a proposal for Company X," AI will fill the gaps with generalities. A proposal becomes stronger the more clearly it defines who the reader is and what decision it's meant to drive. The basic approach is to explicitly provide this context in the prompt.

What elements are needed in a proposal prompt?

Templating the six elements and filling them in each time makes it easier to keep output quality consistent even when the person in charge changes.

ElementExample of what to writeWhat happens if it's missing
Role"As a consultant in charge of BtoB SaaS implementation proposals"Tone and perspective become inconsistent
Reader"IT department head and business division head. They prioritize investment decisions over technical details"Too much jargon, or too shallow
Purpose"Obtain approval to run a PoC"It becomes unclear what decision is needed
InputsInterview notes, one-page issues, constraintsFilled with generalities or fabricated challenges
Output format"Headings + up to 300 characters per chapter, include one table"Too long or structure breaks down
Prohibitions"Do not fabricate figures, examples, or company names. Mark unclear points as [To Confirm]"Plausible fabrications get mixed in

The most effective element is "prohibitions." If you write "do not fill in unclear points with speculation; explicitly flag them as requiring confirmation," the sections needing human review naturally surface. Note that markers like [To Confirm] are for internal review. Be sure to resolve all of them before sending to the customer.

What are the benefits of generating chapter by chapter?

Generating by chapter makes it easier to spot logical inconsistencies or factual errors on the spot, and lowers correction costs.

If you have AI generate the full text at once, the assumptions in the first half and the proposals in the second half gradually drift apart. You only notice this after reading the whole thing. Working chapter by chapter lets you proceed as follows.

  • Generate only the "Current Situation and Challenges" chapter and check it against the interview content
  • Paste the finalized "Current Situation and Challenges" into the next prompt and generate the "Solution Approach" chapter
  • Continue in the same way through "Implementation Details," "Team and Schedule," and "Expected Benefits"

Using the finalized text from the previous chapter as input for the next makes it easier to maintain consistency across the whole document. It takes more effort, but in my experience it often finishes faster than rewriting everything at the end.

How do you verify the quality of a proposal written by generative AI?

Split verification into three layers: "facts," "logic," and "reader fit." Having AI play the objector role is also effective.

Generative AI's writing is fluent, so problems are easy to miss if you skim. Rather than asking "does it read naturally?", the practical approach is to set specific perspectives and check with a checklist.

What is the three-layer check of facts, logic, and the reader?

The three-layer check is a method of verifying in order: proper nouns and numbers, the connection between claims and evidence, and the decision-maker's interests.

LayerQuestion to verifySpecific check method
FactsAre company names, product names, numbers, and dates correct?Cross-check that every number has a source or input origin
LogicDo issue → cause → solution → effect connect?Extract each chapter's conclusion one line at a time and read them side by side
Reader fitIs it in the order and level of detail the decision-maker wants to know?Can you tell "what needs to be decided with this proposal" within the first page?

The fact layer deserves particular attention. Generative AI can naturally add industry statistics or competitor examples that were not in the input. It is safe to decide in advance to keep only numbers for which a human can point to "where in the input materials it is written."

How do you make AI play the role of a critic?

Have another session read the completed draft and raise concerns and questions from the customer's perspective; this lets you find weaknesses in the proposal in advance.

For example, make requests like the following.

  • "You are the division manager receiving this proposal. List five reasons you would hesitate to approve it."
  • "Point out the claims in this proposal that have weak evidence."
  • "If a competitor were to propose for the same project, where would they attack?"

The key is to have the critic role run in a new conversation separate from the one that wrote the draft. In the same conversation, AI tends to affirm its own output. Of the concerns raised, reflect only those the customer would really likely say into the FAQ or supplementary materials.

How do FDEs use generative AI for proposal writing?

FDEs are a role that embeds in the customer's field to discover issues. Generative AI is used as a tool to quickly turn that firsthand information into proposal form.

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is known as a role popularized by Palantir. It is an engineer who is stationed in and closely works with the customer's field, handling everything from issue discovery to implementation. What distinguishes an FDE's proposal from a sales-driven one is that it starts from "what was actually seen in the field." Generative AI does not replace that strength; it is used to speed up organizing and communicating firsthand information.

What makes FDE-style proposals different?

FDE-style proposals are characterized by being built around "which parts of the field's business workflow to change" rather than feature introductions.

PerspectiveTypical proposalFDE-style proposal
Starting pointFeatures of own products and servicesBusiness processes and data observed at the customer's site
How issues are writtenIndustry-wide general issuesIssues at the level of specific departments, processes, and people
SolutionProduct implementationA small working prototype and phased rollout
Success metricsAbstract effects (e.g., efficiency)Measurable metrics agreed with the customer
Next actionContract and implementationShort-term PoC or validation sprints

In FDE practice, proposals are often treated not as a "final deliverable" but as a "hypothesis to validate together with the customer." Therefore, it pairs well with approaches like bringing a generative-AI-produced draft to a meeting with the customer and revising it on the spot.

What are specific situations where FDEs use generative AI?

Organizing notes right after interviews, articulating business workflows, and drafting PoC plans are situations where FDEs can make good use of generative AI.

  • Right after field interviews: Give scribbled notes to generative AI and have them classified into "facts," "customer statements," and "own hypotheses," solidifying the key points while memory is fresh.
  • Visualizing business workflows: Have verbally heard procedures organized into a table of steps and owners, then have the customer confirm it.
  • PoC plan drafts: Provide the hypotheses to validate, the duration, and success criteria, and have it create the plan's structure.
  • Translating technical explanations: Have engineer-oriented explanations rephrased into language for decision-makers.

The fourth is a particularly FDE-like use. FDEs are expected to speak both technical and business languages. Asking generative AI to "explain this technical constraint in terms a division manager can use for an investment decision" can reveal leaps in explanation that are hard to notice on your own. However, the final wording should be decided by the person who knows the relationship with the customer.

What should you watch out for when writing proposals with generative AI?

The points to watch are information leakage, fabrication of facts, and accountability. In particular, rules for handling the customer's confidential information must be set in advance.

Proposals gather highly confidential information such as the customer's internal information, pricing, and organizational structure. Inputting it into AI without limits just because it is convenient can lead to contractual and compliance problems.

How should confidential information be handled?

As a principle, handle confidential information only in AI environments approved by your company, and confirm the scope of the nondisclosure agreement with the customer in advance.

Type of informationHandling guideline
Customer's company and contact namesReplace with pseudonyms outside approved environments
Customer's internal data and business materialsConfirm the NDA and internal regulations before inputting
Own pricing and quote conditionsLimit to internally approved environments
Public information (press releases, etc.)Use while keeping the source

Whether the AI service uses input data for training varies by service and contract plan. It is safest to check the terms of use and data-handling settings, and use it after agreeing with your company's information security officer.

Why shouldn't you submit AI output as-is?

The content of a proposal becomes your company's commitment. Even for AI-written wording, the submitter bears all responsibility.

Common problems with generative AI output are as follows.

  • Plausible numbers mixed in: Unsupported effect estimates or industry statistics get included
  • Overpromising: Definitive expressions like "guaranteed" or "completely" that carry contractual risk get included
  • Similarity to other companies' materials: Generic expressions increase, diluting your company's unique strengths
  • Missing customer-specific context: Points the customer cares about and past history are not reflected

We recommend making it an internal rule that the final check before submission is done by a human, not by AI. Generative AI is effective for creating proposals "quickly," but making them "correct" is the job of the staff members who have engaged directly with customers. People who possess firsthand field information, such as FDEs, should be able to make the best use of this division of labor.
