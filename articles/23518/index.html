Build vs. Buy for AI Systems: How FDEs Advise Customers

Original title: AI 系统自研还是采购：FDE 如何给客户建议

FDE Times · 2026-11-16 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide argues that when customers ask whether to build or buy an AI system, FDEs should neither defend their own product nor dodge the question. Instead, they should decompose the system into layers—model platform, data processing, business logic, and UI—and evaluate each layer against four criteria: differentiation, speed, total cost (including licenses and maintenance), and readiness (people and data). Citing a 2025 MIT NANDA study, it notes purchased AI tools succeed about 67% of the time versus roughly a third of that for internal builds. A worked insurance-claims example shows how to compute a break-even volume (around 42,500 documents monthly) and give threshold-based advice. The article stresses that honestly flagging dependency risks, even when it costs sales, builds the trust that retains customers long term.

Key points

  • The real question is which layers to buy and which to build, not a single choice.
  • Four criteria: differentiation, speed, total cost, and organizational readiness.
  • MIT NANDA 2025: purchased AI tools succeed ~67% of the time; internal builds about a third of that.
  • Give break-even thresholds (e.g., ~42,500 docs/month) rather than one-word answers.
  • FDEs who admit dependency risks, even against their own product, earn lasting trust.

Forward Deployed Engineers break the system into layers using four criteria and give an honest verdict on each layer, even if it costs their own product the deal.

Key takeaways

  • The question truly worth answering is which layers to buy and which to build, not picking a single option for the whole system.
  • Four criteria matter most: whether the layer lets the customer differentiate, how quickly the customer needs it, total cost including licensing fees and maintenance, and whether the customer has enough people and data.
  • An FDE who defends their own product loses trust; an FDE who advises around the outcomes the customer needs keeps customers for the long term.

Sooner or later there will be a meeting where someone on the customer side looks you in the eye and asks: should we build it ourselves, or just buy it from your company? You are the vendor's engineer, and everyone in the room can guess what you will say. That is exactly why your next answer determines how much they will trust you.

The easiest instinctive reaction is to defend your own product. A second instinctive reaction is equally wrong: say "it depends" and change the subject. There is a third approach: use four criteria to break the system down layer by layer, and state the truth even when the conclusion is unfavorable to your own product.

Why defending the product loses customers

Customers do not pay for you to sit beside them; they pay for outcomes. An analysis on Substack by Enterprise Context Management argues that if hands-on support helps customers realize value faster, they will accept it, but they will not mistake that support for the outcome itself.

An FDE focused on the product rather than on outcomes gets seen through quickly.

The data also does not support building on instinct. According to Fortune, a 2025 study by MIT NANDA found that buying AI tools from specialized vendors or partnering with them succeeds about 67% of the time; internal builds succeed at only about a third of that rate.

But this does not mean buying is always right. The same Substack analysis flags a risk that FDEs should raise proactively: an engineering team embedded at the customer can gradually turn into a permanent implementation department, quietly embedding itself in the customer's daily operations.

Stating this risk candidly, even when it concerns you personally, is the fastest way to make customers believe everything else you say.

The right question is which layers to buy and which to build

An article on TechTarget, "Build vs. buy AI: A CIO's decision matrix," points out that the real question is rarely either/or, but which parts of the AI stack to buy and which to build in order to preserve differentiation.

HatchWorks describes the most common hybrid approach: buy the platform, then build the custom parts and the customer's proprietary AI on top of it.

So when you hear this question, the first thing to do is resist answering right away. Draw the system as several layers, typically the model platform, the data processing layer, the business logic layer, and the user interface, then apply the four criteria to each layer.

Four criteria, judged in order

Differentiation. An executive at EY suggests that if a capability is tightly bound to the customer's industry and is key to how the customer stands apart from competitors, the default should be to build the data and proprietary AI in-house. HatchWorks offers a quick test: if the customer's competitors can buy the same thing, it is unlikely to become a competitive advantage.

Speed. The TechTarget article notes that when speed is decisive, buying almost always wins. However, HatchWorks cautions that a fast demo can mislead decision-makers. The question to ask is how long it takes to run on the customer's real data; how long it takes to produce a demo says nothing.

Total cost. Buying has its own cost curve, as licensing and subscription fees grow with scale. Building means taking on maintenance, and HatchWorks stresses that maintenance is precisely the price of owning software, never a secondary concern. Only by counting both types of costs is the comparison fair.

Readiness. Darshan Naik of Capgemini believes that people, business readiness, and data are the biggest factors determining success or failure, yet they are often underestimated. Even if a layer is worth building, if the customer has no one to maintain it, it is not worth doing now.

Applied to a real case

Suppose an insurance company wants to use AI to process claims and asks whether it should build everything in-house. You break the system into four layers and run through the criteria one by one:

LayerDifferentiating?SpeedCosts to examine closelyRecommendation
Model platformNo, competitors can buy the sameAvailable immediately by buyingLicensing fees grow with claim volumeBuy
Document extractionLowBuying is fasterBilled per documentBuy, test with real claim forms
Claims rules and dataHigh, industry-specificSlower but worth itMaintenance borne by the customerBuild
Staff-facing interfaceMediumDepends on the teamLow maintenanceUse an off-the-shelf solution, customize gradually

The claims rules and data layer is where the customer can pull ahead of competitors. This insurance company's experience in handling claims is something no competitor can buy, so it is worth investing people and time in this layer. But the fourth criterion forces a further question: after you leave, who on the customer's side will maintain these rules?

The break-even point matters more than today's price

The document extraction layer deserves careful math. The numbers below are purely illustrative assumptions. Suppose the purchase price is $0.20 per document, and the customer processes 10,000 claims per month, i.e., $2,000 per month.

The build option is equally hypothetical: an upfront investment of $60,000, plus $6,000 per month in maintenance and infrastructure. Spreading the build cost over 24 months of use adds $2,500 per month. So the total build cost is roughly $8,500 per month, largely independent of claim volume.

Break-even is reached when $0.20 times the number of claims equals $8,500, i.e., about 42,500 claims per month. At 10,000 claims, buying costs less than a quarter of building.

If the customer rolls this out company-wide and claim volume reaches 100,000, buying would cost $20,000 per month, more than twice the build cost.

So the right recommendation is neither a simple "buy" nor a simple "build," but: buy now, re-evaluate when monthly claim volume exceeds roughly 40,000, provided the customer has maintenance staff by then. What you hand the customer is a threshold to watch, which is far more useful than an opinion.

What to say in the meeting

When the customer asks, steer the conversation toward outcomes: "Before we discuss buy vs. build, what outcomes must you achieve this quarter?" This tells you how much speed matters.

Then ask: "If your competitors had exactly the same tool, where would you still beat them?" This reveals which layers are truly differentiating.

Then ask two more questions, one on cost and one on readiness: "When volume grows tenfold, who bears the added cost?" and "After the vendor's engineers leave, who on your team will maintain what has been built?" Finally, proactively raise the risk of depending on a vendor's engineering team, including depending on you.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is answering with one word for the whole system: "buy" or "build." The second is letting a demo decide what should be decided by testing on real data. The third is comparing licensing fees against upfront build costs while forgetting maintenance, or conversely, forgetting that licensing fees grow with scale.

The hardest mistake to spot is: a layer genuinely enables the customer to differentiate, you recommend building it, but the customer has neither the people nor the data. Right strategy, wrong timing, is still wrong.

Turning it into a job-search advantage

Before your FDE interview, prepare a story about a time you put the customer's results ahead of your own product's short-term interests. A four-criteria table you have actually used, with recommendations for each layer and break-even points, turns that story into evidence.

Your resume should state outcomes, for example: "Advised the customer to purchase a procurement platform, build the business rules layer in-house, and create a plan to hand maintenance over to the customer's team."

Practice this week: take a project you know well, break it into layers, fill in the four-criteria table, and then ask a colleague to play a difficult customer while you defend each cell of your judgment.

An FDE who has once said "you should buy this part from someone else" will be more easily trusted when recommending that the rest be handed to their own team.
