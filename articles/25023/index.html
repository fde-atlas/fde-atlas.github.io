How to Write a Meeting Minutes Email: FDE Client Meeting Follow-Up Template

Original title: 会议纪要邮件怎么写：FDE 客户会议跟进模板

FDE Times · 2026-10-27 · 7 min read

Summary

This guide teaches forward deployed engineers (FDEs) a 15-minute, five-step process for writing post-meeting recap emails. The email should stay under 150 words, contain only decisions, action items, and open questions, and be sent within 24 hours. Every item follows a task–owner–deadline format with one named person and one specific date, replacing vague promises like “the team will follow up soon.” Using a case study of deploying a support-ticket classification agent at a logistics company, the article also stresses documenting dependencies between client and engineer tasks, and getting scope boundaries confirmed in writing to prevent later disputes.

Key points

  • Emails stay under 150 words with three sections: decisions, action items, open questions.
  • Each task has exactly one owner and one specific deadline, with time zone noted.
  • Send within 24 hours; the writing process takes 15 minutes total.
  • Note dependencies between client deliverables and your own deadlines.
  • Get scope boundaries confirmed in writing to avoid disputes later.

A meeting-summary email of under 150 words, written in five steps, locking down scope, data handoffs, and delivery dates before disputes arise.

Key Takeaways

  • Record only decisions, action items, and questions that need a customer reply. Write each one in the format "task – owner – deadline."
  • Every task has exactly one owner and one specific date. Never write "the team," never write "as soon as possible."
  • Keep the email very short (no more than 150 words) and send it within 24 hours of the meeting.

"We'll take a look at the data, and the team will get back to you soon." That sentence sounds polite enough, but a week later, nobody knows who is supposed to check the data, and nobody knows what the deadline is.

Willow Voice explains the problem: when a task is vaguely assigned to a group, everyone makes their own assumptions, and it is precisely those assumptions that let work slip through the cracks.

For a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), this is more than a matter of writing style. In a guide on FDE weekly meetings, GUVI emphasizes that a brief written summary prevents later disputes over "what was discussed and what was promised."

In a real deployment project, "promises" mean the scope of work, the data the customer needs to deliver, and the date by which you need to deliver your results.

This article introduces a 15-minute process: an email template, five steps for filling it in, a self-check after each step, and the most common mistakes. Everything revolves around the same hypothetical case, so by the end you'll have an email ready to send.

What is the final output, and what do you need to prepare?

The final output is a very short email; Vibe sets the cap at 150 words. The email keeps only three sections: decisions, action items with owners and deadlines, and open questions that need a reply from the recipients.

You need three things: your raw notes from the meeting (typed or handwritten), the list of attendees from both sides, and a calendar for setting dates. No special tools are required. The template below can be saved as a snippet in your email client:

主题：[项目] – 会议纪要 [日期] – 请于 [日期] 前确认

各位好，

决定
1. ...

行动项（任务 – 负责人 – 截止日期）
1. ... – [一个人的名字] – [具体日期，时区]

待确认问题
1. ... – [由谁回答] – [截止日期]

请在 [日期] 前确认或指出需要更正之处。
如未收到回复，我们将按上述清单推进。

The case running through this article: you are an FDE deploying an agent that automatically classifies customer support tickets for a logistics company. Today's meeting is about pulling historical ticket data for a pilot run. Attending from the customer side are operations lead Lan and data engineer Minh.

Step 1: The last two minutes of the meeting are worth more than fifteen minutes afterward

The 15-minute clock starts before the meeting ends. Before everyone leaves the room, read the decisions aloud, then ask directly: "Who will do this, and by when?" Getting an answer on the spot is far faster than chasing it by email afterward.

Self-check: Every action item in your notes has a name next to it. Mark any missing name with a "?" to handle in Step 4 — don't guess.

Step 2: The "Decisions" section contains only what has been settled

Minutes 0 to 3: Open the template and copy in the decisions. A decision is something both sides agreed to; a point someone merely raised doesn't count. In the case, one decision might be: "The agent will be piloted on English-language tickets first; multilingual tickets will be handled in a later phase."

Granola points out that a decision without a specific owner has no one accountable for it. So if a decision creates work, move that work to the next section and assign an owner.

Self-check: Reread line by line and ask yourself: would the customer nod at this line? If you're unsure, it belongs in the open questions section.

Step 3: A task assigned to the whole team is a task no one does

Minutes 3 to 8 are the most critical. Granola recommends a simple format: task, owner, deadline. Vibe is stricter: each item gets one person, one task, one date.

GUVI explains the value of this: naming an owner makes accountability clear, important work becomes harder to forget, and vague promises like "we'll take a look" have nowhere to hide.

Applied to the case, the passive sentence at the start of this article becomes:

修改前：
数据会检查一下，团队会尽快回复您。

修改后：
1. 导出英文工单，客户信息做脱敏处理 – Minh – 10 月 29 日（周四）
2. 审核分类标签清单 – Lan – 10 月 30 日（周五）
3. 用该文件运行智能体并发送分类结果 – [你的名字] – 11 月 2 日（周一）

That original sentence actually contained three tasks for three people. Only after splitting it apart do you see that your task depends on Minh's — a point we'll use again later.

Self-check: Search your draft for "team," "will be," "soon," and "ASAP," and rewrite whatever remains. If the customer is in another time zone, follow GoTranscript's template and note the time zone next to the deadline.

Step 4: Turn every question mark into a question someone will answer

Minutes 8 to 11: Move the items marked "?" in Step 1 into the open questions section. Each question follows the same rule as action items: one answerer, one date. For example: "Do the tickets contain customer phone numbers, and do they need to be redacted before sending? – Lan to confirm – by Thursday, October 29."

The confirmation line at the end of the email matters more than it looks. PostHog's FDE handbook describes how the team puts the scope into a short document, sends it to the customer for confirmation first, and only then makes commitments. The meeting-summary email does the same thing on a smaller scale: once the customer confirms by a specific date, the scope is locked in writing.

GoTranscript's template also includes a line worth copying: if there is no reply, both sides proceed according to the listed items. With that sentence, even if the customer never responds, everyone knows which list the work follows.

Self-check: Every question has a specific answerer, and the confirmation line includes a specific date.

Step 5: Write it very short and send it within 24 hours

Minutes 11 to 15: Cut. Lengthy thanks, a blow-by-blow recap of the meeting, explanations of rationale — all can go. The 150-word cap forces you to keep only what the reader needs to act on.

The 150-word standard was set for English emails. When writing in other languages, keep its spirit: cut all narration, but not a single name or deadline.

On timing, both Willow Voice and Granola recommend sending within 24 hours of the meeting. Compressing the process into 15 minutes lets you start the moment you leave the room, rather than putting it off until the next day.

Self-check: The email contains all three sections, doesn't recap the discussion, and was sent within 24 hours.

What does an FDE's email need beyond the standard template?

Common meeting-email templates stop at "task – owner – deadline." In deployment projects, two more things matter that generic templates rarely mention: the dependency chain between customer tasks and your tasks, and the scope boundaries that need the customer's sign-off.

Back to the case. Your deadline of Monday, November 2 only holds if Minh's files arrive by Thursday, October 29. So note the dependency directly on your own line:

3. 运行智能体并发送分类结果 – [你的名字] – 11 月 2 日（周一）
   （依赖第 1 项 Minh 的文件；文件延迟，此截止日期随之顺延）

Suppose the files arrive on Friday instead of Thursday. You simply reply in the same email thread: the files arrived a day late, so the pilot results shift to Tuesday, November 3. No one needs to argue, because the original email already recorded who delivers what on which day, and which task is waiting on which.

Scope is the other half. The decision to "run English-language tickets first" sounds like a technical detail, but it is actually a scope boundary.

If someone on the customer's side asks weeks later why the agent doesn't yet support multilingual tickets, the answer is in an email the customer confirmed — exactly the kind of dispute GUVI says a written summary helps avoid.

Practical rule: whenever a decision made in the meeting narrows or expands what you need to deliver, put it at the top of the decisions section and mention it in the confirmation line.

Which mistakes make the email useless?

The most common mistake is writing the email as meeting minutes, recapping everyone's points and burying the action items in the last paragraph. Close behind is assigning one task to two people as "co-owners": with two names, each person easily assumes the other is doing it.

A subtler mistake is listing only the customer's tasks and omitting your own. Reading it, the customer will feel like you're assigning them work. Include your tasks too, and mark dependencies as described above, so the customer sees that both sides have work to do and deadlines to meet.

What use is this email thread to the customer?

During deployment, recap emails help everyone remember where the project stands. If someone new joins on the customer's side midway, or a deadline arrives without the data, a thread of emails with names and dates can answer the question with a single forward, saving a dedicated meeting to reconstruct "who said what."

For career development, this skill is easier to demonstrate than you might think. Instead of writing "strong communication skills" on your resume, describe how you consistently sent recap emails after every customer meeting, with action items that had owners, deadlines, and scope confirmed by the customer.

When interviewing for customer-facing roles, bring a sample with all customer information removed. A real, brief email with names, dates, and dependencies is more persuasive than any self-assessment.

The next time someone says "the team will look into it and get back to you soon," you'll know what to ask: who on the team, by what date, and where things stand on the work your task is waiting on.
