The problem was already there before the name appeared.

Original title: 名称出现之前，问题已经在那里

pafa.github.io · 8 min read

Summary

The article argues that studying FDE requires separating the history of the job from the history of the name. Customer-facing engineering long predates the term: around 1960 IBM created systems engineering roles where engineers wired control panels for clients and later moved into application design, and by 1969 some of these services became paid offerings. On naming, a 2009 Science report already described Palantir staff as forward-deployed engineers, while Shyam Sankar recalled in 2024 that he coined "forward deployed engineering" in 2007; the author treats these as distinct types of evidence. The piece proposes an open working definition—FDEs work close to customers and deliver substantive engineering—and stresses distinguishing cross-boundary labor from clear business accountability between product and service.

Key points

  • IBM created systems engineering roles around 1960; paid services began in 1969.
  • A 2009 Science report is the earliest verifiable public use of the FDE title.
  • Sankar's 2024 recollection of naming it in 2007 is not contemporaneous evidence.
  • The author defines FDEs as delivering substantive engineering near customers.
  • Labor can cross boundaries, but product-versus-service accountability must stay clear.

After a machine is handed over to a customer, the work has sometimes only just begun.

Around 1960, IBM's systems engineers helped customers wire control panels for accounting machines so the machines could fit specific business needs; later, their work increasingly involved application and systems design. This is the record left by IBM engineer T. G. Peck in 1985 when reviewing the company's systems engineering history. The article also notes that IBM formally announced the establishment of a group of systems engineering positions on December 5, 1960. 1

There was no large language model then, and no name like FDE. But the difficulty is easy to recognize: the vendor knows what the machine can do, the customer knows what work they need to accomplish, and between these two kinds of knowledge lies a distance that requires engineering labor to cross.

This distance changes shape. Control panels need wiring, software needs configuration, data needs interpretation, and automated systems need to know when to stop. Advances in tools can make some of this work disappear, and can also bring more work that was previously not worth doing or not feasible into people's plans. Thus, a recurring organizational question emerges: who walks to the user's side and turns technical capability into usable working methods?

To understand the FDE, it is best to start from here. Tracing its origins is not just about finding the moment when someone first uttered those three English words. We also need to distinguish how a kind of work emerged, how a name spread, and how companies assigned responsibility, authority, and resources to this work. Mixing these histories together makes it easy to write familiar labor as a miracle of sudden birth, and equally easy to overlook later changes in how the work was organized simply because it had precedents.

An old problem, without a single unified old name

Systems engineering, customer engineering, applications engineering, technical support—these names look similar, but the responsibilities they carried were not necessarily the same. Even within the same company during the same period, people serving large customers and those responsible for small system installations might face different kinds of work. The most reliable way to read this history is to look at what these people actually changed, rather than pasting today's job titles back onto the past.

Peck's article also records a business change: in 1969, IBM began offering some systems engineering services as paid options, including programming and detailed design; subsequently, paid services and branch-level marketing support gradually developed into different arrangements. 1 The same kind of technical labor could help sell products, or become a service customers purchased separately. Which department carried it and which revenue stream paid for it would affect how teams chose projects.

This point deserves a pause at the start of the FDE's history. Today's discussions of "whether it belongs to product or services" tend to imagine the two as naturally separate categories of people. But customers do not encounter problems according to the vendor's departmental boundaries. An installation may expose product defects, a paid service may produce tools worth reusing, and a technical judgment made during a sales phase may determine whether subsequent delivery is feasible. Labor can cross boundaries, but business accountability still needs to be clearly drawn.

Another historical record from IBM presents this intersection more concretely. The company's retrospective on its Information Management System (IMS) states that in 1963, IBM and customer-side engineers studied parts tracking and version management for an aerospace project; the design team also included other participants. The related technology was later aimed at the commercial market, and IBM announced the launch of IMS in 1968. 2 This book does not call this an early FDE project, and there is no evidence directly connecting this team to Palantir's later organization. But it provides a necessary comparison: turning customer-specific problems into sellable software was something the computer industry had long needed to handle.

In this comparison, the words "turning into" cannot be omitted. The fact that a parts management project worked does not mean it was naturally suited to other organizations. The names, rules, and staffing arrangements in the original project need to be identified: which were the habits of this one customer, and which were broader software capabilities? Once a feature entered the product, who would handle compatibility, documentation, and long-term maintenance? These questions do not get answered automatically just because the work originally came from an important customer.

Conversely, there is no need to demand that all on-site labor give birth to products. Repairing a device, completing a migration, or training a customer team may itself be a result worth purchasing. If that is what was promised from the start, it is unfair to dismiss such work with "it never became a platform." What truly needs vigilance is a mismatch between promises and the actual way of doing business: a company tells a story of repeatedly sellable products while its revenue long depends on people reinvesting effort each time; or a customer believes they have bought ongoing service while the vendor considers the engagement over after a single feature delivery.

Therefore, the prehistory of the FDE is not a table of successive title replacements. It is a history of several old problems being continually recombined: the distance between product and use, the distance between specialized knowledge and business experience, and the distance between the immediate project and the long-term product. These show that the new name has objects for comparison, but they do not prove a single line of inheritance exists among all those objects.

Two kinds of time records: participants' recollections and contemporaneous word evidence

On July 24, 2009, Science published John Bohannon's report on network analysis. The article opens by describing Palantir's Shyam Sankar and Asher Sinensky demonstrating data analysis, directly writing their job titles as forward-deployed engineers. 3

This is a useful contemporaneous record: by that date at the latest, this name had already appeared in public introductions of Palantir personnel. What it proves is clear, and also limited. The report does not say the position was established that day, does not announce who invented the name, and certainly does not give a complete lineage from the first employee to the whole organization.

The company's founding date is another kind of date. Palantir's listing documents place the company's founding in 2003. 4 This helps readers organize the company's history, but it cannot directly answer when FDE began to be used. Founding a company, releasing a product, and writing a recruiting description are all events that can be marked on a timeline, but they are not the same event.

In 2024, Shyam Sankar provided another clue in an article under his own name: he recalled that in 2007 he named this engineering arrangement forward deployed engineering. 5 Thus two time records need to be placed side by side here: 2007 is a participant's recollection of the naming made years later, and 2009 is the earliest contemporaneous public word evidence verified by this book. They can be cross-referenced, but they cannot be merged into a nonexistent 2007 document.

This treatment does not strip history of its story. On the contrary, it shifts attention away from fighting over "firsts" and toward the changes truly worth explaining: why did engineers not merely accept a requirements document, but instead participate directly in the customer's problems? What were they able to modify? And after the problem was solved, what did the software company leave behind? Chapter 3: Palantir's Field Work and Product will explore these questions through Palantir's specific materials; here, we first give readers a ruler for recognizing them.

It should also be noted that early records are not a certificate of success. The same Science report discussed the debate over the effectiveness of network analysis, reminding readers that connections in a model are not always the same as important connections in reality. 3 This makes the meaning of "being close to the problem" more complete: being close to users can help engineers understand information; being close to customers does not automatically guarantee that the information is reliable, the judgments are correct, or the uses are appropriate. Engineering deliverables still need to withstand scrutiny beyond their scope of use.

If this history is told only as engineers overcoming bureaucratic obstacles, readers will miss the other half of the responsibility. Customers can explain their business, but they may also carry their own parochial goals; systems provide stronger capabilities, but they may also amplify existing errors. A new position can help locate where errors occur, but it cannot replace the organization's judgment about purposes, evidence, and consequences.

What history changed is how engineering labor was organized

There are gaps between these records, and they cannot be drawn as a lineage passing directly from IBM to Palantir; but we can compare what changed.

IBM's 1969 materials turned part of engineering labor into a service that could be purchased separately. Business questions followed: which work would be covered by product sales, and which would require separate payment? Palantir's materials place a different tension into the division of roles: some people face customer outcomes, some build the shared product, and the two sides need constant back-and-forth. As can be seen in the chapter Palantir's Field and Product, allowing frontline contributions of code is not the same thing as the product team actually accepting suggestions.

There are two different historical explanations here. The first emphasizes technology: a customer's conditions of use cannot be exhausted by a single product specification, so joint engineering is needed. The second emphasizes business: vendors want to drive adoption and expand accounts, and are therefore willing to take on labor near the customer. Real organizations may be driven by both at once; an engineer helping to close a contract does not prove his product contributions were small, and having contributed to the product does not prove the whole model is economically sustainable.

Therefore, when tracing turning points, this book brings together customer difficulties, the scope a product can cover, the authority of engineering staff, and the sources of investment. Only by seeing the changes over time can we discuss why an organization needed to adjust. The emergence of a name is a locatable event; the persistence of a model requires more explanation.

First, separate the history of the name from the history of the work

To compare materials across different companies, this book adopts a deliberately open working definition: an FDE is someone who works close to customers' actual work and carries out substantive engineering delivery; Forward Deployed Engineering can also refer to the organizational approach that supports such work. "Substantive" here means that actual building and change can be pointed to. This book also tracks whether what is learned can flow back into software or platform building: this is an important question for judging whether an organizational arrangement can accumulate capability, and it does not require every project to ship a public feature.

This is a definition this book uses to ask questions, not a global professional certification. When a company calls itself FDE, we record its exact words and then check whether the responsibilities it claims match its public projects. When a company does not use the name but exhibits similar mechanisms, we note the similarities while preserving the difference in names. Merely using AI, working with customers, or having purchased software from a particular platform is not enough to be classified into the same organizational practice.

At this point two threads can be held simultaneously: customer-facing engineering labor predates the name FDE, and the naming may in turn push companies to reconfigure product and delivery responsibilities. Existing records are not yet sufficient to fill all the gaps between the two threads.
