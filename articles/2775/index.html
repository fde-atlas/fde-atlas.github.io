FDE roles grew 42x in hiring over two years — behind the high pay lies on-site work and burnout

Original title: FDE岗位两年招聘增长42倍 高薪背后是驻场与职业倦怠

虎嗅 · 2026-09-29 · 10 min read

Summary

This article follows an engineer nicknamed Yupi whose high-paying FDE job began with pasting QR codes on factory equipment. Citing LinkedIn's 2026 workforce report, it notes FDE job postings grew 42-fold globally between 2023 and 2025, far outpacing AI engineer roles at 13-fold. OpenAI launched deployment subsidiary DeployCo with over $4 billion, Anthropic formed a $1.5 billion joint venture with Blackstone and Goldman Sachs, and Google Cloud opened 1,500+ AI deployment roles. Salaries are high—OpenAI pays $162,000–$280,000, with senior packages reaching $350,000–$550,000. But the reality includes constant on-site travel, blurred role boundaries spanning engineering, pre-sales and operations, and burnout. The author argues FDE fills a genuine need, like SRE in the cloud era, though the hype contains title inflation and unproven economics for smaller clients.

Key points

  • FDE postings grew 42x globally in two years, versus 13x for AI engineers, per LinkedIn's 2026 report.
  • OpenAI, Anthropic and Google Cloud each committed billions or thousands of roles to AI deployment in May 2026.
  • FDEs own the 'last mile' from model to business value, juggling coding, pre-sales and on-site operations.
  • Six-figure salaries concentrate at top labs; most roles involve travel, vague boundaries and burnout.
  • The model faces untested economics below large enterprise clients, echoing China's ToB customization trap.

Full text

This article comes from the WeChat public account: 脑极体, author: Coral

Yu Pi never expected that after receiving an offer with a monthly salary of 50,000 yuan, his first day on the job would be going to a factory to stick QR codes.

The position he was hired for was called FDE, Forward Deployed Engineer. The job description listed production-grade Python coding, large model systems experience, and RAG setup; the interview covered Agent architecture and Prompt engineering. But at the client site—a manufacturing factory—the actual work involved crawling into the workshop, recording each machine's "nickname" amid the roar of machinery, and personally sticking QR codes onto the equipment for positioning.

No office towers, no floor-to-ceiling windows, no ready-made data pipelines—everything about the FDE role seemed far from AI's spotlight.

Yet on social media, golden narratives about FDEs keep emerging. Phrases like "the most future-proof job in the AI era," "earning 100,000 yuan a month," and "hybrid talent" have never left this position. Reportedly, during the two years when the entire industry was laying off workers, FDE hiring demand grew more than fortyfold. OpenAI's starting annual salary is over 1 million RMB, and ByteDance offers up to 70,000 yuan per month for its "Doubao AI Large Model FDE" role.

A position that was almost unknown in the past has, in just two years, become the AI industry's hottest "golden rice bowl." What exactly is it? Why has it risen against the tide of layoffs? And how much of the glamorous hiring data is inflated?

The tech industry in 2026 is experiencing an unprecedented split.

On one side, the layoff wave continues to spread. According to the monthly report from the US employment consulting firm Challenger, in the first half of 2026 alone, cumulative layoffs in the US tech industry approached 140,000 people. Amazon cut about 30,000 positions in a year, Meta laid off 8,000, and Oracle's total headcount dropped from 162,000 to 141,000 over the past twelve months—a 13% reduction. Oracle stated in its annual report: "The application and deployment of AI technology in our operations has resulted, and may continue to result, in a reduction in headcount."

On the other side, a position called FDE is surging at a staggering rate against the trend. LinkedIn's 2026 Workforce Report shows that between 2023 and 2025, global FDE job postings grew 42-fold, while AI engineer positions grew only 13-fold in the same period. ByteDance offers FDE experts a monthly salary of 30,000 to 50,000 yuan with 15 months' pay per year, Ant Digital offers 40,000 to 60,000 yuan per month, and Zhipu Huazhang's FDE lead position pays 60,000 to 80,000 yuan per month. In the US, OpenAI offers FDE annual salaries between $162,000 and $280,000, while Anthropic offers $200,000 to $300,000.

When Yu Pi saw these numbers, he was scrolling on his phone at his desk at his previous company. It was a mid-sized enterprise services company where he had done six years of backend development—written Python, tuned APIs, and built some small-scale RAG projects. The company had a round of layoffs at the start of the year; he stayed, but the colleague at the next desk left, and the workloads of those who remained grew heavier. Yu Pi started browsing job apps, and the term FDE kept appearing.

After some research, Yu Pi decided to submit his resume. When he went to Beijing for the interview, the interviewer asked how he understood "production-grade large model systems." He talked about the retrieval-augmented generation pipelines he had built and the clients he had worked with, and passed the interview, becoming an FDE at a top tech company.

Among the colleagues who had left his company, some did algorithms at big tech firms, some worked on Agents at startups. When they heard he was going to be an FDE, reactions split into two camps: one said "isn't this just high-end outsourcing?" and the other said "I heard this role is insanely hot—you're so lucky."

To understand FDE, you have to go back to its birthplace. The term was first coined by Palantir in the mid-2000s. At the time, its government and defense clients had extremely sensitive data environments and highly customized architectures, making remote delivery simply impossible. An architect who flew away after delivering a solution left the client with a pile of tickets to process, not a working system. So Palantir simply embedded its own engineers long-term at client sites—people who could both write code and understand the business—and thus the FDE was born.

Unlike traditional AI engineers or ML engineers, FDEs don't write core model code; instead, they embed model capabilities into clients' business systems, bridging the "last mile" from model to value. Simply put, AI engineers are responsible for model metrics, while FDEs are responsible for business outcomes.

Technology, business, and trust—none of the three can be missing, and that's precisely why compensation has been pushed ever higher. LinkedIn data shows the median annual salary for FDEs is about $199,000, while mid-level FDE total packages at frontier labs like OpenAI and Anthropic generally fall in the $350,000 to $550,000 range, and senior or even Principal levels breaking $1 million is not uncommon. Translated to the domestic context, FDEs at top companies like ByteDance and Alibaba commanding million-RMB annual salaries are everywhere. Yu Pi was one of those swept up in this high-salary hiring wave.

So why did FDE suddenly explode in popularity? What undeniable forces are behind its breakout?

The fundamental reason for FDE's rise lies in a simple judgment: model capabilities are being rapidly leveled, but three things cannot be leveled in the short term—integrating models into legacy systems, passing security and compliance reviews, and getting frontline employees to actually use them.

In May 2026, three of the world's top AI companies made almost the same move at the same time. Anthropic, together with Blackstone, Goldman Sachs, and other institutions, launched a joint venture with $1.5 billion in committed capital, whose core business is helping enterprises deploy Claude. OpenAI established an independent deployment subsidiary, DeployCo, with an initial investment of over $4 billion. Less than two weeks apart, Google Cloud's CEO publicly posted a large-scale recruitment call for FDEs, with over 1,500 AI deployment-related positions opened internally. All three companies reached the same conclusion: selling APIs alone is no longer enough. After receiving the models, clients often get stuck at "don't know how to use it," "can't connect the data," "results are unstable," or "afraid to use it after launch"—they need a role that goes down to the field and actually makes things work.

According to Liepin's report, FDE positions are highly concentrated in four cities—Shanghai, Beijing, Shenzhen, and Hangzhou—together accounting for 68.33%. By industry, AI/internet remains the largest source of demand, accounting for 72.36%.

When Yu Pi first entered the FDE world, he naively assumed that client engagement would take up only a small portion of his time, and that most of the time he would still be in an office tower, endlessly attending meetings, writing code, and aligning on plans.

But Yu Pi quickly discovered that among his client engagements, traditional industries like retail and manufacturing had gradually become the tail and the bulk—FDE was expanding from an internal buzzword in the AI circle to the whole industry and society.

And this process would not be easy. His first client was a manufacturing factory. The client wanted AI predictive maintenance, but the factory had never even collected basic data like temperature, vibration, or runtime. Yu Pi had to settle for second best: first straightening out the repair request process, letting AI help workers organize repair information, while structurally saving each fault description and repair result to accumulate data for the future.

It's not just about AI capability—what FDEs face is a question of organizational capability, process maturity, and data infrastructure. What an FDE can do is expose these problems one by one, then take the first step with a minimal viable solution.

Only after three months on-site did Yu Pi gradually figure out what FDEs are actually selling.

Selling outsourced custom projects is the most common and most exhausting model. Clients pay, you go on-site to customize and deliver, and you earn project fees. They throw the task of "figuring out the problem" to you. Many clients haven't even figured out what they want themselves, and the requirements evaporate mid-conversation. Over-customization also makes it hard to distill into a product, with a visibly low ceiling. Yu Pi's first project was exactly this kind: the client initially said they wanted "AI predictive maintenance," but it wasn't until the third week of discussions that they discovered the real problem was that quality inspection standards themselves were not unified.

The second is selling product implementation: having a mature Agent product and sending people to help clients connect data and systems. It's called FDE, but it's essentially implementation. Yu Pi later moved to this track, and only then did he actually use the things written in the job description.

The third is selling knowledge: doing consulting and training, avoiding heavy delivery, helping bosses and employees understand AI and transform their working methods. A peer Yu Pi knows runs a one-person company doing AI infrastructure consulting for traditional industries, with projects booked solid for three months, and is already preparing to expand hiring.

Others skip delivery and go into matchmaking instead—running communities and hackathons to connect people looking to switch careers with companies urgently needing AI transformation.

Yupi found that, from this angle, the FDE is like a translator, translating AI's capabilities into solutions enterprises can understand; a sensor, feeding the enterprise's real pain points back to the product team; and a catalyst, proving through AI-driven efficiency gains in certain areas that the work is worth continuing.

Any job that blows up inevitably gets watered down, and FDE is no exception.

The million-dollar-salary narrative circulates online, but the real experience of frontline FDEs is far less glamorous than the recruiting copy suggests.

One employee abroad even complained: "FDE is simply the worst role I've had since becoming an engineer."

From Yupi's personal experience, being on-site is the norm and drifting is the price. In the early stages, sales, delivery, and after-sales support are all on you alone. At his busiest, he worked nearly 50 hours a week, with three days a week on-site or traveling. Mornings were spent at the client's site in hours of meetings to confirm requirements; the rest of the time was spent coding—seven or eight hours straight when deadlines were tight, sometimes even leaving an AI agent an extra-long task before bed to run overnight. A former Palantir FDE described being on-site 3 to 4 days a week, working at one client for about a year, on a team of 4 to 5 people.

But exhaustion isn't the hardest part. The hardest part is never knowing what role you'll have to play tomorrow.

An FDE at a domestic AI company admitted there's almost no fixed office, weekend emergency support is routine, and long-term travel has thrown many people's schedules into disarray. You have to write high-quality code and hunt bugs like an R&D engineer, handle requirements and pitch solutions like a pre-sales engineer, and deal with on-site failures and coordinate multiple teams to push projects forward like an ops engineer—playing several roles at once, with a very low error tolerance, since customer satisfaction is directly tied to performance reviews and on-site problems must be solved immediately.

In Yupi's third week at the factory, the client's boss pulled him aside and asked: "Can your AI predict which machine will break down tomorrow?" Yupi said they'd need data first. The boss said: "Isn't data being recorded all along?" Yupi followed the workers to look—the so-called "records" were handwritten in an oil-stained ledger, in scrawled handwriting, with some dates even written wrong. He spent considerable time typing the ledger into Excel, then another week persuading the workshop supervisor to have workers fill in two extra columns every day.

Ironically, this preliminary work, which had nothing to do with AI, directly determined whether AI could even get started.

Moreover, not every company leader hoping to improve quality and efficiency with AI understands the most basic principles of AI, so frontline FDEs juggling multiple roles also need to practice explaining things like agents and skills in plain language to traditional-business novices. After all, the final deal-closing rate also depends on the FDE.

This explains why burnout has become a buzzword in FDE circles. Frequent travel, compound stress, and blurred professional boundaries are pain points shared by nearly all frontline practitioners.

That said, even for those juggling multiple roles and rushing around day and night, the social media stories of "solo freelancers earning 100k a month" or "zero-experience career switchers earning a million a year" don't apply to everyone. FDEs who actually earn million-yuan salaries are concentrated in top AI companies and elite consulting firms, and typically require 5+ years of engineering experience plus client-facing work experience, like Yupi's—and even then it's not stable. Hence FDE's other nickname: "on-site outsourcing."

Anyone who's done ToB business in China knows the pain: clients want customization, private deployment, and on-site presence. In the end, SaaS becomes a project business, and a product company becomes an outsourcing company. Over the past decade, many ToB startups have crashed and burned on this path.

The FDE narrative is valuable in Silicon Valley because two premises hold: first, there's a reusable platform behind it; second, frontline discoveries can feed back into the product, making the next client's deployment faster and cheaper.

Customized delivery means labor costs grow linearly with the number of clients, which contradicts the software industry's pursuit of decreasing marginal costs. Palantir can sustain this model because its deal sizes are large enough and its clients are concentrated in government and large enterprises. But when the FDE model trickles down to small and mid-sized clients that need AI, whether per-client profit can cover on-site costs is an unproven question.

Many FDE workers complain that some clients are simply freeloading—paying a small fee to acquire AI knowledge, then canceling the deal and building their own workflows. But in an era when AI hasn't yet fully penetrated the market, pricing too high upfront would lose 90% of potential clients.

Still, dismissing FDE entirely as a rebrand is too simplistic. Whenever a technology moves from the lab to production, a role dedicated to "making it run" inevitably emerges. The cloud computing era gave rise to SRE and DevOps; the large model era is giving rise to the FDE. SRE was once also seen as a variant of ops, before becoming an independent technical discipline. FDE may be following the same path.

The FDE boom has a solid foundation of real demand, but it's not a golden rice bowl suited to everyone.

On the positive side, the growth speed this role offers is rare. An engineer who has spent three months embedded at a client's site may understand AI implementation better than a back-office team does after three years of iteration. On the other hand, the role's boundaries are being diluted. Some analysts point out that as demand surges, more and more positions with the FDE title are actually sliding toward pre-sales, solutions architecture, or even customer success.

AI moving from the lab into business workflows does require a group of people who understand both engineering and business, and are willing to get their hands dirty on-site. This demand won't disappear when the bubble recedes. But within that 42x growth figure, there is indeed a mix of title inflation, role consolidation, and unproven value expectations.

Yupi, who has plunged into this world, doesn't know whether he's caught a rising wind or walked through a narrow door that's been carefully packaged. He only knows that tomorrow at 8 a.m., he still has to be at the workshop on time.
