How to Read FDE Job Requirements: A Case Study of Together AI

Original title: FDE 招聘要求怎么读：以 Together AI 为例

FDE Times · 2026-11-08 · 6 min read

Summary

Using three Together AI job postings from 2026, this article teaches how to read FDE job descriptions properly. The same Forward Deployed Engineer title covers three distinct roles: validating GPU clusters before delivery, selecting and tuning inference engines, and advising on post-training methods and data strategy. Two roles share the same $270,000–300,000 OTE range despite requiring different preparation. The author recommends starting with the verbs in each posting, and building evidence with measured benchmarks for every requirement. A worked example shows how to optimize a high-traffic chatbot: enable prefix caching and chunked prefill before trying speculative decoding.

  • Two postings list identical US base ranges of $270,000–300,000 OTE.
  • Verbs like select, configure, optimize, and test describe the actual daily work.

Key Takeaways

  • At Together AI, the FDE title maps to three different jobs: GPU cluster validation, inference optimization, and post-training.

  • For each requirement, prepare evidence with measured data, and be ready to answer the questions customers are certain to ask.

In Together AI's 2026 job postings, a single Forward Deployed Engineer title corresponds to three jobs. The first engineer validates GPU clusters before they are delivered to customers; the second selects and tunes inference engines; the third helps customers choose post-training methods and develop data strategies.

If you just skim the posting and prepare for a "generic FDE" role, you might spend a month grinding system design and algorithm questions, when the posting actually cares about questions like: why does a certain engine run slowly on a certain class of hardware.

Reading a job posting is itself a skill. It closely resembles what a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) does on-site with customers: taking a requirement written in business language and turning it into measurable technical work.

Together AI's three postings are all written very concretely, making them excellent material for practicing this skill. The same reading approach applies to any FDE job posting at an AI infrastructure company.

Three postings, three different jobs

The Strategic Inference role requires the FDE to select, configure, and optimize inference engines for each customer, and demands expert-level hands-on experience with engines such as vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, or SGLang.

The GPU Clusters role is infrastructure-centric: running test suites before clusters are delivered to customers, and troubleshooting low-level bottlenecks in InfiniBand networks and NVLink topologies. This posting was taken down on June 8, 2026, but it remains a useful reference for understanding infrastructure-oriented FDE roles.

According to Together AI's product pages, its clusters range from instantly available self-service clusters to deployments with thousands of GPUs. At this scale, a faulty node that slips through validation is no longer an internal issue—it becomes an incident in a customer's system.

The Post-Training role requires the FDE to recommend the appropriate post-training method for each use case and help customers develop a matching data strategy.

The Inference and Post-Training postings list the same US base salary range: $270,000–300,000 OTE. The pay is identical, but the work content and preparation paths are completely different.

The verbs in the posting are your daily work

When reading a posting, look at the verbs first, then the nouns. The Inference posting states that the FDE will select, configure, and optimize inference engines based on hardware, model architecture, and workload characteristics.

One sentence, three verbs, three variables. So in your preparation, practice answering: if only one of these three variables changed, what would you tune first, and why?

The GPU Clusters posting uses the verbs "design and execute" for the pre-delivery test suite, and specifically names NCCL, DCGM, and GPU Burn. The phrase "pre-delivery" indicates that this test suite is the final gate before the cluster reaches the customer.

NCCL tests check communication bandwidth between GPUs via operations like all-reduce; DCGM runs health diagnostics on each GPU; GPU Burn keeps GPUs running under sustained high load so that failures surface before customers encounter them.

Example: turning the Inference posting into a practice project

Suppose your customer runs a high-traffic customer service chatbot. Every prompt is long because it carries conversation history, while responses are short. This is a concrete workload profile: prefill-heavy, decode-light. Faced with this workload, what would you tune first?

The Inference posting names three levers: tuning the KV cache, using speculative decoding, and choosing the optimal tensor parallelism. The KV cache stores the keys and values of already-processed tokens, avoiding redundant computation, at the cost of occupying GPU memory.

Speculative decoding lets a small model guess the next few tokens first, and the large model only needs to verify them. Tensor parallelism splits each layer's matrices across multiple GPUs.

One detail worth noting: the two techniques best suited to this chatbot are not mentioned in the posting.

The vLLM documentation explains that automatic prefix caching only shortens the prefill phase; in multi-turn conversations, it can reuse already-computed results instead of reprocessing the entire chat history every time.

The heaviest part of this workload is prefill, so the first step is enabling prefix caching.

The second step is chunked prefill. According to the vLLM documentation, this technique batches large prefills together with requests that are decoding, and prioritizes decode, thereby improving inter-token latency. When prompts are long, it prevents other users' responses from stalling mid-generation every time a heavy prompt arrives.

Speculative decoding comes later. The vLLM documentation notes that it mainly helps reduce inter-token latency at low-to-medium QPS and when the workload is memory-bandwidth-bound. A high-traffic, short-response chatbot falls outside this range—enabling it first takes effort and yields little benefit.

So a good answer looks like this: first measure the current time-to-first-token and inter-token latency; enable prefix caching and measure again; add chunked prefill and measure again; only try speculative decoding when the data shows decode is the real bottleneck. Following this line of thinking, you can organize every requirement in the posting into a preparation table:

Requirement in the postingQuestion the customer will askEvidence you need to prepare
Select an engine based on hardware and workload"Should we use vLLM, SGLang, or TensorRT-LLM?"Benchmarks of two engines on the same model and the same set of prompts
KV cache tuning"Why does memory run out as soon as concurrent users increase?"Throughput curves as the cache-reserved memory limit varies
Speculative decoding"How can we make responses come out faster?"Latency comparison with the draft model on and off, with a clear explanation of when it doesn't help
Tensor parallelism"What if the model doesn't fit on one GPU?"Latency and throughput comparison when the model is split across different numbers of GPUs
Optimize for the workload profile"What should my chatbot enable first?"TTFT and ITL measured after each step, in the order above

The third column matters most. "Having used vLLM" does not prove expert-level hands-on experience; a repo containing benchmark scripts, measured data, and a write-up explaining why the results came out the way they did does.

Apply the same method to the other two postings

For the GPU Clusters posting, the customer's question is almost always: "Is this cluster ready?" Good evidence is a delivery checklist you wrote yourself, specifying which tests run in what order, what thresholds count as passing, and how to handle failures.

If you don't have a large cluster to experiment with, renting a few GPUs for a few hours is enough to run NCCL tests and DCGM diagnostics, then walk through the results item by item. An all-reduce bandwidth below expectations makes a perfect starting point for the InfiniBand and NVLink troubleshooting that the job posting asks for.

Two phrases in the Post-Training posting are worth noting: "the right post-training approach for each use case" and "data strategy". This suggests the difficulty lies more in choosing the right method and the right data than in the model itself.

The evidence you need to prepare is a single fine-tune of a small model: build an evals set before training, present a before-and-after comparison table, and then explain in a short paragraph why you chose this approach for this use case.

The resume advice in all three postings is the same: replace "familiar with vLLM, KV cache" with a single sentence that clearly states the context, what you changed, and the measured numbers before and after. A line like that shows the reader cause and effect, not a string of keywords.

Common Preparation Pitfalls

The most common pitfall is preparing from a generic template. Applying for an Inference role but only talking about RAG and prompts is off topic; the same goes for applying for a role like GPU Clusters without ever having read the output of NCCL tests.

The second pitfall is staying at the architecture level. If your interview answers contain only system architecture diagrams and never get down to engine configuration, you probably haven't touched the work described in the posting: selecting, configuring, optimizing.

The third pitfall is memorizing the list of technologies in the posting and applying it to all workloads. The chatbot example above shows that the named technology isn't necessarily the first step; what the interviewer wants to hear is how you derive your choices from the workload and back them up with measured data.

The next time you open an FDE job posting, don't ask yourself whether you're qualified; instead, for each requirement, ask: what evidence do I already have on GitHub that I can show?
