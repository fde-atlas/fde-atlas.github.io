Look for the Spark: A Conversation with Boaz Francis

FDE Hub · 2026-11-17 · 20 min read

Summary

An interview with Boaz Francis, who leads Forward Deployed Engineering globally at Wonderful after roughly five years leading deployments at Palantir and running an accelerator that onboarded about 80 startups. He discusses how to recognize whether you are an FDE, why ex-founders thrive in the role, the 'spark' he looks for in interviews, whether FDEs can be trained, and how hiring differs between Palantir and an early-stage startup building FDE culture from scratch.

Key points

  • FDEs sit between engineering and customer work, enjoying both.
  • About one in six or seven of their FDEs are ex-founders.
  • Interviews test composure, idea generation, and agency under pressure.
  • Palantir hires new grads; new sites need experienced hires first.
  • Culture and environment can turn the right raw talent into FDEs.

Full text

Boaz Francis leads Forward Deployed Engineering globally at Wonderful, working with the local CTOs and deployment teams in each of the more than thirty countries where it operates. Before that, he spent five years at Palantir leading deployments in the US, London and the EU, was an FDE hiring manager for about three years, and set up a startup accelerator that onboarded around 80 startups onto Palantir’s AI platform. We talked about how you know if you’re an FDE, why ex-founders do so well in the role, the spark he looks for in interviews, and whether you can train an FDE at all.

You started as a software engineer, but very quickly moved into forward deployed engineering. What brought you there, and why did you pick it?

I studied computing, then did a master’s in computing with AI and ML, and wanted to go a very technical route. I started as a software engineer in investment banking and wanted to go deep into systems and see how they work.

Quite quickly I realised that writing the code itself wasn’t the thing that was the most exciting to me. I was much more excited by seeing how things fit into an organisation. I would be the person to go to the trading desks and try to understand how they use our product, not just get the tickets and read them.

Over time I got better at that and became the contact person for different parts of the organisation, and started moving in a much more customer-facing, technical way. Then I met a friend I studied with who went to Palantir after university. I was describing the things that I like and the things that I’m frustrated by, and he said, “I think you’re a Delta. I think you’re an FDE.”

What even is an FDE? He said, “I have to refer you today, and you’ll just figure it out.” Very quickly after I started at Palantir, I was like, my God, I’ve always been an FDE. That’s my thing.

Now you’re Head of FDE at Wonderful. What attracted you to leave Palantir, join an early-stage startup and build it up to where it is at the moment?

I was always excited by startups, which is also what drew me to doing the startup accelerator and getting closer to that scene at Palantir. I absolutely loved the work and everything I was doing, and I ended up getting connected with the team at Wonderful.

I think it was the first time that I left a conversation with one of the startups and I was like, my God, they really do believe that this is going to be the next big thing. It was still a small startup, and I was thinking, how do you go from a large company like Palantir to a smaller startup like Wonderful at the time?

But the opportunity was very exciting. It was FDE from the beginning: how do we set up this organisation in a way that would actually get us all the benefits of forward deployed engineering in the AI era? That was very exciting to me, and so was the value that I bring from having deployed for about five years. To me it was a great opportunity that was very hard to miss.

Can you set the scene for where Wonderful is today, in terms of the organisation and what you do?

Wonderful is the AI OS for enterprises. We think about ourselves as the AI operating system for a business. We do everything from the data connections and integrations into your existing architecture through to building workflows. We think about them as customer-facing workflows, employee-facing workflows, and back office operations, which might be more to do with internal ops, processes and automations.

The overarching goal is bridging the gap between AI capabilities, which today are extremely impressive, and what enterprises actually end up putting in production, which in some cases is very far behind what the capabilities are. That gap is what we do with our product and deployment.

There’s huge demand for FDEs. How do you hire people for a role that emerged just about a year and a half ago? What do you look at, and what should people interested in moving into this role pay attention to?

Maybe to start with: how do you know if you’re an FDE? It’s kind of confusing sometimes, looking from the outside. What do you actually do day to day as an FDE?

Some people are incredibly technical, top engineers, but they’re not interested in being an FDE. If you’re the kind of person that is super excited by building, putting your headphones on and coding away all day and not really speaking to anyone, you’re probably not going to enjoy being an FDE, even if you’re a top software developer.

If you think about FDEs, it’s usually people that are missing something. Maybe they’re coming from software, but they’re missing seeing the value of the things they’re building actually in the hands of the people who use the software. Or people that come from more of the customer side. They really understand the business, but they never really take it through to running things in production and going through the end-to-end.

So if you’re in that sweet spot where you like to do both, you can flex your technical muscles, you’re quite versatile, and you get excited by pitching to a CEO and showing your work, then I think that space in between is probably where FDEs shine.

There aren’t many people with all these skills. Some are good engineers who see themselves in an FDE role and would like to work more with customers. Others are comfortable with customers and have the communication skills, but don’t have a technical background. How do they get to a point where they can successfully fulfil this role?

First of all, with the FDEs that we’re hiring at Wonderful, you need to have some technical background. You’re with clients, yes, but you’re actually building systems, you’re producing and contributing back to the platform. You need to be able to jump into the architecture of an organisation and understand where we come in and what makes sense.

So if you have no technical background and you want to be an FDE, the first thing to do is teach yourself. Build your project at home. There are a ton of different ways that you can learn today.

If you’re earlier on in your career, maybe from a science or engineering background, and you think this is maybe for you, then just try it out. Go build a thing. Try to spot a problem around you and build a solution for it. If you see that you enjoy that and you repeat the process a few times, then maybe that’s the path for you.

But a lot of it is also in the personal attributes. Tech is a big part of it, obviously, and experience helps. But you really need to be the kind of person that gets excited by the challenge, gets excited by the uncertainty. We always say ambiguity.

A lot of what an FDE does is: we have a customer, they have some problem, and we need to figure out how to build a solution that makes sense. That sometimes is very broad, and you need to understand not only the technical side but also the operational side. What are the current processes? Who owns the data, who owns the decision, who even cares? Once the decision gets made, what happens in practice?

If you have that grit and that motivation and that curiosity, and I always say this, the spark as well: when things get tough, you light up and you start coming up with new ideas. That’s the kind of mindset that you need to succeed as an FDE.

Would it be fair to say there’s a minimum technical bar you need to clear, which may differ by company, but once you clear it, the other skills make the difference between a really good candidate and one that is good enough?

When we say FDE right now, we’re talking about forward deployed engineer. If we think about forward deployed engineering, we do the standard pod structure of implementation teams or deployment teams that have an FDE, the forward deployed engineer, and a deployment strategist. Deployment strategists usually come more from the outcome and value world: how do we actually make a difference for a business? How do we measure our success? How do we achieve some outcome that will meaningfully generate value?

For deployment strategists, it really, really helps if they have some form of technical understanding, because at the end of the day you need to sit in front of a client and talk credibly about the solution we’re building. If you don’t understand it, it’s going to be very hard for you to do.

But if you’re the CTO type that gets excited by the technical direction, making sure that things work and things will scale, and that we’re building the right thing, then FDE is for you. If you’re more of the CEO type, how do we become a partner, how do we position ourselves, how do we make sure we’re working on the right problems to solve, then deployment strategist is another way of getting into forward deployed engineering.

Are there particular roles that are a good starting point for moving into FDE? Solutions engineer, software engineer, customer success?

If we think about where people come from, we have, I think, about one in six or seven ex-founders. We actually see that people that come from entrepreneurship, and have taken a bet on themselves to figure out how to make something work in the real world from scratch, tend to do very well as FDEs. It’s basically the same job, just with the support of Wonderful behind you to help you through the process, and the resources and the platform to give you the leverage. We do that part for you, and then you can just come and solve cool problems with customers.

So that’s the one type. Then there are people that went into industry, maybe as software engineers, usually from app dev kind of worlds. We even see some people from the DevOps side of the technical industry that end up missing some of the more outcome-based, workflow type of work. So there’s quite a spectrum.

To the solutions engineering point, I think yes. If you’re frustrated by only being able to build the first stage, and you want to actually continue and make it work in practice, in production, then that could be a good avenue for you to grow.

When you sit in a room with candidates, there are guidelines, but there’s also intuition: you talk to someone, it clicks, and you see this person can be a good FDE. What is that? Can you describe it in words, or do you just know when you meet someone who’s good?

When you meet someone who’s truly exceptional, you leave the interview and you’re like, wow. It’s not always super straightforward to point a finger at it and say, that’s why I was so impressed. Which I think is a feature of how differently FDEs manifest in different profiles, and the kind of spikes that they can have.

But the most important thing is you need to see that passion, you need to see that idea generation, you need to see the technical capabilities and intuition, but you need to see that spark that I was mentioning.

Especially in the way that we do interviews, coming from the decomposition style of interview, you get put in a situation with a lot of pressure and a very clear goal that you’re trying to achieve, and things might get complicated along the way. If you go at it from different directions and continue with a positive attitude, coming up with solutions, then you know. If we’re trying to deploy wherever we are, and we have a deadline that we need to meet and some problem we need to solve, is this person going to get excited and trust that they can figure it out?

When I was starting as an FDE, someone asked me if I could do something, and I said, “Yeah, let me give it a try.” And they were like, “That’s unacceptable. Either you tell me, yes, I’ll do it, and you go and figure out how to do it, or you tell me, no, I can’t do it, and tell me why.”

I actually really like that as a mindset. If you’re the kind of person that, if you say yes and commit to something, you know that whatever it is, you’ll figure out how to make it work, I think you would really like that kind of job.

Is that what they call high agency these days?

We always say we want to see agency, we want to see urgency, we want to see all of those things. When I talk about agency, I’m talking about someone who’s going to go and make something work without having the structure of being told what to do or being given a responsibility directly. If you see a gap around you and you tend to just gravitate toward it and go and solve it, or take ownership over it and start running, then that’s the kind of agency that I’m looking to see.

I’d love to hear about the differences in hiring at Palantir versus now at Wonderful. Wonderful’s trajectory is pointing towards reaching Palantir, but at this moment they’re still at very different stages. What would you say those differences are?

One realisation I had, coming from the place of FDEs that is Palantir to a new environment where the FDE culture and the organisation itself are being built: first of all, it is a very exciting space. But one of the main differences is how you put that motion in place, in the way that we talk about FDE, with independence and accountability actually being in the hands of FDEs.

One thing that is very common at Palantir, for example, is hiring directly from university. Very early talent, new grads. When you see all those raw skills, all the right ingredients in someone to be successful as an FDE, and you drop them into an environment that has a ton of support around them, people who’ve been FDEs for many years, then that person ends up shaping up very quickly to become an FDE. Some people take slightly longer, some people do it straight away, but you have that little bit of a buffer of people showing you the ropes to ramp up.

For us at Wonderful, and actually I think that’s already updated a little bit since we first spoke, imagine us starting up a new site in a new country. If the first person that you hire has all the right skills in a raw fashion, but they don’t have any experience, they’re going to find it very, very difficult to put things in motion and scale up the team themselves. So one thing that we found is that I look for some of that experience in the smaller sites earlier on.

Now, when a lot of our countries are reaching maturity, we see the same thing that happens at Palantir. If we bring someone who’s earlier talent and they land in an environment that already works as a forward deployed engineering organisation, then they can ramp up very quickly. There’s that tipping point where experience starts to matter less or more in different environments.

Everyone suddenly wants to hire FDEs, and there’s just not enough supply. Do you go for candidates who need additional skills and try to shape them, or find experienced candidates and try to attract them from companies where they’re already FDEs?

One underlying belief that we have is that you can’t compromise on the talent. You can’t bring someone who’s only just missing a little bit. On the other hand, if you have someone that you can see has it all in place, all the different ingredients to be successful, but maybe something is not quite mature enough or not all the way there, I think it’s almost like a bet that you’re taking on that person to grow. That kind of person is actually very exciting, because the upside is very high.

In terms of where people come from, maybe a cool little anecdote. I was speaking earlier this week to a bunch of different people that are building FDE organisations, and we had this conversation: what do you do? We can’t all just hire ex-Palantir people. The whole discussion was, can you train an FDE, or do they have to just be an FDE?

The conclusion for some people was, okay, so we have to hire ex-Palantir, but how do we do it? And then I actually said, you know, Palantir doesn’t hire ex-Palantir people. And that still works.

So I think there’s a much bigger part that is the culture of the organisation and the environment that you’re creating. If you put the people with the right attributes, the right kind of person, in that environment, they end up becoming an FDE. So the backgrounds probably matter less if you’re getting that right kind of person.

Let’s talk about culture. You have a somewhat unique situation at Wonderful where you stand up a country as such, around thirty-five countries or so. How do you preserve the culture of the whole FDE team, down to the level of a country, with people who are just starting in this role?

It’s definitely a challenge. If you think about the regular model of having three hubs, then it’s much easier when everyone’s in one place to make sure that everyone is thinking the same. It’s a thing that we actively put effort toward. To me, this is one of the most important parts of building a successful organisation: making sure that the environment is right and the culture is supportive.

How we do it: we always try to do some level of face to face. We bring people over, we fly people around. We do a lot of collaboration in that way, to make sure that people are connected, and that if you’re in a little site that is just starting out in some new country, you don’t feel like you’re not part of something that is much bigger, that is Wonderful.

Do you have a formal onboarding process, or do you throw people into the deep end of the pool and make sure they figure it out?

I think it’s a little bit of both. We do have an onboarding process that shows you the platform and the organisation, and helps you get introduced to what Wonderful is and our vision as well.

One thing that is very important for us to bring up very early on: if you look at our trajectory, you can tell straight away we’re very ambitious about where we’re aiming to get to. For us to do this in a way that is excellent and continues to grow, people need to be part of that vision. We hire excellent people, super strong people with agency and urgency and all of that, but really understanding that we have this bigger mission of bridging that gap with AI and enterprises, and doing that at a global scale, is something that we’re trying to convey very early on.

Do you want to share more about your plans, particularly in terms of hiring? I know you’re expanding the team in the UK and looking for FDEs to join.

We’re hiring out a team. We’re hiring basically anywhere that you can think of, across Europe, UK, LatAm, APAC. We’re looking for those FDEs that I mentioned before: the people that are super excited to go in and figure things out, and feel like they want to actually make a difference.

If you’re an ex-founder, or if you think that you’re a future founder, I actually think FDE is a really good stepping stone to jump into that space, where you get the autonomy and you get the ambiguity and the open space to create, but you still have the backing of the company.

We’re hiring FDEs, deployment strategists and a little bit of GTM all across the globe. So if you’re interested in the role, you know who to reach out to.

I’ve heard it a few times from different people in the space: not only are ex-founders really good candidates for FDE roles, but FDEs are also likely to go and start their own business. What happens there, that you suddenly become really keen on starting your own business after spending a number of years as an FDE?

I think it’s about problem solving. The biggest learning that I can say I had is, yes, you learn the tech, you learn how enterprises work, and you learn all the different industries that you pick up along the way. But the essence of an FDE is achieving outcomes. In order to become really good at that, you need to be able to come in and very quickly understand how to solve the problem.

So you go and solve another problem and another problem, and you see similarities between different places, and how maybe I can use some of the knowledge I already have from a different place to solve a new problem. You continuously do that as you’re working as an FDE. You get dropped into unfamiliar places and you need to very, very quickly figure them out, understand them, learn the business, learn the tech, and just produce something of value.

So you get used to spotting problems around you and trying to think how you would solve them. And I think sometimes that gets you passionate enough to then decide to try and start a company around it.

They say if you can build and you can sell, you’re in a really good place. Problem solving is part of building. But selling matters too: as an FDE, you always have to sell something to different stakeholders and get buy-in, from the highest level down to the operators who are going to use the product. Would it be fair to say you gain those skills as well?

I think saying sales is a little bit, I don’t want to say misleading. One thing that maybe I didn’t mention before, which is a big part of the role, is that you have to be really opinionated. You are in the best position there is inside an enterprise. You’re talking to the ops guys, you’re talking to the business, you’re talking to IT, and you have access to leadership.

So you’re sitting in the middle of this new organisation, you see all the different parts from the outside, and then you have access to actually go and come up with some solution or a workflow or an agent that is going to make a difference to that business. Being able to take that all in and form your own opinions of what is the right thing for us to do now, and what is the right thing for us to do next.

I think just being able to take that in, make your own opinion, and then share that opinion. I think people like that, especially an enterprise that has all of these problems around and is trying to figure out what to tackle next. If you can be of help, then they’d want to work with you more. So it’s not as much selling as trying to pitch a product and see who buys it.

Do you think AI is the main reason for this increase in demand for FDEs?

I think it’s a big reason, yeah. Building became faster and cheaper, and enterprises don’t want to have to change themselves in order to buy a new product. It used to be okay that you get some SaaS product and then you need to adapt to be able to work with it. Today you don’t have to. We can build a solution that works exactly for you, in the way that you run your business.

That’s on one side. On the other side, we’re now able to see all the different ways that people want to use our product. We send out these very capable FDEs that can very quickly adapt and build on top of our existing knowledge, and then they can go and contribute that knowledge back in the form of a PR or feedback. The loop has become much, much faster with AI, because the building part is shortened.

That acceleration loop actually provides a lot of value on both sides. And I think why we see last mile engineering and all of that coming up more and more is because it actually is very valuable, and it’s much clearer with AI.

Is it here to stay for a number of years?

I hope so. I believe it is. If anything, I think I’m seeing a shift, even just with candidates that come to the interviews. The value of writing code as an expertise is going down, and the value of being able to solve problems and build solutions on top is going up. So if anything, I see a shift toward forward deployed engineering.

Is there anything you would like to wrap up with?

Maybe one last thing. Like I said, when I jumped into forward deployed engineering, I suddenly found that it’s like my place, and things just started becoming so much more fun. So if you think it might be for you, take that leap, take that step, trust yourself, and hopefully I’ll see you in the future. And don’t forget to have fun.

Boaz Francis is Head of Forward Deployed Engineering at Wonderful. He’s hiring FDEs, deployment strategists and GTM roles across the UK, Europe, LatAm and APAC.
