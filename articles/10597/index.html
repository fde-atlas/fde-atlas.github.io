Bridging the Last Mile of AI Deployment! Shanghai Accelerates the Cultivation of New FDE Tech Talent

Original title: 打通AI落地最后1公里！上海加快培育FDE新型技术人才-品玩

Summary

This article uses the case of Shiyuan Technology, whose on-site team cut a production line changeover from hours to 55 seconds, to introduce the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role: engineers embedded at customer sites who adapt AI models to real business processes. It argues that 95% of enterprise AI deployments fail due to talent gaps, lack of trust, and non-standardized requirements, and describes how Shanghai is systematically cultivating FDE talent through open compute platforms, China's first FDE training program at Shanghai Innovation Institute, STEM education pilots, and policy support. Shanghai's AI talent pool is nearly 300,000, about one-third of the national total.

On a consumer electronics production line in Shanghai, changeover and machine retooling used to be a chronic headache: whenever the model changed, engineers would spend anywhere from two or three hours to most of a day fiddling with the equipment.

After Ru Binxin, co-founder of Shiyuan Technology, and his team took over, they spent their days by the production line collecting abnormal samples and their nights back in the lab iterating on models—this cycle went on for months, and they ultimately compressed that time to 55 seconds. The number of product models handled per changeover also expanded from 5 per day to nearly 300.

Ru Binxin, co-founder of Shiyuan Technology

What made this possible was a group of people known as FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers). They are a new type of technical role in the AI era—instead of sitting at headquarters writing code, they go directly into clients' factories, banks, and hospitals, embedding the capabilities of large models into real business processes on the front line. Facing explosive demand for this kind of talent, Shanghai is actively building the stage: from computing platforms to training systems, from university pipelines to policy support, a systematic FDE talent cultivation campaign is accelerating.

I. New Industry, New Demand, New Profession

"Forward Deployed" is a military term referring to special units deployed on the front line to carry out missions. FDE borrows this term to describe a type of technical talent that is both capable of fighting tough battles and going deep into the front line.

Here's an analogy: if a large model is a powerful off-road vehicle, then most enterprises' actual business scenarios are a potholed, weed-covered country dirt road—messy data formats, a hodgepodge of systems, and employees clinging to habits they've held for twenty years. No matter how good the vehicle is, it's useless if it can't get on the road. What an FDE does is first quickly lay down a "gravel road" on site to get the vehicle running; once it's running smoothly, headquarters, based on field feedback, turns that gravel road into a "highway" leading to more customers.

What makes such people rare is that they are true "bilinguals" between technology and business: they can understand model architectures as well as read PLC control protocols in a factory; they can write code, and they can also understand the jargon-filled requirements when meeting with clients, then translate between the two languages.

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) talent model

II. The Three Hurdles of AI's "Last Mile"

Globally, 95% of enterprise AI adoptions ultimately end in failure. Where does the problem lie? Not in the models being insufficiently strong, but in that "last mile."

First, the talent gap. The market has no shortage of pure coders or PPT-wielding consultants, but it desperately lacks "T-shaped talent"—people who truly understand models on the technical side and truly understand the industry on the business side. Traditional algorithm engineers often know little about production lines, supply chains, or compliance systems; traditional software engineers struggle to quickly cross the threshold of understanding models. This chasm is the deep-rooted reason why AI projects so frequently fail.

Second, the trust black box. When AI gives a recommendation, why should on-site managers believe it? An international logistics optimization project once hit such a deadlock: everyone at the client organization was skeptical of the AI algorithm, worried about who would be responsible if something went wrong. The FDE team's breakthrough was: for 100 consecutive days, holding stand-up meetings with on-site staff every morning without fail, verifying data parameters one by one, and screening out the most critical 1% from hundreds of metrics—winning trust with time and facts. Once trust was established, AI recommendations began to be genuinely executed, and the financial statements improved markedly.

Third, non-standardized requirements. Every factory has different production logic, every bank has different compliance requirements, and standardized SaaS software is like "selling shovels"—responsible only for the process, not for outcomes, regardless of whether you dig up gold or mud. The FDE model's logic is the reverse: help you dig up the gold first, then price by the gold, forcing itself to truly solve problems. This is why global hiring for FDE-related positions has grown 800% month over month, making it one of the fastest-growing job types in the industrialization of AI.

III. Real Industry Scenarios Are the Best Training Ground

Shanghai's judgment is this: in the second half of AI competition, what's at stake is not just the algorithm "arms race," but the "frontline war" of implementation. The core capabilities of an FDE cannot be cultivated solely in classrooms and labs; they must be tempered under real industrial pressure. Shanghai's advantage lies precisely in having industrial scenarios that are complex enough, high-value enough, and diverse enough.

In manufacturing, AI is entering core processes such as changeover scheduling, quality-inspection vision, and predictive equipment maintenance, where FDEs need to understand PLC control protocols as well as deploy lightweight edge inference models; in business management, FDEs must embed the semantic understanding capabilities of large models into high-precision business processes such as compliance review and risk-control decision-making; in healthcare, FDEs need to find the engineering landing point between model capabilities and regulatory requirements. Every industry places unique cross-domain demands on FDEs.

To this end, Shanghai is pushing forward on three dimensions in coordination.

Opening up a "training ground" of computing power and tools. Relying on industrial cluster platforms such as Mosu Space and Model Community, computing resources are being opened up to small and medium startup teams. Standardized MLOps processes and a unified model evals system allow FDEs to grow quickly along a clear technical path, without having to "reinvent the wheel" from scratch on every project. For a young team just starting out, this means a substantial lowering of the entry barrier.

Launching the nation's first dedicated FDE training program. Shanghai Innovation Institute has officially launched the nation's first Forward Deployed Engineer training program, with an initial goal of cultivating 200 industry "wall-breakers." The curriculum takes the exploratory journeys of benchmark companies such as Palantir as its starting point, teaching the forward deployed engineering paradigm through real cases, and emphasizes sending technical talent directly to the business front line to define products within industrial scenarios.

Shanghai's first FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) training program

Starting early with the educational foundation. All 16 districts of Shanghai have fully advanced STEM education pilots, with 227 schools applying to participate and more than 9,300 project-based learning cases formed; multiple STEM universities have co-built practical courses with leading AI companies, allowing students to participate in the design of real engineering pipelines while still in school. FDE capability cultivation is being moved earlier, into the student years.

At the policy level, Shanghai's emphasis on FDEs is moving from market-driven spontaneity to systematic deployment. At the 2025 World AI Conference, the Shanghai Municipal Commission of Economy and Informatization revealed: the city's AI talent pool has reached nearly 300,000 people, about one-third of the national total. That is the confidence—and the foundation for continued escalation ahead.

Morgan Stanley once faced such a hurdle: when the ChatGPT API was first released, the bank wanted to introduce AI-assisted asset advisors to process massive volumes of documents, but the advisors generally distrusted this "black box"—afraid to use it, unwilling to use it. The FDE's intervention was not just writing a RAG retrieval-augmented solution; more importantly, it meant staying on the business front line, repeatedly communicating with, demonstrating to, and adjusting for the advisors, translating "what AI can do" into "which parts of your daily work can be handed to it." Once trust was established, the tool truly began to flow.

This detail may be the deepest reason the FDE profession exists: between technology and business, there is never just one API call missing—there is also a person missing, one willing to stay stationed between the two for the long term, translating repeatedly and patiently smoothing things out.

The growth path for FDEs is becoming ever clearer in Shanghai. Open computing platforms, standardized toolchains, abundant industrial scenarios, and increasingly tight collaboration between universities and enterprises are turning the emergence of FDE talent from "occasional" into "systematic supply." This group of people who can bring technology from the lab into the industrial field, and then loop back from the field to optimize the technology, may be Shanghai's AI industry's most promising long-term competitive advantage.
