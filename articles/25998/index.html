How to Cut Implementation Admin Work So FDEs Can Focus on Delivery

Quivly · 2026-10-28 · 6 min read

Summary

This Quivly guide argues that FDEs lose much of their week to admin work around implementation: status reports, chasing customer inputs, keeping plans current, capturing blockers, and translating technical issues. It breaks this work into five categories, describes what automation and AI can handle in each, and stresses keeping humans in the loop for high-stakes sends and plan changes. It closes with simple metrics—admin hours, outstanding inputs, kickoff-to-value time—to measure impact.

Key points

  • Audit one to two weeks of admin work across rollouts before automating anything.
  • Make the project itself the source of status reports; AI drafts narratives for review.
  • Use structured forms, a visible outstanding-request list, and scheduled reminders to chase inputs.
  • Plans need dependency-aware dates, automatic status rollups, and readiness gates tied to technical checkpoints.
  • AI can propose tasks and risks from conversations, but a person must confirm them.

Ask a forward deployed engineer (FDE) or implementation lead where their week went, and the answer is rarely "configuring the product." A large share of implementation time goes to work around the work: writing status updates, chasing customers for inputs, keeping the project plan accurate, turning chat threads into tasks and translating technical issues for CS and the customer.

None of that work is optional. Customers need updates, plans need to be current and blockers need owners. But most of it is repetitive, rules-based and dependent on information that already exists somewhere in your systems. That makes it a good candidate for automation and AI, and removing it is one of the most direct ways to shorten time to value without adding headcount.

This guide breaks implementation admin into five categories, shows what can be automated in each, explains where a person should stay in the loop and offers a simple way to measure the impact. For the broader onboarding picture, including nudges and stall detection after launch, see our guide to AI-driven onboarding.

Audit Where Implementation Admin Time Goes

Before automating anything, spend one or two weeks logging admin work across a few active rollouts. You are not looking for precise numbers. You want a rough picture of which tasks recur on every project, which ones depend on information that already lives in a system and which ones genuinely need judgment.

Most teams find the same five categories:

  • Status reporting for the customer, CS and internal leadership.
  • Chasing customer inputs such as SSO metadata, admin screenshots, data files and sign-offs.
  • Plan upkeep when dates slip, scope changes or dependencies move.
  • Capturing blockers that surface in calls, email and chat threads.
  • Technical issue communication between FDEs, engineering, CS and the customer.

Rank each category by how much time it takes and how predictable it is. Start automating where both are high.

Replace Manual Status Reports With Live Project State

Status reports are usually a summary of information that already exists: which tasks are done, which are overdue, which milestones are at risk and what the team is waiting on. The manual part is gathering it and writing it up.

The fix is to make the project itself the source of the report. When tasks, milestones, owners and due dates live in one place and stay current, a status update becomes a view rather than a document. AI can then draft the narrative layer, a short summary of progress, risks and next steps, for a person to review before it goes to the customer.

Quivly's Projects workspace is built around this idea. Health, blockers and planned versus actual effort stay in one place, and the same project context is available in Slack and AI assistants, so people can ask for the current state instead of assembling it by hand.

Automate Customer Input Collection and Follow-Up

Few things stall an enterprise rollout like waiting on the customer. The FDE asks for SSO metadata on a call, follows up by email, then again in a shared channel, and loses track of which requests are still open across several accounts.

Automation helps in three ways:

  • Structured requests. Use forms tied to project tasks so the customer knows exactly what is needed, in what format and by when.
  • One visible list of what is outstanding. Every open request should show its owner on both sides and how long it has been waiting.
  • Scheduled, polite reminders. Follow-ups should go out on a cadence through the channels the customer actually uses, without the FDE having to remember each one.

In Quivly, templates set the milestones, owners and dates for a project, and forms collect the files, screenshots and answers the team is waiting on. Requests go out through Slack, email and a customer portal, and replies stay on the project. If your team struggles with what to ask for and when, our guide to technical onboarding sign-offs covers the inputs most rollouts need.

Keep the Plan Current Without Manual Upkeep

A project plan that is out of date is worse than no plan, because people trust it. Yet keeping it current is tedious: one slipped integration task can push back several dependent milestones, the customer-facing timeline and the internal forecast.

Look for three behaviors in whatever system holds your plan:

  • Dependency-aware dates. When a task slips, dependent milestones should move with it automatically.
  • Automatic status rollups. Project health should reflect the state of its tasks and milestones, not a manually set color.
  • Readiness gates. Cutover should depend on technical checkpoints, such as SSO and provisioning or a migration dry run, so the plan cannot claim readiness that does not exist.

Quivly's FDE workspace supports milestone templates with dependencies and automatic status rollups, customer readiness gates tied to technical checkpoints and a shared timeline for CS and engineering. Slipped dates roll forward to dependent milestones. For more on structuring the plan itself, see our guide to go-live readiness.

Turn Conversations Into Tracked Tasks

Blockers rarely arrive as tickets. They show up as a line in a call, a reply in an email thread or a message in a shared channel: "SSO is blocked on their admin. Can someone own the follow-up?" If nobody converts that into a task with an owner, it gets lost.

This is where AI is genuinely useful. An agent can watch project channels and calls, recognize when something looks like a task, risk or blocker and propose it with the evidence attached. The key design choice is that a person confirms it. In Quivly, agents turn the conversation into a proposed task, risk or blocker with the evidence attached, and someone still hits apply. That keeps the plan accurate without letting automation create noise.

Route Technical Issues to the Right People Automatically

When an integration fails or data stops syncing, the FDE needs technical detail, the CSM needs a plain-language summary and the customer may need a message. Doing that translation by hand, often on a coordination call, is slow and easy to get wrong.

A better pattern is to generate each view from the same incident. Quivly sends the FDE an alert with error logs, affected endpoints and a recommended resolution, while the CSM gets a plain-language summary and a suggested customer message. Both alerts link to the same incident timeline, and issues that exceed the FDE's scope are routed to engineering. Quivly can also compare error patterns across accounts to flag whether an issue looks isolated to one customer or affects a cohort. For a deeper look at early warning signs, see our guide to implementation risk signals.

Keep People in Charge and Measure the Impact

Automation should remove busywork, not judgment. A few guardrails help:

  • Review before external sends. Draft customer updates and reminders automatically, but have a person approve anything high stakes, such as messages to executives or about a slipped go-live.
  • Confirm before changing the plan. Let AI propose tasks and risks, and let the owner accept them.
  • Keep an audit trail. Every automated action should show what triggered it and what it was based on.

To measure impact, compare a few rollouts before and after against simple indicators: hours of admin logged per project, number of status meetings, how long customer inputs stay outstanding, how often the plan disagrees with reality and the elapsed time from kickoff to first value. Our guide to measuring time to value explains how to set that baseline. The goal is not zero admin. It is an implementation team that spends most of its time on configuration, integration and customer outcomes.
