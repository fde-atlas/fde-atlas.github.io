The hottest new job in tech: FDE?

Original title: 科技圈最火新职业FDE？

小宇宙 · 2026-08-17 · 7 min read

Summary

This podcast episode explains the rise of the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), a role created by Palantir to solve problems on-site with customer data, since sensitive data cannot leave client premises. Using a hammer-versus-bookshelf analogy, it distinguishes FDEs from product and sales engineers, and argues the role requires three multiplicative skills: coding, communication, and domain knowledge. In the US, Palantir FDEs earn RMB 1.7–4.5 million annually with hiring up 800% in a year. In China, however, average LLM contract values (~RMB 3.91 million, skewed by a few large deals) and lowest-price bidding make this model hard to sustain. The episode outlines three adaptation paths: focusing on large state-owned projects, having one FDE serve several smaller clients, and productizing accumulated deployment experience.

Key points

  • FDEs work on-site because sensitive client data cannot be moved.
  • The role demands coding, communication, and industry knowledge combined.
  • US FDE pay reaches RMB 1.7–4.5 million; hiring grew 800% yearly.
  • China's ~RMB 3.91 million average contract size can't cover FDE costs.
  • Three paths: big clients, one-to-many consulting, productized solutions.

Today let's talk about a very hot job in the tech industry called FDE, the Forward Deployed Engineer. This role mainly exists to solve the problems that arise when advanced technologies like AI are deployed at customer sites. However, the development of this profession in China has encountered many challenges due to differences in the market environment.

Yes, this topic is getting a lot of attention right now, so let's get straight into it. Alright.

Let's first talk about how the FDE role came into being and what its purpose is. Why is it that companies spend big money on AI systems, only to have them end up sitting on the shelf?

The reason behind this is actually quite funny. A company spends millions on an AI system, signs the contract, gets it deployed, and passes acceptance. But afterwards, nobody knows how to use it and nobody maintains it. Exactly. Then three months later, when the boss asks about it, IT can only say it's on the server, and the boss is speechless while IT feels awkward.

That sounds really awkward. Is this kind of awkwardness a common phenomenon in the industry?

It is indeed very common. This situation happens every day around the world. It's not that the product is bad; it's that the product needs to actually be put to use. In that process, no one does the connecting work, which is why the FDE role was created, specifically to fill this gap.

What Is an FDE?

Now I understand what an FDE actually does. How is it different from a traditional engineer?

FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer. Essentially, it's an engineer who goes to the customer's site and, within the customer's environment, uses the customer's data to directly solve real problems for them. They're not writing code back at the office.

So they're more like a technical soldier sent to the front line.

Exactly. And this role was originally created by a company called Palantir, which specifically serves the US military, government, and intelligence agencies. Their data was too sensitive to be brought back to the company, so they had no choice but to send engineers to the customer's site to solve problems on the spot. Later, people discovered this approach was extremely effective, and it gradually caught on.

An Example to Instantly Understand FDE

With that said, what is the fundamental difference between an FDE, an ordinary engineer, and a Sales Engineer?

A vivid analogy can explain it: the product engineer's job is to build, back at the company, a super hammer that can theoretically nail all nails, and then they consider their job done. The Sales Engineer takes that hammer and does all kinds of demos for customers, gets the contract signed, and their job is done too.

So is the FDE the person who only truly steps in to solve the hard problems after the contract is signed?

Exactly right. As soon as the contract is signed, the FDE goes over to the customer, takes a look, and realizes the customer doesn't want a hammer—they want a bookshelf.

The wall is concrete, and the neighbors won't allow random drilling. So the FDE helps assemble the bookshelf and makes sure it won't fall over, so the customer ends up satisfied. That's where the FDE's value truly begins.

Got it. When an FDE helps a customer implement an AI project, how exactly do they turn the customer's vague ideas into a system that can actually be used, step by step?

What Does an FDE Actually Do?

Usually, the information an FDE receives at the start is just the customer saying, "We want to use AI to improve efficiency." At that point, the FDE will ask very detailed questions to figure out what the customer's business actually is, what data they use, what systems they currently run, and where they're stuck.

So the first step is translating vague requirements into a technical plan that can actually be executed.

Then the second step is that the FDE quickly builds a prototype to test whether the direction is right. Once there's a prototype, they polish it into a formal system, including connecting databases and running compliance tests. Finally, it's truly deployed to the production environment to ensure stable operation. In the end, the FDE also brings the problems customers encounter in actual use back to the company to help the product team continuously improve the product. That's right, so the FDE is really the eyes, ears, and hands that the company embeds at the customer's side.

With that said, to handle so many stages, what abilities does an FDE actually need?

What Kind of Person Does This Role Require?

It's a bit brutal to say, but an FDE must possess three abilities at the same time, and these three abilities are multiplicative. If any one of them is close to zero, the overall effectiveness is zero.

That sounds like a really high bar. So what are these three abilities specifically?

The first is genuinely knowing how to write code—you have to be able to independently set up systems on the customer's servers, write APIs, process data, and go live. And if something breaks in production, you have to go troubleshoot and fix it yourself. The second is genuinely knowing how to communicate—you have to be able to translate what the CEO means by "improving efficiency" into requirements the technical team can understand, and also translate technical plans into business value the boss can understand. And you have to fight on the customer's turf, away from home, without much backing.

The third is genuinely understanding the industry. If you go to a hospital, you need to understand medical compliance; if you go to a bank, you need to know financial business logic; if you go to a factory, you need to understand the production line. Right—if you don't understand these, the systems you build will never even get through the customer's door.

Now I understand the FDE role. What is the salary level roughly like? Why is that the case?

Salaries: Enough to Leave You Speechless

In the US, at companies like Palantir, an FDE's annual salary plus equity plus bonus falls between 1.7 and 4.5 million RMB. And top FDEs can exceed 4.5 million. My goodness. Job postings for this role surged 800% within a year. Right, that's because people who simultaneously meet all three conditions—strong technical skills, strong communication, and industry knowledge—are extremely rare. But market demand suddenly exploded, causing a severe talent shortage, so prices naturally got pushed very high. Understood.

What is the core difference between the FDE business model in the US and the FDE business model in China?

US vs China: Same Role, Two Different Math Problems

In the US, customer contract amounts are very large. A single Palantir project for the military is worth several million dollars. Yes, and enterprise contracts from OpenAI and Salesforce are of the same magnitude. So they can fully afford to pay FDEs very high salaries. Customers get excellent service, products actually get used, so customers are more willing to renew and place even bigger orders, and the whole thing forms a positive cycle.

So their high deal size is the key that keeps this flywheel spinning.

China is a completely different math problem. In 2025, China had over 7,500 large model bidding wins, with a total value of nearly 30 billion RMB. But if you do the math, each project averages 3.91 million. However, that average is pulled up by a few super-large deals. Exactly. Most projects are actually between a few hundred thousand and one to two million. Now consider that an FDE's total annual cost easily exceeds one million. Many projects' total value isn't even enough to cover the cost of one FDE, not to mention that much of China's procurement awards contracts to the lowest bidder. So the FDE's high-end service simply can't demonstrate its value.

Given that the market environment in China is like this, what options do Chinese companies have to make FDEs work?

So What Should China Do?

Right now, people are mainly taking three paths. The first path is to only take big projects from top central state-owned enterprises or government affairs. These projects have money, long cycles, and are willing to accept deep customization, which can support the high cost of FDEs. The barrier is high, but the deal size works.

So the first path is essentially concentrating resources on the big customers best able to absorb the value of FDEs.

The second path is having one FDE serve 3 to 5 small and medium customers simultaneously, visiting them regularly like a doctor. This approach sacrifices depth of service but expands the number of companies served, essentially turning the FDE into a senior technical consultant.

The third path is to package the industry experience, deployment templates, and best practices accumulated by FDEs into a solution package. What customers are actually buying is the FDE's capability, not their time. The difficulty with this approach is that it requires a lot of effort upfront to accumulate and productize—just like Palantir, which only achieved scale after going through such a transformation.

Okay. Finally, let's talk about the specific differences between China and the US when it comes to the talent gap in AI implementation.

Both sides face an awkward situation: product teams don't understand the customer's real environment, and customer teams don't understand what AI can actually do. So there's a communication gap in the middle. In the US, this gap can be filled by hiring an FDE with an annual salary of several million dollars. But in China, how much this gap is worth and how to systematically solve it—everyone is still in the exploratory stage. So whoever figures this out first can seize the initiative.

Today we talked a lot about the birth of the FDE profession, its unique value, and the challenges and opportunities it faces in the different market environments of China and the US.

That's right. That's all for this episode. Thanks for listening, and see you next time. Bye-bye.
