What Is an FDE?

learn.netgramlabs.com · 3 min read

Summary

This beginner tutorial defines Forward Deployed Engineering and the FDE role. The term borrows from military language: engineers are placed at the customer's "front line" — their data, systems, and constraints — to adapt a product into a working solution in context. The author argues most B2B and AI products fail not from weak technology but from the gap between a generic product and a specific customer's workflow, which FDEs close. Key points

  • FDEs are hybrid engineers who write production code and understand customer business context.
  • The role exists to close the gap between generic products and specific customer workflows.
  • The core loop: understand the problem, build in the customer's environment, feed learnings back to product.
  • The FDE title is most common at companies selling complex or AI-driven products.

Forward Deployed Engineering (FDE) is one of the fastest-growing roles in modern tech, sitting at the intersection of software engineering, customer work, and product. This tutorial builds the foundation: what the field is, the person who does it, and what a typical day looks like.

  • What Is a Forward Deployed Engineer?
  • What Does a Forward Deployed Engineer Do?

Forward Deployed Engineering is a way of building software where engineers work directly with customers, often on-site or embedded in the customer's team, to turn a product into a solution that actually fits their problem. Instead of shipping code and walking away, the FDE stays close to the use case.

The term comes from "forward deployed" — troops or equipment moved close to the front line. In tech, the "front line" is the customer's real environment: their data, their systems, their messy constraints. FDEs bring engineering capability to that edge so the product gets adapted fast, in context, by someone who can write code.

Why it matters: Most B2B and AI products fail not because the core tech is weak, but because the gap between a generic product and a specific customer's workflow is huge. FDEs close that gap.

A Forward Deployed Engineer is a software engineer who works at the boundary between a company's product and its customers. They are technical enough to write production code, but they also spend real time understanding the customer's business, data, and goals.

Think of them as the engineer who "deploys forward" — into the customer's problem space — rather than staying behind the product line. They are not salespeople, and they are not pure backend engineers; they are a hybrid who can demo, build, debug, and translate.

A simple way to picture it:

TraitFDE
Primary locationAt the customer's problem, not just the codebase
Core skillWriting code and understanding the business
OutputWorking integrations, prototypes, tailored solutions
Feedback loopDirectly shaped by live customer needs

Note: The title "FDE" is most common at companies selling complex or AI-driven products (Palantir, OpenAI, Databricks, and many startups). At other companies the same work might be called "customer engineer," "implementation engineer," or "solutions engineer."

An FDE's work is a mix of building and listening. On any given week they might write a custom integration, clean a customer's dataset, run a proof-of-concept, train the customer's team, and feed product gaps back to engineering.

Here is a representative week, not a strict schedule:

  • Monday: Join a customer call, learn they need to connect the product to an internal PostgreSQL database with unusual schema.
  • Tuesday: Write a small connector script and a notebook that loads their sample data.
  • Wednesday: Demo the working prototype to the customer's technical lead; they ask for a change in how results are filtered.
  • Thursday: Push the change, fix two edge-case bugs in their data, document the setup.
  • Friday: Write a one-page note to the product team: "Customers consistently hit this limitation — we should fix it in the core product."

The pattern: go to the customer → understand the real problem → build something that works in their world → bring the learning back. That loop is the job.

Best Practice: Always write down what you learned from customers in a form the product team can reuse. The feedback you collect is often more valuable long-term than the one-off code you ship.
