How to Build a Data Platform in 5 Steps: The Design Sequence FDEs Use in Practice | FDE Research Institute

Summary

This FDE Soken article lays out a five-step method for building data platforms: requirements definition, architecture design, pipeline implementation, quality and governance operations, and in-house handover. It argues that a data platform only creates value when specific business decisions become faster and more accurate, so projects should start with a single use case rather than company-wide integration. It also describes how Forward Deployed Engineers work end-to-end from requirements to implementation and hand over to internal teams in three stages: delegation, collaboration, and autonomous operation.

Key points

  • Data platform building follows five steps, each with deliverables and exit conditions.
  • Start with one use case: stalled decisions, existing data, and measurable results.
  • Architecture uses four layers (raw, cleaned, business model, consumption) with clear ownership.
  • Quality checks at layer boundaries across six dimensions, with verified alert routing.
  • FDEs own the vertical loop; handover completes only when staff make changes independently.

How should you approach data platform construction? How many steps should the overall process have?

Data platform construction proceeds in five steps: requirements definition, architecture design, implementation, quality operations, and handover to in-house teams. You can still make progress by reordering the steps, but if you start with layer composition or product selection without first deciding "whose decisions, and which ones, should this make faster," you may end up with a working platform that goes unused while only maintenance costs remain.

A data platform is not an asset that generates value on its own. Value emerges only when concrete business decisions—such as inventory allocation, utilization tracking, or demand forecasting—become faster and more accurate on top of the platform than before. When an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) enters a site, the first thing they confirm is not the tech stack but "which decisions are currently stuck."

What does the overall picture of the 5 steps look like?

The overall flow is: use case definition → layer architecture design → pipeline implementation → quality and governance operations → handover to in-house teams. Each step has deliverables and exit criteria, and you obtain business-side agreement before moving to the next.

#StepPurposeKey DeliverablesExit CriteriaTypical Duration
1Requirements definition and use case selectionNarrow down to a single business decision to solveUse case definition document, list of required itemsBusiness side agrees that "we can make decisions with these items at this precision"2–4 weeks
2Architecture designDecide layer and responsibility boundariesLayer diagram, data flow, technology selection notesA single path from raw data to usage can be drawn2–4 weeks
3Pipeline implementationFlow target data through the layersIngestion and transformation processing, business models, UI or APITarget decisions can be executed with real data4–10 weeks
4Quality and governance operationsDetect degradation and deviationsQuality standards document, threshold monitoring, permission designA deliberately induced failure notification reaches the responsible person2–6 weeks
5In-house handoverReach a state where changes can be made internallyOperational procedures, change history, handover recordsAn internal staff member can complete one change independently4–8 weeks

Durations vary greatly depending on business scope and the number of data sources, so treat these as rough guidelines for deciding whether to start. In practice, we find that more projects spend more time on Step 1 agreement and upstream coordination in Step 4 than on Step 3 implementation.

Why start with a single use case rather than a company-wide rollout?

Narrowing the target to one use case makes the scope finite, so value validation and the next investment decision cycle quickly. Conversely, if you try to collect company-wide data first, the more departments involved, the fewer points of agreement, and the slower the start.

The difficulty of a company-wide-integration-first approach lies not in technology but in decision-making. Which department's master data is the source of truth, and which side should code value discrepancies lean toward—these issues cannot be settled without the benchmark of a concrete business decision. Conversely, if you have the premise "this decision requires these items," the source-of-truth master is determined naturally. Working through one use case and then expanding to the next ultimately brings you closer to company-wide integration as well.

How do you proceed with the initial requirements definition and use case selection?

Requirements definition is the work of narrowing down to a single business decision to solve and agreeing with the business side on the required items, precision, and freshness. If you start adding "nice-to-have data" at this stage, the scope balloons and you fail to deliver results within the deadline.

What often determines whether something is usable in the field is not data volume but freshness requirements. Decisions that suffice with daily updates and decisions that require same-day reflection differ in both required architecture and cost. If freshness is not quantified during requirements definition, you will end up redesigning after the design phase.

What criteria should you use to select a use case?

The selection criteria are three points: the decision is stuck, the data already exists, and results can be counted. Starting with a business area that meets all three lets you demonstrate value in a short period.

  • The decision is stuck: Choose a business area where staff make decisions by intuition or manual aggregation and are personally aware of the struggle. A platform built for an area where no one struggles will not be used.
  • The data already exists: If data that has not yet been recorded is required, the work starts with changing business operations, which is too heavy for a first project.
  • Results can be counted: Prioritize business areas that already have measurable metrics, such as work hours, rework counts, or lead times. Metrics that require starting new measurement will delay value validation.

What should be documented in requirements definition?

Document the item definitions used for decisions, the precision and freshness that constitute the pass line, and an explicit list of exclusions. In particular, a document stating "what we will not do this time" becomes the benchmark for sorting out later additional requests.

The key point in item definition is to write the meaning, not just the name. A column named "sales" can yield different numbers per department depending on whether it is at order time or invoice time, tax-inclusive or tax-exclusive, and whether cancellations are included. In FDE practice, this definition alignment is completed before design and fixed as the item names of the business model layer. Some products, such as Palantir's Foundry ontology (Palantir official documentation), provide mechanisms to explicitly model business concepts and relationships, but the same definition work is necessary even without such products.

How do you design a data platform architecture? How should layers be divided?

The basic principle of architecture is to divide it into four layers—raw, curated, business model, and consumption—and assign clear responsibility to each. The purpose of separating layers is not technical tidiness, but creating a state where you can pinpoint "where it broke."

The concept of layering itself is widely standardized: the medallion architecture called bronze/silver/gold, and classifications like ODS, DWH, and data marts, all share the same aim despite differing granularity. What matters more than naming is whether each layer has an owner and change rules assigned to it.

How many layers should the architecture have?

A four-layer architecture keeps raw data immutable and separates owners across the curated, business model, and consumption layers. Three layers can suffice, but omitting the business model layer causes definitions to diverge by department.

LayerRoleChange rulesPrimary owner
Raw data layerHolds acquired data as-isNo processing. Append only, never overwritePlatform side (IT department)
Curated layerAligns types, code values, and missing valuesTransformation logic managed in code with history retainedPlatform side (IT department)
Business model layerShapes data into business concepts such as customers, equipment, and ordersChanges to field definitions require business-side approvalJoint between business departments and platform side
Consumption layerProvides dashboards, APIs, and applicationsPermissions separated per user; additions require a requestBusiness departments

The design of never overwriting the raw data layer is unglamorous but pays off the most later. When a transformation logic error is found, you can rebuild if raw data remains; if only processed data exists, you start with negotiating re-acquisition.

How do you decide technology selection and scale assumptions?

For technology selection, it is safer to base the decision on whether the first use case can run within the deadline, rather than on future visions. Start by defining data volume and freshness requirements, then begin with the minimal configuration that satisfies them.

As decision criteria, most selections converge on these three points: (1) whether daily batch suffices or near-real-time is needed, (2) which cloud and BI tools are already under contract, and (3) whether it is a technology someone in-house can operate. Underestimating the third point means construction finishes but internal handover stumbles. A configuration the operations staff cannot read ends up locked into external dependency. While I cannot say for certain, I have the impression that projects leaning on technologies already present in-house take root better than those adopting the latest stack.

How do you ensure data quality and governance?

Quality and governance should not be bolted on afterward; embed checks and permissions per layer and define notification recipients. If you start measuring quality only after construction, rework becomes large because the source of defects lies on the business side.

A common misconception about quality assurance is that downstream cleansing can solve it. When the cause is insufficient input-screen constraints or person-dependent master management, the same defects recur the next month even if corrected after ingestion. Detailed improvement procedures are a separate topic, so here we limit the scope to what should run in parallel with platform construction.

What should quality measure, and where should it stop data?

Measure six dimensions—completeness, accuracy, consistency, timeliness, uniqueness, and validity—and stop data at layer boundaries. Placing checks before crossing layers prevents defective data from flowing into the consumption layer.

In implementation, provide at minimum the following three types of checks.

  • Volume and freshness monitoring: Notify when ingestion counts deviate significantly from the previous day, or when the last update time exceeds a threshold. This is the most cost-effective part of failure detection.
  • Format checks: Stop violations of types, lengths, code values, and ranges before loading into the curated layer. Letting them through silently corrupts downstream aggregations.
  • Business rule checks: Detect business-impossible combinations at the business model layer, such as an order date after the delivery date, or utilization above 100%.

Once detection is built, deliberately break data to confirm notifications reach the responsible person. Monitoring left with no notification recipient is, in practice, the same as not existing.

Who should own governance, and to what extent?

A division where business departments own the data and the IT department owns detection and platform maintenance tends to work realistically. Leaning entirely on the IT department prevents changing input rules, and only format checks multiply.

In permission design, define visibility by business decision unit, not by department. For fields containing personal information or trading terms, prepare separate masked columns at the business model layer and, as a rule, have the consumption layer reference those. If AI usage is anticipated, refer to a risk management framework such as NIST's AI Risk Management Framework (official NIST page) and document criteria for which data may be passed to models beforehand; this lightens later audits.

How does an FDE work in data platform construction, and how do they hand over to internal ownership?

An FDE goes into the field and consistently handles everything from requirements to pipeline implementation, then transfers operations to the company. The difference from a division-of-labor model of receiving requirements and building is that iterating—revising the design while observing the business—is the premise.

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role established by Palantir, where engineers personally handle everything from workflow design to implementation and testing at customer sites (Palantir official blog). Data platform construction is one of the areas where this way of working fits best, because the source of defective data lies in field input operations, and fixing it requires both negotiation with the business side and implementation.

What does an FDE handle?

An FDE handles a single vertical loop from business interviews through layer design, implementation, and setting up detection. The difference from a division-of-labor setup lies in the speed of reflecting discovered issues into the design on the spot.

StepTraditional division of laborFDE model
RequirementsBusiness side and consultants create a requirements documentThe implementer observes manual work on-site and determines required fields themselves
DesignAn architect creates a design documentThe minimal layer configuration needed for the decision at hand is decided on the spot
ImplementationA development vendor builds according to the design documentThe same person implements and revises definitions based on field reactions
QualityOperations handles issues after deliveryGoes back to the source input operations and proposes fixes
HandoverSubmits a procedure manual and finishesWorks alongside until internal staff can make changes independently

It does not mean one person does everything; it is closer to reality to understand it as a small team holding vertical, end-to-end responsibility. At VACAN Technologies as well, the basic model is that the same person handles everything from discovering issues through implementation and adoption on-site.

How should the handover to internal ownership be designed?

Handover proceeds in three stages: delegation, collaboration, and independent operation, and is considered complete when the internal team can make changes on their own. Submitting documentation is not a completion condition.

  • Delegation: The FDE handles implementation and operations, recording the rationale behind decisions. The key is to also document what options were not chosen.
  • Collaboration: The internal team takes the lead while the FDE shifts to a review role. At this stage, the internal team actually performs threshold changes and item additions.
  • Independent operation: The internal team reaches a state where they can complete changes, verification, and releases on their own. External parties are involved only at key points, such as consultations on specification changes.

If handover is not included in the initial contract and considered later, the handover work itself becomes an additional negotiation item. It is easier for both parties to explicitly state the handover steps at the estimation stage of the build, including a concrete completion condition such as "the internal team completes one change on their own." The value of a data platform is determined not at the time of construction, but by whether the internal team can continue making changes themselves.
