The 3 Jobs Hiding Behind the FDE Title

Reveneau · 2026-09-08 · 10 min read

Summary

Drawing on a November 2025 analysis of 1,000 job postings using Revealera data, the article argues that the forward deployed engineer title covers three distinct jobs: production engineers embedded with customers (60%), sales engineers with implementation duties (30%), and internal tools builders with no external customer (10%). They differ in coding time (70–90% versus 30–40% of the week), pay structure (equity versus commission), and accountability for production. The author proposes three screening questions about coding share, quotas, and end users, and urges vendors and hirers to specify which job they mean. Postings grew 1,165% year over year, which explains why adjacent roles get relabelled to attract candidates.

Key points

  • A 1,000-posting analysis splits FDE roles roughly 60/30/10 across three jobs.
  • Production roles report 70–90% coding time; sales-adjacent ones 30–40%.
  • 70% of postings mention equity, 8% commission, and none are quota-carrying.
  • Hirers should name the specific job in the first line of descriptions.
  • FDE postings grew 1,165% year over year, driving title inflation.

An analysis of 1,000 postings carrying the title, published by Henley Wing Chiu in November 2025 using Revealera data, split them into a production engineer embedded with customers (60 percent), a sales engineer with implementation duties (30 percent), and an internal tools builder who faces no customer at all (10 percent). The three differ in how much of the week is spent writing code, how pay is structured, and who is accountable for production. Anyone hiring for the title, taking the job, or buying the service needs to know which one is being offered.

Key takeaways

  • The title covers three distinct jobs, splitting roughly 60 percent production engineer, 30 percent sales engineer with implementation duties, and 10 percent internal tools builder.
  • Coding time is the fastest test: the production version reported 70 to 90 percent of time writing code, the version closer to sales 30 to 40 percent.
  • Compensation structure is the second test. Across all 1,000 postings, 70 percent mentioned equity and 8 percent mentioned commission or on-target earnings, and none were quota-carrying.
  • A vendor claiming a forward deployed engineering team can mean any of the three. Ask which, and ask what share of the week their engineers spend in your repository.

If you are hiring for this title, applying for it, or buying it from a vendor, the first question is which job is actually being described. The title does not tell you, and the difference is large enough to change the outcome.

The best public evidence on this is an analysis of 1,000 job postings carrying the title, published by Henley Wing Chiu on 18 November 2025 using data from Revealera. It found the postings sorted into three groups.

Job one: the builder, 60 percent of postings

This is the role the guides describe. A production engineer embedded with a customer, writing code that runs in that customer's systems, accountable for the deployment.

In this group, 70 to 90 percent of the week goes on coding. The advertised salary range in the analysis was $140,000 to $250,000. Pay is structured like engineering pay: base plus equity.

This is the job that produces the outcomes people associate with the model, and it is the one where the three tests from our definition page all pass.

Job two: the sales engineer with a better title, 30 percent of postings

This role supports the sale and helps with implementation afterwards. Demos, proofs of concept, technical objection handling, then assistance getting the customer live.

Coding drops to 30 to 40 percent of the week. The advertised range in the analysis was $120,000 to $200,000 plus commission, and the pay structure is the clear sign: variable compensation tied to bookings.

This is a legitimate and valuable job. It is presales, and calling it forward deployed engineering creates two problems. Candidates expecting to build production systems arrive and find they are demoing. Buyers expecting an engineer who owns their deployment get someone whose incentive ends at signature.

Job three: the internal tools builder, 10 percent of postings

This one surprised us. In 10 percent of postings the role faced no external customer at all: building internal tooling for go-to-market or revenue operations teams, with the company's own staff as the users.

The advertised range was $100,000 to $180,000. The work can be good work. It is not forward deployment in any sense the term originally carried, because the whole point of the model is being deployed forward, into somebody else's environment.

Three tests that sort them in one conversation

Ask for the coding percentage. "What share of a typical week does this person spend writing code that gets merged?" A number under 50 means job two or three, whatever the title says.

Ask about the quota. Across all 1,000 postings, 8 percent mentioned commission or on-target earnings and none were quota-carrying, while 70 percent mentioned equity. If a role carries a sales target, the incentive is the deal rather than the deployment. That is not disqualifying and it is worth knowing before you accept or buy.

Ask who the user is. If the answer is "our sales team" or "our revenue operations group", it is job three.

What this means if you are hiring

Say which job you are hiring for, in the first line of the description. Vagueness here is expensive twice: it brings in more of the wrong candidates, and the ones you hire leave when they discover the mismatch.

The experience mix in the data is worth planning around. Of the 1,000 postings, 12 percent targeted zero to two years of experience, 60 percent three to five years, 20 percent six to eight, and 8 percent nine or more. The role is being staffed mid-level, which is consistent with First Round Review's finding that ten or more years at a large tech company can be a warning sign for this work, because the role needs someone who operates without the support structure a big company provides.

Our job description page has a template that names the job in line one.

What this means if you are buying

When a vendor says they will give you forward deployed engineers, the claim is compatible with all three jobs. Three questions answer it.

What share of the week will these engineers spend in our repository? Who is accountable, by name, if this does not reach production? Are these engineers compensated on our deployment going live, or on the contract being signed?

A vendor selling job one will answer all three without hesitating. A vendor selling job two will reframe the third question. Our questions to ask a vendor page has the rest of the list, and how to choose a partner covers the evaluation.

Why the title spread this fast

Job titles spread to more roles when demand is higher than supply, and this demand grew faster than almost any role in the industry: the same analysis measured postings with the title up 1,165 percent year over year, comparing January to October 2025 against the same months in 2024, with October 2025 the highest volume on record.

When a title becomes the one candidates want, roles get relabelled to attract them. That is the whole mechanism, and it is why the definitional discipline on this page matters more than it would for an established job.

Our position

We think the industry should keep the term for job one and stop using it for the other two. Forward deployment means being deployed into someone else's environment with accountability for what happens there. A presales engineer and an internal tools engineer are both doing real work, and neither is that.

For what it is worth on our own account: our engagements are job one. Engineers in your repository, production code, a named production date, and a named handover date. That is the service, and it is the only version of this we sell.

Check before you decide

  • Ask what share of a typical week is spent writing code that gets merged, and treat under 50 percent as a different job
  • Ask whether the role or the vendor's engineers carry a sales quota or commission tied to bookings
  • Ask who the end user is, by team name, and check it is not the vendor's own go-to-market staff

How can I tell whether an FDE role is really an engineering job?

Ask what share of a typical week is spent writing code that gets merged. Genuinely production-focused roles reported 70 to 90 percent, while the version closer to sales reported 30 to 40 percent. Then ask whether the role carries a quota or commission, because incentive structure reveals what the role is measured on.

Do forward deployed engineers get commission?

Usually not. Across 1,000 postings, 70 percent mentioned equity, 8 percent mentioned commission or on-target earnings, and none were quota-carrying. A forward deployed role with a quota attached is closer to sales engineering, which is a different job with a different incentive.

What is a forward deployed engineer?

A forward deployed engineer is a software engineer placed inside a customer's organization to build and release production software there, against that customer's real data and real systems, and who stays accountable for the deployment until it is running. The title is often written FDE, and the variant FDSE means forward deployed software engineer. Palantir popularised the role and AWS, OpenAI, Anthropic and Google now use the title. Three tests separate it from the roles that resemble it: production code, the customer's environment, and ownership of the outcome.

What does a forward deployed engineer do?

A forward deployed engineer spends most of the week writing production code inside the customer's systems, and the rest of it finding out what the code should do by sitting with the people who will use it. A useful guideline from teams that run the model well is about 20 percent of the time facing the customer and 80 percent building. When that ratio inverts, the engineer has become an account manager and the deployment has stopped moving. Across 1,000 job postings, the three duties named most often were working directly with customers, building and deploying AI systems, and integrating systems and APIs.

Why forward deployed engineering is growing

Forward deployed engineering grew because enterprise AI has an integration problem that nobody can solve from outside the customer's building. Three things happened close together: MIT NANDA reported in July 2025 that 95 percent of generative AI pilots produced no measurable profit-and-loss impact, a16z argued publicly in June 2025 that AI companies should hire teams focused on implementation, and in June 2026 AWS committed $1 billion to a forward deployed engineering organization. Job postings carrying the title grew 1,165 percent year over year. Each of those numbers deserves its caveat, and this page gives them.

Forward deployed engineering explained for executives

Forward deployed engineering means putting engineers inside your organization, or your customer's, to build and run a system rather than to advise on one. It costs more per engineer than any other delivery model and it is the only one that reliably gets an AI pilot to a production system. Three questions decide whether you should buy it: is the contract value large enough to pay for an engineer, do the environments differ from each other, and will anyone change the plan based on what the engineers find. Two noes means buy something else.
