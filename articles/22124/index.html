Summary

This Daily Economic News feature examines the explosive rise of the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role in China. Maimai reports FDE job postings grew 1522.73% in the first seven months of the year, topping AI-related hiring, while Liepin data shows an average salary of 408,000 yuan, with Tencent, ByteDance and Meituan offering around one million yuan. Interviews with practitioners reveal a messier reality: vague client requirements, interviews yielding mostly useless information, organizational resistance blocking deployments, and small teams failing to collect final payments on 70-80% of contracts. Interviewees define FDEs as people who translate AI capabilities into business results, predict companies will grow in-house FDE capabilities, and suggest external FDE firms may eventually monetize through equity gains alongside venture capital rather than service fees.

Key points

  • FDE postings on Maimai grew 1522.73% year-over-year, leading AI hiring.
  • Average FDE salary is 408,000 yuan; big tech offers near one million.
  • Practitioners describe work as generalist outsourcing: on-site research, prototyping, iteration.
  • Organizational resistance and misaligned expectations leave 70-80% of final payments uncollected.
  • Interviewees expect in-house FDE capabilities and equity-based business models ahead.

Full text

2026-11-18 17:19:41

NBD Reporter: Zheng Xinwei | NBD Editor: Wei Guanhong

This summer, Wei Quyun attended an industry conference on FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers). Over those three hours, the entire audience kept circling back to one core question: What exactly is an FDE? Even when the conference ended, there was still no answer.

Wei Quyun at the FDE industry conference. Image source: interviewee

But that hasn't stopped it from becoming the most sought-after role in the AI (artificial intelligence) circle.

The spark originated more than 20 years ago with Palantir's on-site delivery model. This May, the world's top AI companies Anthropic and OpenAI simultaneously set up companies betting on enterprise AI deployment, and the flame of the FDE has "spread" from Silicon Valley all the way to China.

A report released by the job-hunting platform Maimai shows that in the first seven months of this year, FDE job postings on the platform grew 1522.73% year over year, topping the list of expanded AI technology roles. According to statistics from Liepin Big Data Research Institute, the average annual salary for FDE-related positions is 408,000 yuan, with 28.25% offering more than 500,000 yuan. Tech giants like Tencent, ByteDance, and Meituan offer annual salaries as high as around one million yuan.

Behind the high salaries and expansion lies the complicated reality of frontline implementation. Recently, National Business Daily reporters (hereinafter referred to as NBD reporters) spoke with multiple FDE practitioners, glimpsing the true picture beneath the glamour of the boom: they handle miscellaneous technical tasks at companies, talk with CEOs (chief executive officers) to uncover needs, dive into frontline stores shaking milk tea to find implementation scenarios, and some small teams struggle to collect 70% to 80% of final payments after taking on orders...

Wei Quyun once served an AI startup as an FDE. His work was highly varied: helping the company build marketing automation workflows, iterating the product's AI capabilities, and connecting AI-powered store monitoring systems—anything touching software or hardware. "In a not-so-mature company, an FDE is essentially an all-around technical outsourcer."

This is not an isolated case. Multiple interviewees admitted that they are essentially "technical outsourcers." Zhou Ziyao, an FDE entrepreneur and founder of Yaohan Digital Technology, told NBD reporters that a large number of FDE teams have emerged in the market this year, and people in the industry often self-deprecatingly call themselves "makeshift crews"—everyone is crossing the river by feeling the stones.

With the rise of the FDE role, questions like "What exactly does an FDE do?" and "How does it differ from product managers, pre-sales solutions engineers, after-sales customer engineers, and other roles?" have not reached a consensus. In the view of the multiple practitioners interviewed by NBD reporters, FDE work does indeed overlap with many traditional roles. The greatest common denominator they offer is: someone who can translate AI capabilities into business results for enterprises and connect AI into real workflows.

Gan Yifan, co-founder of Rolling AI, doesn't even see the FDE as a specific job title, but rather as a "capability package." "Just like electrical engineers in the electrical age, whose core was replacing human physical labor with electricity-driven machines, the core of the FDE is replacing repetitive human intellectual labor with AI," he explained to NBD reporters.

Gan Yifan broke down this "capability package" using Palantir's Echo-Delta pairing system (mainly referring to a pair of collaborative roles in the FDE model): Echo stays close to the business, finding opportunities on the frontline where AI can amplify value and making it run in both new and old workflows; Delta handles technical implementation, using various engineering methods to turn opportunities into runnable systems. In addition, he proposed that Chinese enterprises also need a third capability role—the "knowledge governor"—because Chinese enterprises generally lack the organization and consolidation of explicit knowledge, processes, and expert experience.

Rolling AI's team is very flat; Gan Yifan also goes on-site and writes code himself. Everyone on the team is an "all-around super individual," yet with different emphases on the above capabilities. Several people form groups to serve enterprises, with a middle-platform team providing underlying technical support behind them.

"There are two thresholds for companies hiring FDEs," Gan Yifan told NBD reporters. First, being a small expert in a certain field who understands how the industry operates; second, having experienced transformation projects in the industry and knowing what happens when new productivity enters an enterprise. Compared to technical experts, business-savvy experts are more critical: "The business expert is the '1,' and technology is the '0's that follow—without the '1,' nothing can be added."

Familiarity with industry business is indeed a widely valued quality for FDEs. Wei Lei, an FDE entrepreneur and founder of OpenFDE, said in an interview with NBD reporters that to do FDE work long-term, one must focus on a specific vertical—in the end, it comes down to "understanding the customer." And "reading the customer" is often the first challenge FDEs face after entering a company.

Although many business leaders are anxious about AI applications, they don't clearly understand the actual effects and pathways of AI implementation. Requirements are often vague, so consulting and research become the first step after entering.

Gan Yifan gave an example: the first thing his team does upon entering a company is to reach consensus with the client from a management consulting perspective on issues like the boundaries of AI capabilities, application expectations, the company's financial situation, and business goals. After aligning understanding, they go to the frontline: for fast-moving consumer goods, they follow the channels; for a milk tea chain, they get health certificates and go to stores to shake milk tea. They spend two to three weeks on the business frontline formulating hypotheses about how AI can optimize systems and improve processes, then two to three weeks building prototypes and testing and evaluating them. After confirming feasibility, they iterate quickly, promote during iteration, and expand from a small scope to the entire company within two months.

This process—from consulting and research to prototype design, implementation and trial-and-error, and iterative maintenance—is the FDE work path described by most interviewees, but this long chain is not easy from the very beginning.

Wei Quyun's on-site workplace. Image source: interviewee

After the National Day holiday, Wei Quyun and five colleagues entered a subsidiary of a state-owned central enterprise for an FDE project. The team spent two weeks interviewing employees from 12 departments and compiled 800 to 900 issues. "You could feel that employees were more venting emotions than making constructive suggestions," Wei Quyun said. The interviews were mentally exhausting because 80% to 90% of the information was useless and couldn't help the team analyze the company's pain points or find the next direction.

"The core of FDE on-site work is becoming an expert who understands the customer's business," Wei Lei shared his experience serving a company with 7 billion yuan in annual revenue: "Including in-depth business discussions with the CEO, thorough communication with all levels of the company, uncovering needs, and earning trust."

"Earning trust" can be even harder than "uncovering needs." Organizational resistance is an obstacle consistently mentioned by interviewees. Gan Yifan's team once did AI-based store scheduling for a company, trying to save labor costs through refined operations. The results were significant, but implementation was hindered because employees' incomes dropped: "Without incentives, store managers simply won't push it."

Gan Yifan said that AI applications can't just run in an enterprise by simply building technical tools; organizational resistance is inevitable, requiring deep work within the organization on coordination and incentives. "AI has difficulty understanding emotions and context between people. A change that is perfectly reasonable on paper may still fail to move forward—a certain business department may be underperforming, but since it fought alongside the boss from day one, it has to stay there."

To overcome resistance to change, the determination of the "top leader" is crucial. Multiple interviewees said they only take orders where they can "reach agreement with the top leader," because "without very strong determination from the boss, nothing can be pushed forward." Wei Quyun was deeply impressed by the kickoff meeting on his first day at the central enterprise subsidiary: "All the mid- and senior-level staff gathered—it was quite a grand occasion. The chairman spoke personally, requiring every department to cooperate with us."

"Real FDE implementation services are extremely delivery-heavy and results-oriented," Zhou Ziyao said. Developing a single pain-point scenario takes one to two months, and deeper transformation requires a year or more of accompaniment. "We do all the dirty, tiring work and earn hard-earned money, and clients often don't understand." He had just quoted 200,000 yuan for a first-phase project to a semiconductor company, and the young boss, who had originally been very determined, wavered a bit after hearing it.

Currently, the market has no unified pricing standard. Different teams serve different clients, in different forms, with different client standards, so pricing systems vary. Zhou Ziyao's team mainly serves medium-sized enterprises with annual revenue between 50 million and 300 million yuan. On-site pricing differs by city, averaging 1,800 yuan per person-day (a common workload measurement unit in the software and IT services industry).

Rolling AI customer case. Image source: screenshot from Rolling AI's official website

Rolling AI targets private enterprises with annual revenue above 5 billion yuan. Clients subscribe monthly to a small team of several FDEs to obtain services like data analysis and causal inference, plus knowledge assets such as AI employees and industry know-how accumulated from previous cases. "A company's annual subscription is roughly in the high single-digit millions of yuan," Gan Yifan said, adding that the team typically accompanies an enterprise for at least six months.

Gan Yifan observed that this "heavy business" is facing real difficulties of varying degrees: more and more small teams and even individuals are taking on contracts as FDEs, and winning contracts is not hard, but "seventy to eighty percent of them never collect the final payment." This problem was corroborated by some interviewees. "The problem often lies in misaligned expectations—enterprises cannot articulate their needs, and FDEs lacking the capability to judge may either fail to assess them properly or over-promise just to sign the deal, leaving both sides unable to save face."

Gan Yifan expressed his concern: many people rush into the field after seeing only the surface-level hype around FDEs. Without familiarity with an industry, they may instead use AI to amplify their own misconceptions when serving clients, producing the opposite of the intended effect. "Having reverence for an industry is a basic professional service ethic."

The market chaos goes beyond this: pricing systems are chaotic, and practitioners without real-world case studies either charge exorbitant prices or undercut competitors with low bids, while the services they actually deliver do not match.

More consistent criticism is aimed at "exploitative" training courses. Xie Yao, an FDE entrepreneur and founder of OpsGo AI, categorizes these courses into three types: those targeting laid-off tech workers in their thirties and forties who want to become FDEs, claiming "enroll and we guarantee you a job"; those selling FDE certificates to fresh graduates, certificates that have nothing to do with actual work; and "AI executive bootcamps" for business owners, taught by instructors with no real-world implementation experience, promoting tools that are hard to put into practice.

"There are indeed some rather hasty moves in the market, and some low-quality FDE services have damaged the industry's reputation. But in an environment where enterprise management in China is not standardized, FDE will still be a fragmented market with enormous room for growth," Wei Lei told NBD reporters.

"AI transformation is like teaching people to use computers and Office 20 years ago," Xie Yao said. "Companies founded ten years from now will be born natively adapted to an AI-friendly business environment, and won't need anyone to teach them how to use AI at all."

Gan Yifan also believes that FDE services that purely sell "person-days" or deliver by project will struggle to become a sustainable business model in China; in the future, every enterprise will have native FDE teams that grow out of its own business. "It may not even be a formal organization—it could just be certain people who possess the FDE capability package, becoming key players driving AI transformation within small business units. This will happen at scale in the next six months." In his vision, external FDE companies in the future may no longer live on service fees, but instead ride the capital-driven asset transformation logic, working with venture capital firms to transform companies and industries with AI capabilities, and earning a share of equity appreciation.

What kind of enterprise can truly succeed at AI transformation? Gan Yifan's answer: openness, willingness to learn, and initiative.

"An FDE's role in an enterprise is very much like a maternity nanny," he said by way of analogy. The enterprise has given birth to a child called AI, which is extremely "disruptive" and upends many household routines. A good "nanny" must have professional skills and also be able to read the room—knowing who makes decisions on what matters and who can cooperate. Moreover, the "nanny" will not stay forever; the goal is to teach the family to raise the child themselves.

And for the "parents," what matters more is proactively learning how to raise the "child," rather than dumping everything on the "nanny." Gan Yifan has met quite a few owners of companies worth tens of billions of yuan. "We also 'force' them to learn cutting-edge tools, so they can form an accurate judgment of AI's effectiveness," Gan Yifan said. "But business owners who can truly succeed at AI implementation must have a sense of ownership."

A "nanny" who grows alongside the "parents"—this may be the truest portrait of the FDE business at its current stage: what it sells is not technical tools, but a period of time spent accompanying an enterprise through the transition of AI-driven productivity.
