PrivateLink vs. VPN: A Network Selection Guide for FDEs

Original title: PrivateLink 和 VPN 区别：FDE 网络选型指南

FDE Times · 2026-10-26 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide helps FDEs choose between PrivateLink and Site-to-Site VPN when deploying into customer environments. The decision hinges on three questions: who calls whom, where the data lives, and whether CIDR ranges overlap. PrivateLink suits cases where the customer calls your service, even with overlapping 10.0.0.0/16 ranges, since interface endpoints occupy customer subnet IPs. VPN fits when two networks must interconnect, but requires customer network teams to configure gateways, non-overlapping CIDRs, and offers 1.25 Gbps per tunnel (5 Gbps only via Transit Gateway or Cloud WAN). The article also covers Azure Private Link, common mistakes like assuming PrivateLink is bidirectional, and five questions to ask before writing Terraform.

Key points

  • Choose based on call direction, data location, and whether CIDR ranges overlap.
  • PrivateLink works with overlapping IP ranges since endpoints sit in customer subnets.
  • VPN requires customer network team scheduling and non-overlapping address spaces.
  • Standard VPN tunnels provide 1.25 Gbps; 5 Gbps needs Transit Gateway or Cloud WAN.
  • Common errors: treating PrivateLink as bidirectional and forgetting endpoint services deny all by default.

Key Takeaways

  • Three questions determine the choice: who needs to call whom, where the data lives, and whether the two parties' IP ranges overlap.
  • PrivateLink is the right fit when the customer calls your service, even if both sides use the same IP range. A VPN is the right fit when you need to connect two networks and the customer has a network team responsible for configuring equipment.
  • The most common mistakes: assuming PrivateLink is bidirectional, and forgetting that a VPC endpoint service by default doesn't allow anyone to connect.

At the second kickoff meeting, the customer's security lead puts it bluntly: their systems have no public IP addresses and never will. Your service runs in your company's own VPC, but the data lives in the customer's network. Only now do you discover that both sides are using 10.0.0.0/16.

Which connectivity option you choose at this point determines everything that follows: who has to configure what, which team has to free up capacity, and whether anyone needs to change the network address plan. The choice basically comes down to two options: PrivateLink (called Private Link on Azure) and a site-to-site VPN.

Both keep traffic off the public internet, but they solve different problems. A Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) should figure out which one to propose before the customer's network team even asks.

Expose a service, or connect two networks?

The easiest way to remember it: PrivateLink brings a service into the consumer's network, while a VPN joins two networks together.

According to AWS documentation, PrivateLink lets a VPC access a service as if it were one of its own internal services, without an internet gateway, NAT, public IPs, Direct Connect, or a Site-to-Site VPN.

The connection is initiated by the consumer, and the provider decides whether to accept or reject it. Traffic between a VPC endpoint and an endpoint service always stays within the AWS network and never touches the public internet.

A site-to-site VPN is an entirely different matter. It is an IPsec connection, and each connection has two encrypted tunnels that can be used in parallel for higher availability. Because the two networks are joined together, AWS recommends using non-overlapping CIDR ranges when connecting multiple VPCs to the same on-premises network.

Example: two VPCs that both use 10.0.0.0/16

Picture this: you're an FDE at a company that offers a model inference API. The customer's application runs on AWS and needs to call your API.

Both VPCs use 10.0.0.0/16. Whether it's AWS's site-to-site VPN or Azure's VNet peering, cloud providers recommend or require non-overlapping address ranges. So usually one side has to re-plan its network addresses, which can easily balloon into a standalone project far beyond the scope of your deployment.

PrivateLink sidesteps this problem. AWS explicitly states that PrivateLink is the appropriate solution when VPC IP ranges overlap; the AWS networking blog also notes that you can expose endpoints between VPCs with overlapping addresses without changing the underlying address plan.

The reason is that interface endpoints live in the customer's own subnets and take IPs from the customer's address range, so no routing conflicts arise.

The practical sequence is as follows. As the provider, you put a load balancer in front of your service, then create an endpoint service based on that load balancer.

The endpoint service is not open to anyone by default, so you must grant access to the customer's specific AWS principal, usually their AWS account. The customer creates an interface endpoint in their own subnet, and then you accept the connection request.

The final step is done by the customer, and it's the one security teams love most. The endpoint sits in the customer's subnet, so they can control access with their own security groups. For example, only their application servers are allowed to call the endpoint on port 443. They don't have to expose anything outward, nor trust a broad network route into your VPC.

When you need to call into the customer's network

Now flip the direction. Your agent needs to read a database in the customer's on-premises data center. PrivateLink follows a "consumer-initiated connection" model and doesn't automatically give you a reverse path. Having an endpoint doesn't mean the two networks can talk to each other.

There are still options. AWS says providers can run services on on-premises servers, and resource endpoints also support accessing resources in on-premises environments, so PrivateLink remains worth considering even when the customer's infrastructure isn't on AWS.

But if the real problem is "the two networks need to communicate," a site-to-site VPN is usually the more natural answer. Its costs should be spelled out from the start.

The first cost is people. The customer must configure their own VPN device (the customer gateway device), and by default that device must also generate traffic and initiate IKE negotiations to establish the tunnels. So your go-live date depends on the customer network team's schedule.

Don't confuse "initiate" here with PrivateLink's "initiate a connection." IKE is just the step that establishes the tunnel.

Next is addressing. The 10.0.0.0/16 overlap mentioned earlier becomes a real headache here, because AWS recommends non-overlapping address ranges.

Finally, bandwidth. Standard tunnels provide 1.25 Gbps. High-bandwidth tunnels of up to 5 Gbps are only available through Transit Gateway or Cloud WAN. If your agent needs to sync large volumes of data every night, work out this number in advance.

On Azure, the logic is the same

If the customer uses Azure, the terminology changes but the thinking doesn't. Microsoft states that with Azure Private Link, traffic between a virtual network and a service travels over the Microsoft backbone network, and the service doesn't need to be exposed to the public internet. Providers can create a Private Link service to offer to customers.

One detail worth mentioning to the security team: a private endpoint maps to a specific instance of a resource rather than the entire service, which reduces the risk of data exfiltration.

On-premises networks can still connect via ExpressRoute or VPN tunnels. Microsoft also positions Private Link as an alternative to VNet peering, which requires non-overlapping address spaces. This mirrors the IP overlap problem on AWS exactly.

Hands-on: five questions for the discovery meeting

Before writing your first line of Terraform, get answers to five questions. One: who calls whom? Does the customer call you, or do you call the customer? Two: where does the data live? AWS, Azure, or on-premises? Three: what are the CIDR ranges of each network, and do they overlap?

Four: what is the peak data volume per hour, and will it exceed 1.25 Gbps per tunnel? Five: who on the customer's side is responsible for network equipment, and how long does scheduling a configuration take? If the answer to question one is "the customer calls you" and the answer to question three is "overlapping," you will almost certainly propose PrivateLink.

Mistakes that stall deployments

The most common mistake is treating PrivateLink as a bidirectional pipe. It is designed for the consumer to call the provider. If your agent needs to initiate calls in the reverse direction, that path must be designed separately.

Almost as common is forgetting that endpoint services don't grant access to anyone by default. The customer creates the endpoint, gets an error, and after both sides spend an afternoon troubleshooting, they discover no one ever authorized the other party's principal.

On the VPN side, some teams configure only one tunnel, even though every connection comes with two by default, and then they're caught off guard when that single tunnel goes down. Other teams commit to a launch date before confirming whether the customer's network team can even initiate IKE.

Where this skill fits in your resume

When reading FDE job descriptions, watch for phrases like "customer VPC", "private networking", and "on-prem deployment". If they appear, the role very likely requires someone who can do this kind of work.

On your resume, don't write "familiar with AWS networking". Instead, use one sentence that captures the constraint and the outcome, for example: Delivered services into customer VPCs with overlapping IP ranges using PrivateLink, without re-planning network addresses.

In interviews, don't stop at conceptual definitions. Walk through, in order, what you asked, what you chose, and why you didn't pick the alternative. Customers rarely remember which protocol you used; they remember that their security team didn't have to open one more port to the internet.
