The rise of the forward-deployed engineer (FDE)

LeadDev · 2026-07-23 · 5 min read

Summary

LeadDev examines the surge of the forward-deployed engineer (FDE), a role pioneered at Palantir that is now being adopted by OpenAI, AWS, Microsoft and others. Demand is up sharply even as tech hiring shrinks, but the title is spreading faster than a clear definition. Insiders say most imitators fall short, and heavy travel plus a scarce talent pool raise doubts about the role's long-term sustainability.

Key points

  • FDE job postings rose over 729% year over year in April 2026, averaging $171,911 base salary.
  • OpenAI, AWS and Microsoft Frontier Company ($2.5B, 6,000 engineers) are building dedicated FDE organizations.
  • The role traces to Palantir's Deltas, emphasizing outcomes and customer-facing soft skills over methodology.
  • Palantir's Ted Mabrey says most companies copying the FDE model are 'half measures'.
  • Up to 50% travel (per an OpenAI job spec) creates burnout risk, and enterprises may eventually bring the role in-house.

Key takeaways:

  • FDE demand is surging (up 729%) even as the industry cuts jobs elsewhere.
  • The title is spreading faster than a clear definition, even Palantir calls most copies “a half measure.”
  • Sustainability is shaky: rare skills, heavy travel, and real burnout risk.

The forward-deployed engineer (FDE), which earned the title of the hottest job in startups last year, is now becoming the hottest job in tech. It has firmly entered the mainstream with large enterprises from Amazon to EY and Salesforce all investing heavily in forward-deployed engineering.

This investment runs counter to the broad trend of layoffs and slowdown in job openings within the software development industry. Job postings for FDE positions increased more than 729% in April 2026 compared to the prior year, according to data from Indeed shared with Business Insider, with the average base salary being $171,911 annually.

Mentions of “FDE” within company docs are up 17% over the past six months, according to data from market intelligence platform AlphaSense.

The emergence of the FDE

Major tech players, such as OpenAI and Amazon’s AWS, have committed billions to launch dedicated organizations to embed FDEs with clients.

Microsoft’s new operating division Microsoft Frontier Company, backed by 6,000 engineering experts and a $2.5 billion investment, even claims to go “beyond what has been labeled” as forward-deployed engineering

“The role is definitely real, but the title might have become a little bit frothy,” says Piotr Kraus, a consultant at FDE advisory firm Platypus Technologies and an ex-Palantir FDE. “It feels like a lot of organizations these days are just calling an FDE anybody who’s customer-facing and a little bit technical.”

What sets FDEs apart is their character and determination to solve a problem, Kraus says. If a client says X is the solution, an FDE will really interrogate whether that’s really the case and vocally propose alternatives backed with data and evidence if it isn’t, he says.

Who can be an FDE?

The role’s roots come from Palantir, which, until 2016, reportedly had more FDEs, known as Deltas, than software engineers. Deltas help integrate the firm’s systems into those of its clients, which include government agencies, militaries, and large corporations.

Enterprises today are much like government agencies when it comes to AI and in need of help with integration and enablement. Joe Schmidt, a partner at venture firm a16z, describes enterprises akin to a grandparent getting an iPhone. “They want to use it, but they need you to set it up.”

Palantir didn’t cook up something new, says Jeff Darnton, SVP of platform experience at data management solution Harper. It reinvented the consulting model to focus on results and outcomes rather than time spent and methodology, he says.

Soft skills and the ability to speak to customers are the strongest separator for an FDE, says Darnton. Not only do they need to be technical, but they also need to be able to interchangeably converse with the engineering team and the CTO, he says.

Technologists that are most in demand for these roles are the ones who have deep domain knowledge, says Giancarlo Hirsch, managing director at technology recruitment firm Glocomms.

“The sales engineer at Lacework that only sold to the financial services industry [and] that have drummed up solutions for how to balance cloud computing loads for large enterprises in financial services – those people are insanely valuable right now,” Hirsch says.

It’s a small market to hire from considering there’s only so many Palantir and ex-Palantir FDEs to choose from, says Adam Moore, founder of recruitment firm Morela.

He’s found that data engineers with strong analytical backgrounds have succeeded transitioning into the role, and being able to demonstrate client-facing experience, strong technical abilities, and industry expertise can put a candidate in a strong position to be an FDE.

Is this sustainable?

Kraus believes the small market of available Palantir talent is playing a role in the surge of FDE offerings. He suspects companies are hoping to replicate Palantir’s success and will pitch their FDEs to other companies that don’t have the means to access Palantir or its talent.

“With the popularity of coding engines and AI solutions, it’s not so much about one specific technology [anymore], but actually getting deep into the problem that needs to be solved, so you need somebody who will be very embedded and critical about the existing processes,” he says. “The question is, can they really hire and find a true FDE and can they define the role?”

Ted Mabrey, Palantir’s global head of commercial, said on Substack that tech companies trying to copy Palantir’s FDE model were largely failing, with every replicant he’d encountered being “a half measure.”

Darnton warns there will be a lot of professional services firms that attempt to adopt this that shouldn’t. It’s a way to deploy engineering resources who are on the bench since the software development work is being absorbed by AI, says Chris Slovak, global field CTO at AI delivery platform Unframe.

“[It’s] kind of weird if you think about it, right?” Slovak says. “A lot of the solutions that people are trying to build are to remove human dependencies and automate productivity. [Now] they’re really adding expensive, hard-to-find humans to replace humans – doesn’t seem very sustainable.”

While an FDE has value in some niche areas, Slovak speculates that frameworks will emerge to fix most of the issues and that enterprises will start bringing the role in-house to manage different integrations.

Hirsch echoes similar sentiments. “Otherwise you’re gonna end up having the little kid from Palantir know every single thing about your secret sauce,” he says, referencing comments from Palantir’s CEO Alex Karp on how the frontier labs are leveraging client data, which may create some hesitance in using their FDEs.

The FDE role also has the potential to be unsustainable in a different way. A job spec from OpenAI shows that up to 50% travel is required as an example. The constant travel makes the role susceptible to burnout a lot earlier than others, says Kraus, who often worked in remote, hard-to-reach locations.

“It’s definitely not for everybody,” Moore says. “It’s a very challenging role.”
