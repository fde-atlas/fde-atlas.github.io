What's the difference between an FDE and a consultant? The reality across 42 companies in Japan and key points to check before placing an order

Original title: FDEとコンサルの違いは？国内42社の実態と発注前の確認点

AIエージェントナビ · 2026-09-02 · 11 min read

Summary

This article distinguishes FDEs from consultants on three axes: deliverables (documents versus working software), whether the person defining requirements also implements, and pricing (billable hours versus outcome-based fees). It cites Palantir's official blogs, where FDSEs deny being consultants and stress deploying existing products quickly. A survey of 42 Japanese companies offering FDE-style services found 7 originated from consulting firms and 29 publish no contract terms. The author proposes four questions buyers should ask before contracting, focusing on deliverables, implementation team, product lock-in, and pricing.

Key points

  • FDE vs consulting differs in deliverables, implementer, and compensation.
  • Palantir's blogs call the consultant comparison a common misconception.
  • 7 of 42 Japanese FDE-style providers are former consulting firms.
  • 29 of the 42 companies do not disclose contract or pricing terms.
  • Buyers should verify deliverables and who implements before signing.

The differences come down to three points: deliverables, who does the implementation, and compensation. An "FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)" is an engineer who embeds at the customer's site and handles everything through implementation, but from the client's perspective it can be hard to distinguish from traditional consulting, and the two are often confused.

Three differences between FDEs and consultants

The difference lies not in the name, but in how the project is run and the scope of responsibility.

Deliverables: documents or something that works

What you ultimately receive is the clearest difference.

  • The primary deliverable of consulting is reports and slide decks presenting analysis results and strategy
  • The deliverable of an FDE is the application itself, actually running in the business environment
  • Accordingly, the evaluation criteria also split between the persuasiveness of the proposal and whether operations actually improved

Whether they take responsibility for a visible "working product" is the boundary between the two.

Implementation: whether the same person builds it

Another difference is whether the person who defines the requirements is the one who actually builds it.

  • In the traditional approach, strategy was often drawn up first and then development was handed off to a different company or department
  • In the FDE model, the person who organized the requirements builds it themselves on-site and keeps iterating on it
  • Because there is no handoff in between, rework caused by misinterpretation is less likely to occur

Compensation: hours or outcomes

There are also differences in how fees are structured—what exactly you pay for.

  • Traditional consulting and system development generally billed based on hours worked
  • In the FDE model, contracts that pay for delivering agreed outcomes within an agreed period may be available
  • However, as described later, not all FDE-type services are outcome-based

The official view of Palantir, where the FDE originated

Palantir itself, the US company that created the FDE role, answers this question on its official blog.

Official question and answer

In the official blog interview with an FDSE published by Palantir on November 2, 2020, there is a question headed "Is an FDSE similar to a consultant?" The answer begins with "No, not really."

The person answering is Brian, an FDSE who at the time handled projects for the US Department of Defense. The article is from 2020, showing that the role existed before the generative AI boom.

It is described as a "common misconception"

Another official blog post published on April 8, 2019, discussing the internal title "Delta (= FDSE)," states: "A common misconception is that a Delta is a consultant." This appears in the body of the article, followed by multiple employees explaining the difference. Next is a quote from Elisa, based in New York.

However, consultants generally create a one-time analysis, recommendation, or solution to a specific problem, while at Palantir we work together with our customers to build a long-term solution that allows the customer to continuously improve themselves.

(However, while consultants typically produce one-off analyses, recommendations, or solutions for a specific problem, at Palantir we work with customers to build long-term solutions that the customers themselves can continuously improve)

The decisive difference is "being able to combine off-the-shelf components"

In the 2020 article, Brian places the core of the difference in the fact that existing products can be combined as they are.

Unlike consultants, we can pull most of the pieces together out-of-the-box, meaning we don’t need to reinvent the wheel for each customer and spend years creating a patchwork solution.

(Unlike consultants, we combine many features as standard functionality, so we don't need to reinvent the wheel for each customer or spend years on patchwork solutions)

In the 2019 article as well, Dyon, based in Abu Dhabi, makes the same point.

In my mind, the critical difference is that we are actually deploying existing software products to achieve the customer’s outcomes

(In my opinion, the decisive difference is that we are actually deploying existing software products for customer outcomes)

In other words, the difference Palantir describes is not whether you enter the field, but whether you can get something running quickly using existing products.

FDE offerings by domestic consulting firms

So far this has been the story of where it originated. In Japan, things are a bit more complicated.

7 of the 42 domestic companies come from consulting

When this media outlet surveyed domestic companies offering FDE services based on public information, 42 companies were identified as of August 2026. The breakdown is as follows.

  • 13 on-site implementation type, 8 own-product-based type, 7 consulting-accompaniment type
  • 5 industry-specialized type, 4 enterprise type, 4 talent-matching type, 1 certified partner program type
  • In other words, 7 of the companies calling themselves "FDE-type" originally had consulting as their core business

In Japan, FDEs and consultants are less two opposing options than a situation where consulting firms themselves are shifting toward the FDE model. Note that these 42 companies are those identifiable under this outlet's listing criteria, not an exhaustive survey of Japan.

Each company itself writes that it is "different from traditional consulting"

What is notable is that the ones articulating the difference are not outside commentators but the companies themselves. Atstream Consulting writes the following on its service page.

提案書ではなく“動くもの”を起点に、「聞く・作る・使う」の短いサイクルを高速に回すことで、現場に根づき継続的に成果を生み出すAI活用を支援します。

The company explicitly names the division of labor itself as the problem, explaining that in this model "handoffs inevitably occur, leading to rework from misinterpretation and delays before anything actually works."

In a press release announcing the launch of the service, T3 defined its own positioning as the negation of two existing models.

AIコンサルティングのように「提言して終わり」でも、SES（システムエンジニアリングサービス）のように「指示されたものを作る」でもない、

The same is true of NTT Data Group's Fortience Consulting and the management consulting firm KI Strategy. The declarations of the four companies, lined up, are as follows.

Even under the same "FDE-style" label, the substance differs from company to company

What you should note is the fact that they are divided into seven categories. Even under the same banner, the reality differs.

  • In the consulting-accompaniment type, the consultants themselves enter the field and handle everything from problem definition to implementation
  • In the own-product-premise type, introducing that company's AI product is the starting point
  • In the talent-matching type, FDE talent is introduced or assigned, which changes where responsibility for implementation lies

A description like "We are FDE-style" alone does not tell you which of these it is. That is precisely why you need to verify the substance of the contract, not the label.

Compensation thinking is shifting from man-hours to outcomes

The third difference, compensation thinking, is an issue currently in flux.

Accenture explains that "the thinking is exactly the opposite"

Yasuyuki Hokase, head of Accenture's AI Center, described the relationship with traditional models in an ITmedia interview.

発想が真逆です。従来のコンサルタントやエンジニアを無理やり違う見せ方で売ろうという気は1ミリもありません

What he emphasizes is the shift from man-hour-based to outcome-based standards. Behind this is the question, "In an era when generative AI can dramatically compress development man-hours, is this billing model really correct?" The article explains that what the company practices is an outcome-based compensation model, where payment is received for delivering defined outcomes within a set period.

The current state of outcome-based compensation

However, not all FDE-providing companies use outcome-based compensation.

  • KI Strategy publicly states its pricing structure as "a fixed consulting fee, an outcome-based fee linked to results, or a combination of these, selectable as desired."
  • Meanwhile, of the 42 companies our media investigated, only 13 disclose their fees or contract terms in some form
  • The remaining 29 companies do not list contract terms on their official websites

Do not assume "FDE-style means outcome-based compensation"; check individually what standard each company uses for billing.

Some practicing consultants see it as "nothing new"

From within the consulting industry, there are also skeptical views. In a note article dated June 7, 2026, "gaishikei usagi no Choko-san" wrote the following about moves by Accenture and others.

なんというか今更FDEと言い始められても感はなくもないですが、

On that basis, they wrote this conclusion.

「FDEとかそういうの関係なく、実際に動く業務やシステムを作り切ること」まで今のコンサルには当たり前に求められているので、いかに組織全体でFDE的な価値発揮のスタイルを当たり前のものとしていくか、という話だけなのでは？

This is one writer's perspective, not the industry's consensus, but it offers insight for clients. It means you should choose not by words, but by whether they can actually build something that works.

Four questions to confirm before placing an order

The differences covered so far can be turned directly into pre-order questions. Here are four for verifying the substance of the contract, not the label.

Questions about deliverables

First, confirm whether completion means submitting documents or having a working system.

  • Can they commit to delivering something that actually runs in the field?
  • Do the deliverables include operational screens for field users or automation mechanisms?
  • Are recommendation reports or slides the core of the deliverables?

Questions about the implementation team

Check whether the "handover" that Atstream specifically called out is also embedded in that proposal.

  • Do the people attending meetings make technical decisions with their own hands?
  • Is development premised on being handed off to another company or department?
  • Is the setup one where messages are relayed through intermediaries with every specification change?

Questions about product introduction

Confirm whether this is the "own-product-premise type" in the 42-company categorization. This is not about superiority or inferiority, but because the premise changes.

  • Is introducing a specific product or license the starting point?
  • Is the proposal made after also considering the option of not using that product?
  • Are product usage fees and support costs shown separately?

Questions about pricing structure

Since 29 of the 42 companies do not disclose contract terms, this question must be confirmed verbally.

  • Is the cost structure determined solely by working hours (person-months)?
  • Can the metrics for outcomes to be achieved be agreed upon in writing before the contract?
  • Is there an option to combine fixed fees with outcome-linked compensation?

Conclusion: Choose by substance

  • The differences are threefold: whether the deliverable is documents or something that works, whether the same team does the implementation, and whether compensation is based on man-hours or outcomes
  • Palantir says it is a different thing: its official blog answers "No, not really." to "Are you similar to consultants?", and another article calls it a "common misconception"
  • In Japan, the boundaries are blurred: of the 42 companies calling themselves FDE-style, 7 are consulting firms, and each officially declares its differences from traditional models
  • So you cannot judge by the label: 29 of the 42 companies do not disclose contract terms

At your next meeting, ask at least two of the four questions above: "What are the deliverables?" and "Who does the implementation?" The answers to these two will tell you almost everything about whether the proposal in front of you ends as documents or reaches something that actually works.
