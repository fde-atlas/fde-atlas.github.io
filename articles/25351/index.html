How to Choose a DX Consultant | Key Points to Check on Cost, Implementation, and Adoption Support

Original title: DXコンサルの選び方｜費用の考え方と実装・定着まで頼む確認点

FDE Consulting · 2026-10-26 · 8 min read

Summary

This article explains how to choose a DX consulting firm when you want implementation and adoption, not just a plan. The author sorts DX consulting into four types—strategy, development, tool deployment, and hands-on implementation support—and argues most selection failures come from mismatching a company's stage with the vendor type. Citing IPA's DX Trends 2026 survey: nearly 80% of companies pursue DX, 60% report results, but gains concentrate in digitization and efficiency, while business model and organizational transformation lag; 85.5% lack sufficient DX talent. Seven questions to ask before contracting are listed, including whether the proposal writer visits the site, which metric will move first, who builds the system, and whether there is a post-delivery adjustment period—the last two being the most decisive. On fees, no public market data exists, so the author suggests matching payment to contract type (junior entrusted, fixed-price, or performance-based) and splitting contracts by phase, referencing METI's AI contract checklist. The hands-on role described overlaps with FDE work.

Key points

  • IPA's 2026 survey shows DX results stall at digitization and efficiency, not transformation.
  • Four consulting types exist; failures stem from stage-type mismatch.
  • Seven pre-contract checks; who builds and post-delivery adjustment matter most.
  • No public fee benchmarks; align payment with contract type and phase.
  • The on-site, rebuild-until-adopted role overlaps with FDE work.

DX consulting is support that helps a company's DX from the outside. There is no fixed definition: some companies only draft management visions, some build systems, and some go into the field and keep fixing things until they are actually used. Under the same name of "DX consulting," where the work ends differs completely.

DX itself has a definition in METI's Digital Governance Code 3.0: using data and digital technology to transform products, services, and business models, while also transforming operations themselves, organizations, processes, and corporate culture, in order to establish competitive advantage. To reach the latter half of that definition—changing operations and organization—a proposal document alone is not enough.

This article summarizes the checkpoints for those choosing a DX consultant. The difference from AI-centered transformation is covered in What is AX (AI Transformation)?, and how to choose a consultant focused specifically on AI implementation is covered in How to Choose an AI Consultant, so here I will focus on how to choose support that calls itself "DX" on the assumption that you want it to handle implementation and adoption as well.

Where DX results are stalling

The key points of "DX Trends 2026," published by IPA (Information-technology Promotion Agency) on July 16, 2026, capture the current state of DX well.

Key points of IPA "DX Trends 2026"Details
DX initiativesNearly 80% of companies are working on DX in some form
DX results60% of companies answered that "results are appearing." No major change from the previous two surveys
Initiatives with results"Digitization of analog and physical data" and "productivity improvement through operational efficiency" are high
Initiatives with low resultsFundamental transformation of business models and fundamental transformation of corporate culture and organizational management are relatively low
Relationship with AIOf companies working on DX, 54.2% in total use AI as part of, or at the center of, driving DX
TalentThe total of "somewhat insufficient" and "significantly insufficient" for the quantity of personnel driving DX is 85.5%

In my view, these results can be read as "companies have progressed to the entrance of DX but are stalling at the transformation stage." Converting paper to data and speeding up work could be done in-house. Beyond that, at the stage of changing workflows and organizations, people are lacking.

Most companies looking for a DX consultant are at this stage. So the selection criteria should also focus not on the impressiveness of the vision, but on who will carry the transformation stage forward, and how far, together with you.

Types of DX consultants and where their work ends

I divide support that calls itself DX consulting into the following four types. This is not about specific companies but an organization by work style.

TypeMain workWhere the work endsSuited consultations
Vision typeOrganizing management issues, creating DX direction and plansDelivery of plan and proposal documentsWhat to change first is not decided company-wide
Development typeRequirements definition and system developmentDelivery of a system built to specificationWhat to build is decided
Tool introduction typeIntroduction and setup of specific products or SaaSThe point where the product is ready to useThe product to use is decided
Implementation companion typeGoing into the field, building, and fixing until it is usedWhere operational numbers move and things run in-houseThere is a plan, but nothing is moving in the field

It is not a matter of which is good or bad. Suitability is determined at the consultation stage. If the company-wide direction is not yet decided, starting with the vision type is faster. Conversely, if there are already stacks of plan documents but the field is not changing, layering more vision-type consulting will produce the same outcome.

In my view, most failures in choosing a DX consultant arise from a mismatch between your company's stage and the vendor's type. At the first meeting, ask where the vendor's work ends, and you can usually tell the type.

Seven points to verify when entrusting implementation and adoption

If you want to entrust the work through implementation and adoption rather than stop at a plan, in my view it is good to verify the following seven points in the sales meeting. For each, I have included a question example you can use as is.

1. Will the person who made the proposal come to the field?

If the person who compiled the proposal and the person who actually comes to the field are different, information gets lost in between.

Question example: "How far into the process will the person who compiled this proposal be involved? How many times a week will they come to the field?"

2. Will they decide upfront which numbers to move?

If DX proceeds without measurable results, no one can explain what changed after it ends. Ask whether they will decide on one number to move at the start.

Question example: "In the first month, which number will you target to move? Who will decide that?"

3. Will they come to observe the actual operations?

For support that changes operations, it is necessary to see not only meeting rooms but also the places where work is actually performed.

Question example: "Will you interview field staff and observe actual work? How much time will you allocate for that?"

4. Will they handle the building as well?

If the company making the plan and the company building it are different, interpretation drifts between plan and implementation. Verify whether they will handle the building, or hand it off to another company.

Question example: "After the plan, will your company build the system or AI mechanism? If it is another company, how will you hand it over?"

5. Is there a period for fixing things until they are used?

The day of delivery is only the beginning of transformation. Check whether the contract includes a period to observe usage and make fixes.

Question example: "After delivery, is there a period to observe usage and make fixes? During that period, what will you observe and what will you fix?"

6. What will they leave behind in-house?

A state where things do not run once the outside people leave is not DX but dependency. Ask upfront what will remain in-house when the work ends.

Question example: "When the contract ends, will the procedure manuals, design records, and code remain with our company? Who in-house is expected to take them over?"

7. Will they decide the conditions for stopping upfront?

Continuing initiatives that are not working only accumulates costs. Decide in advance the forum for judging whether to continue or stop.

Question example: "At what point, and based on what, will you judge whether to continue or stop?"

Of the seven, in my view the biggest differences appear in points 4 and 5. A vendor who answers "that is another company's job" considers their work to end at producing the plan. That may suffice for some consultations, but if you want to entrust implementation and adoption as well, it is better to look for another vendor.

How to think about DX consulting costs

Look at what you are paying for, rather than market rates

As far as I have researched, I could not find public statistics showing market rates for DX consulting costs. The amount varies greatly depending on the number of people and duration involved and whether something is built. Rather than comparing amounts, aligning on what you are paying for first makes the differences visible.

What you pay for is determined by the form of the contract. The Civil Code provides for "ukrei" (contract for work), where remuneration is paid for completion of the work (Article 632), and "jun'inin" (quasi-mandate), where work is entrusted to another party (Article 656). Even under a quasi-mandate, it is possible to agree that remuneration is paid for results (Article 648-2).

Form of contractWhat you pay forSuitable stageWhat to verify
Quasi-mandate (paid by time)Time involved or working hoursConcept stage or trial and error in the fieldHow many hours per month and who is involved
Contract for workThe completed deliverableDevelopment where what to build is decidedWhat counts as completion
Quasi-mandate paid on resultsAgreed-upon resultsWhen the target metrics are setHow results are measured and who measures them

The two right-hand columns of the table are my own categorization. In my view, since the outcomes of a transformation stage cannot be predicted in advance, dividing the contract by stage causes less loss for the client than locking everything into a contract for work from the start. Which form applies depends on the substance of the contract, so if you are unsure, it is advisable to have a lawyer review the contract.

Materials to read before signing a contract

For DX using AI, the "Contract Checklist for AI Use and Development" published by METI on February 18, 2025 is useful. It is a document designed to help you consider the scope of use of the data you provide, service levels, and conditions for using AI-generated outputs, and it assumes scenarios where business department staff consider a contract for the first time. It is a good idea to read it together with the other party when deciding how data handed to a DX consultant will be handled.

What to decide internally before hiring

Even if you hire a DX consultant, without someone internally who makes decisions, the proposal will remain just paper. In my view, deciding the following three things beforehand makes the conversation concrete from the very first meeting.

  • One metric you want to move. It can be anything: sales, processing time, number of inquiries, etc.
  • The manager who runs that operation daily. Do not limit meetings to management only.
  • The person who decides internally. The person who decides whether to proceed or not based on the consultant's proposal.

National systems can also serve as a tool for measuring your company's stage. The DX Certification System is a government program that certifies companies recognized as compliant with the Digital Governance Code and ready to promote DX, and it applies to all businesses. Whether or not you seek certification, reviewing your company against the items of the Digital Governance Code makes it easier to discuss internally what is lacking and what to outsource.

The people who drive transformation stages forward in the field

As IPA's survey shows, many companies' DX efforts stall after digitization and efficiency improvements. Moving beyond that point is not the job of those who write plans, but of those who go into the field, redesign workflows, and keep adjusting until the systems are actually used. In my view, this role overlaps with the work of an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer).
