FDE Requirements Discovery Methods: How to Find Customers' Real Problems

Original title: FDE 需求发现方法：如何找到客户的真问题

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 4 min read

Summary

Drawing on Learn Cursor's FDE interview course and a Perspective AI discovery handbook, the article argues that a customer's stated requirement is a hypothesis to test, not the real problem. In its example, a request to automate PR review masked the actual bottleneck: only two staff engineers could approve PRs and were overloaded. It outlines a five-stage discovery process—Scope, Interview, Synthesize, Validate, Spec—each with a concrete completion criterion, such as mapping people and systems on one page or writing a spec teammates can build from without meetings. It also flags three failure modes and notes that Palantir-style FDE interviews test exactly this decomposition skill, rewarding candidates who spend the first ten minutes asking questions.

Key points

  • Treat every stated requirement as a hypothesis; validating it can save three weeks of wasted work.
  • Ask customers to walk through their most recent release and identify where it waited longest.
  • Five stages—Scope, Interview, Synthesize, Validate, Spec—each with a clear completion criterion.
  • Common failures: jumping to solutions, interviewing only the requester, specs that need another meeting.
  • FDE interviews at Palantir test this skill; strong candidates spend the first ten minutes asking questions.

Treat every requirement as a hypothesis to be validated. Ask the right few questions, and you can save three weeks of building the wrong thing.

Key Takeaways

  • A customer's stated requirement is only a hypothesis: behind "automate PR reviews" may lie the fact that only two people have approval authority.
  • Requirements discovery has five phases; scope is complete when people and systems fit on one page; a spec is complete when teammates can start building without another meeting.
  • The problem decomposition section of the FDE interview tests exactly this skill: strong candidates spend the first ten minutes asking questions.

Imagine a customer sends you one sentence: "Our team needs a tool that can automatically review pull requests." A solution instantly forms in your head: an agent that reads diffs, writes comments, and assigns risk scores. Three weeks later you finish the demo, the customer says it works great, and yet the time from PR submission to merge is exactly the same as before.

The Learn Cursor Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) interview course opens with this exact scenario. The customer's engineer hands you a problem statement, and according to the course, this is almost never the real problem.

In this example, the bottleneck is not a lack of automation. Reviews take four days because only two staff engineers have approval authority, and both are drowning in work.

For developers who want to move into FDE roles, this is precisely the skill that separates an FDE from someone who just takes tickets. Writing code you already know how to do; what you need to learn is turning vague requirements into a plan that the customer and your teammates both agree is heading in the right direction.

A Requirement Is Only a Hypothesis

The FDE requirements discovery handbook published by Perspective AI in July 2026 starts from a premise that sounds a bit blunt: what customers say they need is not what they actually need. This is not because customers are lying. They are describing the solution they can imagine, in the language of the tools they are familiar with, rather than describing where the work is actually stuck.

So "we need to automate PR reviews" should be read as "the customer believes automated reviews will make everything faster." That is a hypothesis, and your first job is to test it. If the hypothesis holds, you build with more confidence; if it doesn't, you have just saved yourself three weeks.

What Question Flips the Problem?

The common mistake is asking the customer "which features do you want?" This only gets them to repeat their original request. Learn Cursor recommends the opposite approach: ask the customer to walk you through the most recent change they shipped and point out where it waited the longest.

This question works because it pulls the conversation back to something that actually happened, with dates and specific people involved. Here is a hypothetical dialogue showing how it unfolds:

FDE: Could you walk me through the most recent change your team shipped? From PR submission to production, where did it wait the longest?

Customer: Probably the review. After a PR is submitted, it takes about four days for someone to approve it.

FDE: Who approves during those four days? What are they waiting on?

Customer: Only two staff engineers can approve. They also handle on-call and system design, and they always have a dozen or more PRs piled up.

At this point, the problem has changed. An agent that automatically writes comments will not get PRs out of these two people's queue. A more sensible solution might be helping these two review faster, or clarifying which changes need their approval and which don't, but which one to choose should be validated with the customer first.

Five Phases, Each with a Completion Criterion

A good question is not yet a methodology. The Perspective AI handbook divides requirements discovery into five sequential phases: Scope, Interview, Synthesize, Validate, and Spec. Each phase has a condition that tells you when you can move to the next one.

The completion criterion for scoping is being able to draw the people and systems involved on a single page. In the PR example, that page includes the people writing code, the two approvers, the CI system, and the code repository. This phase ends with an explicit decision point: who the system should help take what action. If you can't say that sentence, you're not ready to interview.

In the interview phase, use event-based questions like the one above with the people who actually do the work every day, not just the person who filed the request. Synthesis means organizing what you heard into a set of opportunities and prioritizing them. A bottleneck mentioned by many people usually deserves more attention than one person's complaint.

In the validation phase, go back to the customer's domain experts and confirm that the top-ranked opportunity is truly the most worth building. With a ranking in hand, you'll be tempted to skip this step, which is exactly why it must be kept. Finally, the spec phase is judged by the "handoff test": a teammate can pick up the spec and start building without any additional meetings.

Mistakes That Make Requirements Discovery Fail

The first and most common mistake is jumping straight to a solution. techinterview.org's analysis of FDE interviews describes exactly this: candidates rush to present a solution as soon as they hear the question. In real work, the result of this mistake is a polished demo that nobody uses.

The second mistake is talking only to the person who made the request. The engineer who filed the ticket sees the symptoms; only the two approvers know why the queue is so long. If your one-page diagram contains only one name, you can be almost certain someone is missing.

The third mistake is writing a spec that requires another meeting to explain. If teammates need a meeting to understand the spec, the decisions are still stuck in your head. Go back and check: are the decision points written down? Is the reasoning for choosing this opportunity made clear?

The Interview Tests Exactly This Skill

This is not just a skill you use after onboarding. According to techinterview.org, Palantir's problem decomposition section tests the ability to break a vague real-world challenge into smaller pieces and then propose solutions. Strong candidates spend the first ten minutes asking questions.

So when preparing for interviews, train yourself to resist the urge to immediately draw an architecture diagram. First ask who is using it, where the work is stuck, and which decision is waiting; then verbally walk through the map of people and systems before proposing any solution.

On your resume, instead of writing "built tool X," write a sentence about a time you discovered the original requirement was wrong and how you adjusted course. That one sentence demonstrates exactly the ability the problem decomposition section is testing.

The next time you receive a one-sentence requirement, don't open your editor first; take out a blank sheet of paper first. If you still can't draw who is waiting on whom, you don't yet know what to build.
