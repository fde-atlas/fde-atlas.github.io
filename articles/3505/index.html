Forward Deployed Engineers Across Industries: Defense, Healthcare, Finance, and Beyond

Oxagile · 2026-07-28 · 10 min read

Summary

This Oxagile article argues that forward deployed engineers (FDEs) close the post-deployment value gap TSIA identifies: software ships, but connecting it to real systems, workflows, and users is the hard part. Industry constraints differ—streaming faces device fragmentation and live-event latency, healthcare must integrate legacy EHRs under HIPAA, finance has near-zero tolerance for errors and heavy audit demands, manufacturing links decades-old equipment, and defense involves classified, air-gapped environments (the model Palantir popularized). FDEs work on-site, integrate with production systems, stay engaged after launch, and feed lessons back into the product. The article lists conditions justifying an FDE model, frames ROI as faster time to value, lower deployment risk, better adoption, and reusable improvements, and suggests most organizations can use external FDE services during high-risk phases rather than building a permanent team, then transition ownership internally.

Key points

  • FDEs address the post-deployment gap between purchased software and realized business value.
  • Deployment bottlenecks differ by industry but follow the same underlying pattern.
  • Four FDE principles: learn on-site, build against real systems, own outcomes, feed lessons back.
  • ROI shows up as faster time to value, lower risk, stronger adoption, reusable components.
  • Most firms can hire FDE services for high-risk phases instead of building a permanent team.

It’s easy to buy powerful software. Getting it into production and delivering measurable business value is a different game altogether. TSIA calls this the post-deployment value gap. Software works as designed, yet organizations struggle to connect it to their systems, workflows, and users1.

As to why, that depends on the industry. For streaming company, the issue could be device fragmentation and live-event latency. A hospital has to connect older health record systems to the new software. A manufacturer connects modern software to decades-old equipment.

But even though the reason changes, the pattern stays the same: software is harder to roll out when a team can’t see every real-world issue ahead of time.

Forward deployed engineers help close the gap by working inside the operating environment , integrating with production systems, and feeding what they learn back into the product. The real question is whether your organization actually needs FDEs, and what business conditions justify adopting this operating model.

Key takeaways:

  • The strongest indicators for an FDE model are complex environments, costly rollouts, and deployment-specific integrations.
  • The cost of building an FDE team should be evaluated against forward deployed engineer ROI, measured through faster time to value, stronger adoption, lower deployment risk, and reusable engineering improvements.
  • Many organizations capture the benefits of the model without building a large permanent FDE department.

How FDEs close the post-deployment gap

The role of a forward-deployed engineer, sometimes referred to as an FDE embedded engineer, embedded software engineer, or embedded systems engineer, can be summed up in four basic principles:

They learn inside the real environment

FDEs work inside the operating environment to understand the workflows and find problems that wouldn’t normally be visible in requirements documents.

They build against real systems and data

They integrate with production APIs, permissions, infrastructure, and existing software.

They stay tied to the result after launch

They stay involved beyond go-live to verify that the software delivers the intended business outcome.

They bring field lessons back to the product team

Recurring integration issues and workflow gaps become product improvements that benefit future deployments.

FDEs across industries

The industries below look different on the surface, but they all expose the same deployment pattern. Each illustrates a different operational bottleneck and shows whether forward deployed engineering would create meaningful value in an organization.

Video, OTT, and streaming

The constraint

Streaming platforms operate across fragmented device ecosystems, unpredictable network conditions, and distribution partners. Everything from advertising to analytics depends on how data moves across the platform.

Why traditional delivery stalls

Successful browser testing doesn’t guarantee reliable playback on Smart TVs, connected devices, or during peak live events. The same goes for a recommendation engine. Disconnected metadata limits recommendation quality.

How FDE changes the outcome

FDEs validate playback across devices, resolve integration issues with advertising and analytics platforms, improve content metadata, and troubleshoot production behavior that only appears under real traffic conditions.

The business result

Possible outcomes to measure:

  • Playback time
  • Failed sessions
  • Device coverage
  • Live-event stability
  • Engagement
  • Churn
  • Ad revenue

Defense and national security

The constraint

Defense deployments, like those that the Palantir forward deployed model was built for, often take place in secure environments with classified data and limited network access. Engineers may also need special clearance and approved hardware to work with these customers.

Why traditional delivery stalls

Much of the operational context only exists inside the secure environment, making it difficult to design and validate integrations remotely. If there’s a plan built in a standard cloud setup, it may need major changes once it reaches a classified or air-gapped network.

This is the model Palantir popularized: FDSEs embed directly with operational teams to configure the platform around mission-specific problems2.

How FDE changes the outcome

FDE engineers adapt the software to secure operating environments and solve integration problems before bringing repeated needs back for reuse.

The business result

Common success metrics:

  • Time to operational use
  • System availability
  • Analyst hours saved
  • User adoption
  • Security approval time
  • Mission readiness

Healthcare and life sciences

The constraint

Healthcare software enters environments where patient safety, privacy, and interoperability all influence deployment decisions. It’s a HIPAA requirement to have safeguards in place for electronic health information, so security has to be part of the rollout from the get-go3.

New applications also need to integrate with older EHRs, lab systems, billing tools, and medical devices, and if the data sharing isn’t safe, it can go against the priorities of Federal Health IT guidance4.

Why traditional delivery stalls

If data doesn’t map correctly or accessibility gets in the way of the tool’s workflow, it creates additional work. Incorrect data mapping, user permissions, or workflow mismatches quickly create manual work and slow clinical adoption.

How FDE changes the outcome

FDEs work with clinicians, IT, security, and compliance teams to fit the different systems and remove any potential workflow friction based on unique users or permissions. Deploying compliant software without disrupting patient care is the challenging part.

The business result

Metrics to track may include:

  • Time to clinical use
  • Fewer manual steps
  • Interface error
  • Clinician adoption
  • Turnaround time
  • Support volume

Finance and banking

The constraint

Finance is tough because the cost of failure is extremely high. Small implementation mistakes can interrupt transactions, delay settlements, or trigger compliance issues.

Financial systems depend on accurate, auditable data and tightly controlled access. Examiners review how these systems are connected and protected5. But transactions move through these systems in seconds, leaving little room for manual intervention.

Every new integration introduces additional security, governance, and access-control requirements, which makes it even harder to control who can access sensitive data and how that data is used.

Why traditional delivery stalls

Generic integration plans rarely account for institution-specific approval workflows, audit requirements, records, reporting, or backup procedures, meaning it might fail review.

How FDE changes the outcome

The FDE engineer works with the teams responsible for operations, risk, compliance, and security. By working together, they can better fit the integration into the approval process, monitor how it performs, test what happens when something goes wrong, and document how the system works.

The business result

Common measures include:

  • Processing time
  • Manual review volume
  • Reconciliation errors
  • Incident rates
  • Audit findings
  • Time to launch

Manufacturing and supply chain

The constraint

On a factory floor, software may need to pull data from an older machine, send it to the company’s ERP system, and warn the team before production stops.

Connecting plant equipment with business systems introduces new integration, security, and reliability challenges6. If there’s legacy equipment that predates cloud platforms and their current security standards, deployment gets far more complex than a typical enterprise software rollout7.

Why traditional delivery stalls

Traditional delivery teams often plan rollouts using diagrams and technical documents. But those don’t always capture everything happening on the plant floor. If machines use older protocols or sensor data arrives in inconsistent formats, even the smallest software changes can completely interrupt production.

How FDE changes the outcome

The difference with FDEs is that the engineer stays close to the plant as the software is put into use. They work with operators, IT teams, and equipment vendors to connect the systems, test the workflow in real production, and fix problems the original plan may not have accounted for.

The business result

What could be tracked:

  • Downtime
  • Throughput
  • Maintenance time
  • Inventory accuracy
  • Scrap
  • Forecast accuracy
  • Time to bring a new site online

When to hire a forward deployed engineer

Forward deployed engineering makes the most sense when deployment remains an engineering problem beyond delivery. It might be worth considering a forward deployed model over a software engineer if more than a few of the following are true:

  • Each deployment environment has a different technical setup
  • Critical workflows only become clear during deployment
  • The rollout depends on customer-owned data, APIs, infrastructure, or third-party systems
  • Product engineers are spending too much time on one-off deployments
  • The deployment requires engineering support after the product goes live
  • Delays affect revenue, renewals, safety, compliance, or trust
  • Lessons from one deployment may serve as improvements the product for future customers

AI projects are particularly well suited for AI FDE support if the model has to work with private data, internal permissions, evaluation rules, and organization-specific workflows. If your team is still trying to figure out the best approach, you can start with AI consulting services.

How forward deployed engineers create economic value

Forward deployed engineer ROI becomes easier to evaluate when each field behavior is tied to a measurable business outcome.

FDE behavior Business outcome Example metrics
Learning in the operating environment Faster time to value Time to first usable release, implementation time
Building with real systems and data Lower deployment risk Integration errors, downtime, support volume
Staying tied to the outcome Better adoption and retention Active use, renewal rate, churn, expansion
Feeding lessons back to product Lower cost on future deployments Reusable components, shorter rollout time, fewer repeated issues

Early discovery identifies deployment blockers before they become expensive. In healthcare, it may be EHR permissions. In streaming, device-specific playback issues. The point is that removing those blockers quicker reduces rework.

Working inside production environments also exposes valuable opportunities. A manufacturer, for instance, might start by tracking how equipment is running and later use that information to plan maintenance.

Outcome ownership improves adoption and retention since the team is staying involved long enough to see if and where users are struggling. The relevant measure may be churn in a subscription business or production uptime in manufacturing.

The pattern is the same across every industry discussed above. The operational constraint changes, but the business outcomes remain remarkably consistent: faster deployment, lower risk, stronger adoption, and a product that improves with every implementation.

One of the biggest long-term advantages is that it improves future deployment economics:

  • Repeated integration = better API
  • Solving common workflow gaps = better shared features
  • Better deployment tools = shorter future rollouts

How to get FDE results without building a field army

Most organizations don’t need to build a permanent FDE department to benefit from the operating model. Many companies use software product development services for larger builds or forward deployed engineering services for work that needs engineers closer to the customer environment. Meaning that starting with a specialized engineering partner during the highest-risk phases of deployment, then transitioning ownership to internal teams as the solution stabilizes is an option.

Start with a clear business goal

Define the outcome before the engagement starts. It could be a production integration, an AI workflow that a specific team uses, a stable streaming release for several target devices, or a working connection between plant systems.

The contract should focus on what the team is expected to accomplish, not how many engineers are assigned to the project.

Use FDEs during the highest-risk stage

Some companies need embedded engineering most during discovery, integration, and launch. An FDE squad can handle those aspects of the project. But once the system is stable, you can move that ownership to an internal product or operations lead.

Set success and exit criteria early

Agree on what “working” means and how you’ll measure it. This should also tell you when engagement should end.

Build knowledge transfer into the work

Pair embedded FDE engineers with internal teams and document architectural decisions, integrations, operational procedures, and known risks throughout the project.

Organizations don’t adopt forward deployed engineering because they’re in a niche or highly regulated industry. They adopt it because software enters environments where requirements surface after deployment.

The industry simply determines what those requirements look like. Those operating conditions, not the industry itself, are the strongest indicator that the FDE model will create value.

FAQ

How much does a forward deployed engineer cost compared to a professional services engagement?

There is no single price difference. An FDE may cost more than a standard consultant because the role usually requires senior engineering skills and ownership of production work.

Should FDEs be billed as headcount, a fixed outcome, or bundled into the license?

A hybrid model often works well. This is where you pay for a defined team and timeframe, with clear outcomes, milestones, and exit criteria.

At what ACV or deal size does an FDE motion start to pay for itself?

There is no universal threshold. Look at the engineering cost as a share of the contract and the chance of expansion or renewal. The more work you can reuse, the more cost-effective the model.

Smaller engagements also justify FDE support when they establish a repeatable deployment model or open a strategically important market.

Which products justify an FDE model, and which don't?

FDEs are useful for enterprise products with complex integrations, operational workflows, high deployment risk, or a large gap between a demo and production use. For self-service products with standard onboarding and repeatable integrations, not so much.
