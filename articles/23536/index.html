FDE Delivery Handoff: How to Avoid Customer Operational Dependence

Original title: FDE 交付交接：如何避免客户运维依赖

FDE Times · 2026-11-14 · 6 min read

Summary

The article argues that the biggest lock-in risk in FDE work is operational dependency: customers own the deployed system but cannot run it themselves. Drawing on Cohere, Insight Partners, Databricks, and PostHog, it recommends designing every engagement for exit from day one, transferring operational tasks weekly, and feeding product gaps back to internal teams. A worked example shows tracking a metric of tasks only the FDE can do, dropping from six to zero over a 12-week project.

Key points

  • Cohere separates operational dependency from technical lock-in as the FDE-specific risk.
  • Insight Partners advises solving the hard problem, then leaving, with exit designed from the start.
  • Track the count of tasks only the FDE can perform, aiming for zero before handoff.
  • Capability building should span the whole project, not a final-week training session.
  • Product gaps should return to owning teams with context, benefiting future customers.

Key takeaways

  • Insight Partners recommends that every FDE project be designed for its ending from day one: solve the hard problem, then leave.

A practitioner quoted by Insight Partners set a seemingly paradoxical goal for their team: "literally work ourselves out of a job." In a profession built on close customer service, this is exactly what doing the job well looks like.

Many engineers' instincts run the opposite way. You're the only one who understands the entire data pipeline, and only you know why the prompts are written the way they are or how to rerun the evals. You feel valuable. Customers call you the moment something goes wrong, and every call feels like validation.

That's exactly the trap. For those doing or aspiring to do Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) work, the sooner you understand why being "irreplaceable" is a risk, the more it will shape how you design every project and how you talk about it in interviews.

FDE lock-in isn't in the code

When engineers talk about lock-in, they usually mean proprietary data formats or hard-to-replace APIs.

Cohere points to a different kind of lock-in that's closer to this line of work: "The lock-in risk more specific to FDEs is operational dependency." Cohere separates it from technical lock-in as its own category, so the way you check for it has to change too. The key question isn't where the code lives, but who knows how to operate it.

Cohere puts the consequence bluntly: an enterprise can own a deployment without the ability to operate it. The code is in the customer's repository, the infrastructure runs on the customer's cloud, and the contract states the customer is the owner.

Yet when the system breaks down at 2 a.m., the only person who knows how to fix it works at another company.

More importantly, Cohere doesn't see this as an unavoidable cost of the FDE model. Cohere writes: "Operational dependency is not an inevitable outcome of working with FDEs." It goes further: a vendor's deep involvement should make customers more independent, not more dependent.

In other words, dependency is the product of specific decisions made during the work, and different decisions can avoid it.

Design the exit from day one

Insight Partners compresses the advice into one sentence: "Solve the hard problem, then leave." In its view, every project should be designed for its ending from the start. Once the results are validated, ownership is handed to the team responsible for productizing and running the system at scale.

Jason Martin of Databricks describes this model in terms of the team's promise to customers: give us your hardest problems, we'll solve them, and then "the IP is yours, we'll hand it over to you."

That promise only means something if the customer also gains the ability to use that IP. Handing over a repository no one on the customer's side can understand is like handing over the keys to a car no one can drive.

That's why Cohere emphasizes timing. Building customer capability must run throughout the project, not be treated as a handover step at the last minute. Two hours of training in the final week rarely gets anyone ready to operate a system; weeks of working side by side can.

Count the tasks only you can do

Suppose there's a 12-week project: you build a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) system for the claims team at an insurance company to help them answer questions about policy terms.

The system has six routine operational tasks: ingesting new documents, rerunning the eval set, adjusting prompts when quality drops, monitoring costs, fixing failures when document formats change, and updating the list of test questions.

The first approach is to do all six yourself, because it's faster. By week 12, "tasks only the FDE can do" is still six out of six. You leave, and three weeks later the legal department changes the contract template, accuracy drops, and the customer's first instinct is to call the vendor.

The second approach is to ask the customer to assign an engineer to work alongside you starting in week two. From week three onward, each week one task shifts from "FDE does it, customer watches" to "customer does it, FDE watches." Start with low-risk work like document ingestion, and save the hardest one—fixing format-change failures—for week eight.

By week eight, that number becomes zero out of six. In the final four weeks, you mostly observe from the sidelines, revise the operational documentation, and handle the genuinely tricky cases.

Both approaches deliver an equally usable system; only the second delivers capability. Six out of six versus zero out of six is a number you can track weekly, put in status reports, and use to show customers how far they are from "no longer needing you."

Leaving the customer doesn't mean leaving the problem

Here's a legitimate concern: if customers no longer need you, where's the FDE's value? Rajkumar Irudayaraj of Alteryx flips the question. He believes an FDE should be "a closed loop for continuous company learning," not "a customer's permanent crutch."

After you leave, the value doesn't disappear—it moves. Every time you had to write a patch yourself because the product lacked a feature, that was a signal to the product team.

PostHog's FDE handbook requires handing such gaps back to the responsible team with sufficient context, because with sufficient context, fixes come faster, and the next customer never hits the same problem.

Back to the insurance example. If failures caused by contract format changes always require manual fixes, the right move isn't to teach the customer to patch things by hand—it's to file a ticket with the team responsible for document ingestion: which documents broke, how they broke, and how big the impact was.

Once the product can handle that situation, the fifth task on the list disappears for all customers at once.

Two approaches to the same project

Put the two approaches side by side, and the difference comes down to a few small decisions—the engineering capability is the same.

AspectThe "irreplaceable" FDEThe FDE who makes customers self-sufficient
Who operates the systemOnly the FDE knows how to run evals, adjust prompts, and handle failuresThe customer's engineers do the work while the FDE observes and revises documentation
When capability is builtOne handover meeting in the final weekThroughout the project, one task handed over each week
What the customer owns at the endThe deployment and the IP, but no ability to operate itThe deployment, the IP, and a team that can operate it
Product gapsHand-patched on site; only that customer benefitsHanded back to the responsible team with context; the next customer never encounters it
Mark of successThe customer calls the FDE the moment something goes wrongThe customer no longer needs to call

Nothing in the left column is technically wrong: the system runs, and the customer is happy in the short term. But every cell on the left is a debt that will eventually be paid—by you, by the colleague who takes over, or by the customer at the worst possible moment.

The story to tell in interviews

If you're preparing to move into an FDE role, look through your existing experience for a moment when you made yourself no longer needed.

Maybe it was a runbook you wrote that let the on-call team handle a certain class of failures on their own; maybe it was teaching another team to run the data pipeline you built.

Stories like these are far more persuasive than "only I could fix it."

On your resume, use concrete numbers to describe the handover's outcome, not just "deployed system X." For example: "Transferred all 6 operational tasks to the customer's 2-person engineering team; the customer ran evals independently from week 8 onward." That shows hiring managers you understand how a project should end.

When reading job postings, watch for terms like handover, enablement, or collaboration with product teams—they reveal how a company views the FDE role.

If you're already an FDE, the first thing to do at your next customer is to list the operational tasks in your first week and ask the customer who will take over each one. This question may sound like you're planning your own exit. In fact, it shows the customer that you're preparing them for the day they can operate independently.

The metric truly worth pursuing is leaving behind a team that can work on its own after you're gone; how often the customer calls you is the wrong way to keep score.
