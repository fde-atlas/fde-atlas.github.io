The forward deployed engineer interview is the hardest technical loop most candidates have never prepared for. Job postings for FDE roles jumped from 643 in April 2025 to 5,330 by April 2026, a 729% increase driven by the AI deployment gap between what companies buy and what they can actually use in production. The interview format is unlike anything a standard SWE prep track covers. Candidates who walk in expecting LeetCode walk out without an offer.

This guide covers the two distinct FDE interview formats, the round-by-round structure at Palantir, OpenAI, and Anthropic, and a specific walkthrough of the case study round that eliminates more than half of applicants.

Why the FDE Interview Is Nothing Like a Standard SWE Loop

FDE interviews test three things equally: technical depth, real-world deployment thinking, and client-facing communication. A standard SWE interview tests almost nothing outside of algorithms and system design in a vacuum. Every round in an FDE loop is oriented around a customer context, because the job itself exists entirely in customer context. The code you write at Palantir or OpenAI as an FDE runs in a client's production environment, not on internal infrastructure. That reality shapes every question in the loop.

The three skills being evaluated throughout:

  • Technical depth at the production level, not the competitive programming level. You need to know how to debug a RAG pipeline at 3am, not how to reverse a binary tree.
  • Deployment judgment under ambiguity. Given a half-described business problem, can you scope what is actually being asked before you propose anything?
  • Customer communication under pressure. Can you explain a technical tradeoff to a CFO who thinks AI is magic and a VP of Engineering who is skeptical it will work at all?

If you are just starting your FDE prep, the most valuable thing you can do before your loop is practice scoping ambiguous problems out loud. Customer-facing interview scenarios are not something standard SWE prep covers at all.

Two Types of FDE Interviews: Palantir-Style vs AI-Company Style

There are two meaningfully different FDE interview formats, and they require different preparation strategies.

Palantir-style (Palantir, Salesforce, enterprise data platforms): Built around raw decomposition and Palantir's Foundry toolchain. The Build-to-Apply take-home project asks you to solve an open-ended problem using Foundry tools. The Decomp round gives you a vague, large-scale enterprise data problem and times how you break it down. Python and SQL at a production level are non-negotiable. The technical bar is high on data pipeline architecture, and the ambiguity in the case is deliberately maximized.

AI-company style (OpenAI, Anthropic, ElevenLabs, Cohere): Adds customer discovery and values judgment on top of technical depth. OpenAI and Anthropic explicitly test whether you understand a customer's problem before you start proposing architecture. They also include rounds covering AI safety judgment and responsible deployment, which have no equivalent in a Palantir loop. The weighting is roughly 50% technical, 50% customer judgment.

The overlap: both formats reject candidates who jump to solutions before scoping. That single failure mode accounts for the majority of rejections across both styles.

The Palantir FDE Interview Structure

Palantir's FDE interview (titled FDSE, Forward Deployed Software Engineer) runs four to six weeks from first recruiter call to offer and includes five stages.

Stage 1 - Recruiter screen (30 min): Motivation for FDE specifically, not just Palantir. They want to hear why you want customer-embedded work versus a pure internal engineering role. Generic answers about impact do not move you forward.

Stage 2 - Build-to-Apply take-home: You are given access to Palantir Foundry and an open-ended business problem. You build a working solution and submit it with documentation. This is not a toy exercise. They are evaluating production-quality thinking, not a demo.

Stage 3 - Learning interview (45 min): The interviewer teaches you a concept you have never seen before and then asks you to apply it. This tests how you process new information under pressure, a core FDE skill because you are constantly learning client-specific systems on the fly.

Stage 4 - Decomp round (60 min): Palantir's signature round. You receive a vague, large-scale enterprise problem with intentionally missing information. The interviewer plays the role of a client stakeholder who does not give you everything you need upfront. The round has a roughly 40% pass rate and carries the most weight of any stage.

Stage 5 - Team fit interview: Behavioral round with the hiring manager and sometimes a senior FDSE. They are checking whether you will function well embedded in a client environment for weeks at a time.

The OpenAI and Anthropic FDE Interview Structure

OpenAI's FDE loop runs three to four hours in the virtual onsite and explicitly weights case studies, customer empathy, and business judgment at roughly 50% of the evaluation. The stages:

Recruiter screen (30 min): Why FDE, why OpenAI, what production AI work have you shipped. They are filtering for genuine deployment experience, not academic AI knowledge.

Take-home assessment (5 hours): Build a working application using OpenAI APIs and record a video walkthrough presenting it as if to a client. This is the first communication test. Engineers who cannot present a technical build clearly do not advance.

Technical screen (60 min): Defense of the take-home, plus additional questions on RAG architecture, LLM evaluation methodology, API rate limiting patterns, and production reliability. They ask things like "Walk me through how you would diagnose high latency in an LLM inference pipeline" and expect you to reason through token generation, network, preprocessing, and caching layers in sequence.

Solution Design round (60 min): You receive a business problem from a hypothetical enterprise customer. The test is whether you ask about the customer's actual goals, constraints, and success criteria before touching architecture. Candidates who jump to "I would use RAG with a Pinecone vector database" within the first three minutes fail this round even if that answer is technically correct.

Values and judgment round (45 min): No equivalent exists at Palantir. OpenAI asks about AI safety decisions in real deployment scenarios. Sample question: "A large healthcare customer wants to use the model to auto-generate clinical notes and present them to patients without physician review. How do you handle that?" They are testing your judgment on responsible deployment, not your knowledge of AI safety literature. There is no single correct answer, but there are wrong approaches.

Anthropic's Applied AI Engineer interview follows a similar structure with additional emphasis on Constitutional AI principles and safe deployment in regulated industries. Their process can run three months from first screen to offer.

The Case Study Round: Strong Approach vs What Gets You Rejected

The Decomp or case study round is the round with the lowest pass rate and the highest weight in every FDE loop. The format: a hypothetical client presents a large, vague problem, the information provided is intentionally incomplete, and you have 45 to 60 minutes to work through it with the interviewer playing the client.

Here is the same scenario handled two ways:

Scenario: A logistics company with 500 warehouse managers on regional systems wants to use AI to automate shipment rerouting. They have SAP data, real-time weather APIs, and GPS tracking.

Weak approach: "I would build a RAG pipeline ingesting SAP data and weather APIs, then use an LLM to generate rerouting recommendations that warehouse managers can approve or override. The stack would be Python, LangChain, and a Pinecone vector database for historical shipment data."

Why this fails: the candidate proposed an architecture before establishing what success looks like, who the actual decision-makers are, what the current failure rate of manual rerouting is, what the SLA requirements are, or whether the 500 regional systems even have compatible APIs. They answered a question that was not asked.

Strong approach: "Before I propose anything, I want to make sure I understand the problem correctly. A few questions: What is the current rerouting process and where does it break down? Is the goal to replace human decisions or to give warehouse managers better information? What does a successful reroute look like versus an unsuccessful one? Are the 500 regional systems on a shared API layer or are they all custom integrations?"

Then, after getting partial answers: "Based on what I am hearing, the biggest risk is that we build a recommendation engine that 500 managers ignore because they do not trust it. Before we touch any data pipelines, I would want to run a two-week observation study with three or four managers to understand how they currently make rerouting decisions and what information they actually rely on."

Why this works: the candidate identified the adoption risk, surfaced the data integration complexity before committing to it, and proposed a low-cost discovery step before the expensive build. This is exactly how an FDE behaves on a real customer engagement.

What FDE Interviewers Look for That Standard SWE Prep Misses

Three specific behaviors separate candidates who pass FDE loops from candidates who fail them, none of which appear in LeetCode or standard system design prep.

Narrate continuously: FDE interviewers cannot evaluate your thinking if you go silent. In the case study round, silence is interpreted as being stuck, not as careful thought. Experienced candidates narrate their uncertainty out loud: "I am not sure whether the data is clean enough for this approach, so I want to flag that as a risk before I commit to it."

Scope before solving: In every scenario or case round, the first action should be a clarifying question, not a proposed solution. The question does not need to be perfect. Asking "What does success look like for this deployment?" before anything else signals the right instinct.

Customer-first framing in technical discussions: When discussing architecture, FDE candidates frame technical choices in terms of customer outcomes, not technical elegance. "I would use batch processing here rather than streaming because the client's SLA is 24-hour windows, which means latency is not the constraint" is better than "streaming is more complex to maintain."

A 4-Week FDE Prep Plan

This plan assumes you have a backend engineering background and have been scheduled for an FDE loop in four to six weeks.

Week 1 - Company and customer fundamentals:

  • Read every public case study published by your target company. For Palantir, that means Airbus, BP, and NHS. For OpenAI, that means Morgan Stanley, Salesforce, and Klarna. Know the business problem, the solution, and the measurable outcome.
  • Practice explaining a complex technical system to a non-technical audience for 5 minutes without jargon. Record yourself and listen back.
  • Build a 60-second narrative covering: here is a problem I discovered in a customer environment, here is how I scoped it, here is what I built, here is the measurable outcome.

Week 2 - Production AI and deployment scenarios:

  • Build something small with your target company's APIs. For OpenAI, build a RAG application from scratch. For Palantir, get access to a Foundry trial and work through their documentation. You need real hands-on experience, not tutorial familiarity.
  • Practice explaining LLM evaluation methodology out loud. How do you know if an AI system is actually working? What metrics matter? What does failure look like in a production deployment?
  • Work through three to five ambiguous case scenarios. Time yourself. Force yourself to ask three clarifying questions before proposing anything.

Week 3 - Behavioral stories and STAR prep:

  • Write out eight STAR stories covering: handling ambiguity, influencing without authority, explaining a technical decision to a skeptical non-technical stakeholder, a deployment that failed and what you did, and a time you had to push back on a customer request.
  • Practice each story out loud at under 90 seconds. Record and cut anything that sounds like setup without payoff.
  • For AI-company loops specifically, prepare one story where you made a judgment call about responsible use of technology under pressure.

Week 4 - Full mock loops:

  • Run at least two full mock case study rounds with a partner playing an ambiguous, partially uncooperative client. Ask them to withhold key information until you ask the right question.
  • Run a full mock technical round covering RAG, API reliability patterns, and production debugging. Do not study LeetCode at this stage.
  • Read the OpenAI or Palantir engineering blog the week before your loop. Know what they are currently working on. Name it specifically in your interviews.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a forward deployed engineer interview and a software engineer interview?

FDE interviews test three things equally: technical depth, real-world deployment thinking, and client-facing communication. A standard technical interview focuses almost entirely on algorithms and data structures in isolation. Standard SWE interviews focus almost entirely on algorithms and data structures. The FDE case study round, where you receive a vague business problem with missing information and must clarify before proposing anything, has no equivalent in a standard SWE loop.

Does the Palantir FDE interview include LeetCode questions?

Not really. Palantir tests Python and SQL in a production context, not LeetCode-style algorithm questions. Their Build-to-Apply take-home uses Foundry tools, and the Decomp round tests how you break down an ambiguous enterprise problem, not whether you can reverse a linked list in under 20 minutes.

How is the OpenAI FDE interview different from Palantir?

OpenAI weights customer discovery and values judgment much more heavily than Palantir. Their Solution Design round explicitly tests whether you understand the customer problem before proposing architecture. They also include a values round covering AI safety judgment, which has no Palantir equivalent. Palantir focuses more on raw decomposition and their Foundry toolchain.

What is the most common reason FDE candidates get rejected?

Jumping to a solution before scoping the problem. In the case study and customer discovery rounds, interviewers are watching whether you ask clarifying questions first. Candidates who launch directly into a technical proposal without asking about business goals, constraints, and success criteria fail even if their proposal is technically sound.

How long should I prepare for a forward deployed engineer interview?

Four to six weeks of focused preparation is the realistic timeline for most engineers. The first two weeks cover the company and customer case fundamentals. Weeks three and four focus on production AI systems and behavioral stories. The final stretch is mock case study practice with a partner who plays an ambiguous, uncooperative client.
