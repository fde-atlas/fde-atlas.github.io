The difference between FDEs and professional services: what is a Forward Deployed Engineer?

Original title: FDE 和专业服务的区别：前线部署工程师是什么

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 6 min read

Summary

This article argues that the real distinction between FDE work and professional services lies in where the custom code ends up, not in job titles. Code that flows back into the core product makes it FDE work; code that stays with the client on project billing is services. Drawing on a16z, Tandem, Thomas Otter, Valletta Software, and PostHog's handbook, it frames customization as product discovery, warns that missing generalization discipline leads to dozens of unmaintainable forks, and explains why the feedback loop often breaks. It closes with practical advice for engineers evaluating FDE roles and describing their experience.

Key points

  • The test is code destination: back to core product means FDE; staying with the client means services.
  • a16z argues differentiation comes largely from deployment, making customization a product discovery mechanism.
  • Without an owner for generalization, vendors accumulate dozens of forks they misleadingly call a platform.
  • The feedback loop breaks because summarizing learnings competes with delivery, and delivery always wins.
  • Job seekers should check reporting lines in JDs and ask interviewers for concrete examples of shared features.

Key takeaways

  • Short answer: work done at a customer site is FDE work if it flows back into the core product; it is professional services if it stays with the customer and is billed by the project.
  • Customization itself is normal: a16z argues that most of a product's differentiation comes from how it is deployed. The real danger is independent code branches piling up.
  • Summarizing lessons competes with delivery for time, so the feedback loop often breaks down. That's why you need to ask clearly: who is responsible for turning custom code into shared code.

In the FDE vs. professional services debate, the short answer lies in where the code goes, not in the job title. If what you write at a customer site gets refined and flows back into the core product, you're doing FDE work; if it stays with the customer and is billed by the project, it's professional services, no matter what your business card says.

This question matters because FDE is a hot role. Semafor notes that the concept was popularized by Palantir: an engineer embedded within a single customer. Semafor also reports that people with the relevant skills are being courted by AI startups.

Yet the "embedded at the customer" model is exactly what raises suspicion: if you have to send people on-site, is the product unfinished? For developers considering a move into FDE work, the answer to this question determines whether the next three years teach you to build products, or just to do projects under a trendier title.

Is a product that needs dedicated people to install a defective product?

In analyzing "trading lower gross margin for long-term competitive advantage," a16z opens with a striking metaphor: enterprises buying AI are like a grandma who received an iPhone. She wants to use it, but needs you to set it up for her. a16z argues this is precisely why heavily service-oriented deployment models are so common.

a16z goes further, proposing that most of a product's differentiation comes from how the same underlying technology is deployed. In other words, customization is not waste—it's where the product creates value.

Tandem puts it even more bluntly when describing how its FDE team collaborates with product and engineering: customization is not a service cost to be cut, but a product discovery mechanism. Viewed this way, there's nothing shameful about being "unfinished." The right question is: after each trip to the front line, is the product one step closer to being "done"?

The downside: forty code branches, called a "platform"

Skeptics don't deny the value of on-site work. Thomas Otter draws the line by who pays: if the customer bears the FDE's costs, they are working on projects, not products. In Otter's view, building for just one customer is project work.

Otter cites Marty Cagan on the long-term cost: thousands of large custom solutions, each of which must be maintained and improved forever. Every fork is a debt with no due date.

Valletta Software describes what happens when no one owns generalization: the vendor ends up maintaining forty custom forks and calls the whole pile a platform. This detail is worth remembering, because it shows that no one deliberately slides into becoming a services company. It happens because no one is assigned to stop it.

FDE vs. professional services: step four decides

Valletta describes FDE work as a series of steps and points out where it diverges from consulting: at step four, the FDE must flow patterns back into the core product. Consultants are done when delivery is complete; for FDEs, the hardest part comes after delivery.

In a July 2026 Substack post, Shivanath warned that building for a single customer easily leads to overfitting. In his view, it is precisely the discipline of flowing work back that makes embedding engineers at customers a product business rather than a labor business.

PostHog's public FDE handbook describes exactly the ideal outcome: patterns that recur across multiple deployments get distilled into reusable components, skills, and product improvements. That's the compounding effect of the feedback loop.

Imagine a startup selling an invoice-recognition AI agent to enterprises, with its first three customers. At the first customer, the FDE writes a converter for that customer's ERP format, notes which parts are customer-specific, then extracts the generic file-reading portion into a connector in the product. By the third customer, if it uses a similar ERP, the integration might require nothing more than configuration.

A services team called FDE would take a different path: three customers, three code branches, each branch understood by only one person. The customers are still satisfied, the invoices still go out, but the tenth customer takes nearly as much effort as the first. Scale that scenario over a few years, and it's exactly the forty forks Valletta describes.

Why does the feedback loop so often break down?

If the test is this simple, why do so many teams still slide? Tandem offers the most practical explanation: distilling front-line lessons is real work, and it competes with delivery for time—and delivery always wins.

Customers have launch deadlines, but no one calls to chase the core product. So if step four is just a vague expectation, with no owner and no dedicated time, it gets pushed to next week, and then the week after.

a16z's financial argument also explains why companies are willing to take this risk. It argues that the only thing a company should optimize right now is growing total gross profit as fast as possible, even if services drag down the gross margin.

That math only works if today's services become tomorrow's products; otherwise, the company is just trading headcount for growth.

Three questions career-switchers often ask

Is an FDE just a consultant who can code? Not if the company genuinely does step four. The on-site work is the same; the difference is whether the code makes it back into the core product.

Can I do professional services first, then move to FDE? On-site experience is a great foundation. What you need to add is the habit of spotting things that recur across customers and turning them into shared assets—that is precisely the definition of the FDE role.

How do I tell whether a company is hiring a real FDE? Ask clearly who is responsible for generalization, and whether there is dedicated time for it. Delivery always wins; a feedback loop with no owner usually exists only on slides.

Read a job description like an org chart

Look for two things in a job description (JD). First, who the role reports to: product, engineering, or services. Second, whether it mentions contributing back to the core product, writing connectors, or shared modules. A JD that only talks about "deployment," "customer support," and "ensuring launch" is usually describing a services role.

In interviews, ask specifically: in the FDE's recent work at a customer, which part became a shared feature, and who merged it? If the interviewer can't give a concrete example, that's a signal worth noting.

On your resume, don't just write "completed deployment for customer X." Tell it in step-four order: what you built specifically for the customer, what you noticed recurring, and how you turned it into something other teams could reuse. Framed this way, the recruiter can see you understand the difference between products and projects.

The debate among investors will ultimately be settled by growth data. For engineers, the signal comes much earlier: open your git log and check whether the code you wrote at a customer site last month is now in the product, or still sitting on that customer's own branch.
