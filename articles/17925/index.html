What Is Contextual Inquiry: On-Site Observation Methods for FDE Requirements Research

Original title: 情境调查是什么：FDE 需求调研现场观察方法

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide argues that requirements interviews capture only the process users believe they follow, while reasoning, motivations, and unnoticed habits are lost—precisely where AI agents fail in production. It introduces contextual inquiry, an ethnographic method where FDEs sit beside end users as apprentices, observing a normal workday and logging exceptions and workarounds in a four-column template, then asking questions only during breaks. Using a logistics complaint-handling example, it shows how observed details (external spreadsheets, carrier websites, human escalation) never appear in the four-step flow diagram. It also warns against interrupting users, acting as tech support, and observing only team leads.

Key points

  • Users describe idealized processes; real reasoning and habits only surface through observation.
  • Adopt an apprentice role: observe quietly, note exceptions, ask questions at breaks.
  • A four-column log links observations to questions and design impacts.
  • Avoid interrupting, rescuing stuck users, and shadowing only team leads.
  • Compare observation notes with interview findings to refine agent design.

People describe the process they believe they follow; only by sitting beside them can you spot habits they themselves are unaware of—and it's precisely in those places that agents go wrong.

Key Takeaways

  • When users describe a process, the reasoning, motivations, and mental models behind it get lost; only by watching them actually work can you discover these
  • Show up as an apprentice: observe quietly, note exceptions and workarounds, and ask questions only when the user pauses
  • Don't become the user's tech support, or you'll mask the very difficulties you're trying to find

You've just finished a requirements interview with the head of operations at a logistics company. The process has been mapped out clearly: complaint emails come in, an employee reads them, selects an incident type in the system, and forwards them to the responsible team. Four steps in total, and your task is to build an agent to automate them.

This diagram is almost certainly incomplete. Nobody is lying to you; people are simply describing the process they believe they follow. The head of forward deployed engineering at OpenAI wrote on Pragmatic Engineer that what customers describe in requirements interviews often doesn't match the reality of frontline data and systems.

For a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), this gap isn't just a UX problem. If the agent is designed around this four-step diagram, it will fail exactly at the points the diagram omits. This article covers the skill for bridging that gap: spending a day beside the user, and observing the right way.

Why do descriptions always look tidier than the actual work?

The reason is simple. Nielsen Norman Group (NN/g) points out that when people recall and summarize their workflows, the reasoning, motivations, and mental models behind them are lost in the summary.

What remains are the steps; the reasons are gone. That's why UserTesting notes that users' self-reported behavior doesn't always match what you actually observe them doing.

No amount of careful questioning can bridge this gap. NN/g argues that real work contexts also reveal behaviors users themselves are unaware of—and you can't ask someone about a habit they don't know they have.

This is also why Paraform, in describing the FDE role, says some constraints only emerge when you see people actually using software.

The practical conclusion: FDEs must show up where the work happens. A Palantir FDE told Pragmatic Engineer that in most weeks they spend several days working on-site at customers.

Show up as an apprentice, not an interviewer

NN/g defines contextual inquiry as a form of ethnographic field research that combines deep observation with interviews. It uses the master-apprentice metaphor to describe the relationship: the master teaches the craft by doing, and the apprentice learns by watching.

Once you take the apprentice's position, the way you behave on-site changes. An interviewer asks questions and waits for answers. An apprentice watches the master's hands, notices where they hesitate and which extra windows they open, and only then asks: "Why did you just do that?" The user no longer needs to put on any show; they just work as usual.

Example: a day on the complaints team

Back to the logistics company. Imagine you sit behind Lan, a complaints specialist, starting at 9 a.m. You don't ask anything; you just take notes in a four-column table. Here's a hypothetical excerpt from that log:

TimeWhat you sawQuestions to ask laterDesign implications
9:12Before selecting the incident type, she opens an Excel file on her second monitor to look up the customer's nameWhat is this file, and who updates it?There's a key-account list outside the system; the agent needs to be able to read it
9:40She copies the tracking number to the carrier's website to check it, rather than checking the internal systemWhy not check the status in the system?Internal data may be stale; before making the agent depend on it, verify it updates fast enough
10:05She hits an ambiguous email and pastes it into the team chat to ask a colleagueWho usually decides in cases like this?There must be a path to hand off to a human; don't force the agent to classify every email on its own
10:30An email describing two incidents at once; she selects "Other"How should emails involving multiple incidents be handled?The current data model assumes each email has only one label

Not a single one of these lines appears in the four-step diagram, but any one of them could break the agent in its first week in production. Notably, Lan isn't hiding anything. To her, these are just "how the work gets done"—not worth mentioning.

When she takes a break, you pull out the "Questions to ask later" column. Only then does the interview begin, and every question is anchored to something that just happened, rather than vague recollection.

Steps to do it yourself

Prepare. Ask to observe an ordinary workday, not a demo. Explain that you're there to learn how they work, not to evaluate them. Bring the four-column log template above.

Watch both routine work and exceptions. AI Engineer's FDE knowledge base recommends that alongside observing routine work, you also observe exceptions with real consequences and informal workarounds. Routine work shows you the ideal path; exceptions and shortcuts show you what the real system is missing.

When a description sounds too smooth, ask for an example. The same source suggests that when a process is described too smoothly, ask the person for a recent example. You might phrase it like this: "When was the last time this came up? Could you open it and show me?" Concrete examples pull people from the idealized process back to what actually happens.

Cross-check against the requirements interview. That evening, compare the log with the requirements documents. Every discrepancy is either a question to bring to decision-makers or a change to the design.

Mistakes that make observation worthless

The most common mistake is interrupting. AI Engineer notes that asking questions while the user works changes their behavior. After being asked "Why did you open that file?", Lan might start "following the rules" just for your benefit. So note the time and save the questions for later.

The second mistake is harder for engineers to avoid: seeing a user struggle and immediately wanting to help. The same source warns that once you become the user's tech support, you may mask the very difficulties you set out to find.

Those three minutes of Lan fumbling on the carrier's website are valuable data. Teach her a shortcut, and that data is gone.

The third mistake is observing only the team lead. The person who runs the requirements interview is usually not the one doing the work every day. Ask to sit next to the end user, as close to the front line as possible.

How does this skill show up on a resume?

When reading FDE job descriptions, watch for phrases like "work on-site with customers" or "collaborate directly with end users." Paraform describes the feedback loop between FDEs and end users as face-to-face. What these job postings implicitly require is observation skills.

On your resume or in interviews, don't write "strong customer communication skills." Tell a specific story: who you observed, for how long, which informal workaround you discovered, and what design change it led to.

A story like that shows recruiters you can dig out real requirements, not just execute the ones handed to you.

The four-step diagram from the requirements interview is still useful: it's your first hypothesis. Spending a day beside Lan is how you test that hypothesis before the code goes to production to be tested for real.
