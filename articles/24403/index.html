In-House AI Development or Outsource to a Development Company? | Criteria for Choosing Between Contracted Development and In-House Development

Original title: AI開発は内製か、開発会社に頼むか｜受託開発・内製化の判断基準

FDE Consulting · 2026-07-19 · 11 min read

Summary

This article argues that whether to build AI systems in-house or outsource to a development company should not be a one-time company-wide decision, but made per project using five questions: competitive importance, post-launch maintenance frequency, undocumented business rules, data sensitivity, and availability of internal talent. Citing IPA's DX Trends 2025 survey, it notes 37.8% of Japanese firms outsource core-system development versus 30.6% in the US, and 82.3% of firms pursuing insourcing cite talent shortages. The author outlines three approaches: in-house, outsourced, or co-building with a vendor then transferring to internal teams. Since AI performance is hard to guarantee upfront, contracts typically use quasi-mandate (junin) structures across PoC, development, and maintenance phases. Before commissioning, buyers must define metrics, AI scope, data permissions, acceptance criteria, post-launch ownership, and deliverables. For eventual insourcing, transfer conditions should be written into the initial contract and progressed in three state-based stages.

Key points

  • Insourcing vs. outsourcing should be decided per project via five questions, not once company-wide.
  • IPA's 2025 survey: Japanese firms outsource core development more than US firms; talent is the top insourcing barrier.
  • Three approaches exist; co-building with a vendor and transferring internally is a distinct option.
  • AI performance is hard to guarantee upfront, so contracts favor quasi-mandate structures in staged phases.
  • Buyers must predefine metrics, data scope, acceptance criteria, and handover items before commissioning.

Once you decide to incorporate AI into your operations, the next question that arises is "should we build it in-house or hire an AI development company?" Few people in-house can build it. But if you outsource, the know-how of how it was built doesn't stay in-house. There are reasons on both sides, so the discussion tends to go in circles.

In my view, this is not a question a company decides only once. You decide it for each thing you build, using five questions. On that basis, for the parts you outsource to a development company, decide the contract form and what you will take over in advance, and for parts you eventually want to move in-house, write the conditions for transferring them into the initial contract. Below, I organize this in order: the current state of in-house development at Japanese companies, three options for how to proceed, the questions for deciding, contracts for outsourced development, what to decide before commissioning, questions to ask development companies, and how to transition to in-house development.

The requirements to decide before building are covered separately in Requirements Definition for AI Development, and the breakdown of costs in Cost Breakdown of AI Development. The job types of engineers who build AI on the ground in business operations are summarized in What is an FDE?.

Japanese companies' system development still centers on outsourcing

IPA's (Information-technology Promotion Agency) "DX Trends 2025" is a survey of companies in Japan, the US, and Germany conducted from early February to late March 2025. The figures cover system development as a whole, not just AI, but they serve as a starting point for considering in-house development versus outsourcing.

The source is Figures 2-13 and 2-14 of IPA's "DX Trends 2025," with 1,480 to 1,500 responding Japanese companies and 509 US companies. In Japan, outsourced development is the most common even for areas central to the business. Meanwhile, the challenges cited by Japanese companies pursuing in-housing are as follows.

Challenges in pursuing in-housing (Japan, 334 companies pursuing in-housing)Response rate

In my view, AI development hits the top two challenges simultaneously. Few people can build it, and the models and tools used change rapidly. It is also worth noting that the third challenge, "judgment is difficult," exceeds 30%. In-housing does not advance merely by declaring a policy; you need to decide what criteria to use to divide work between in-house and outsourcing. The fourth challenge, "transition is difficult," shows the difficulty of later moving externally built systems in-house. This point is covered in the final section.

There are three ways to proceed with AI development

Thinking of it as a binary choice between in-house and outsourcing narrows your options. In my framework, there are three ways to proceed.

ApproachWho builds itMain contractWhat remains in-house when finishedSuited forCommon stumbling blocks
In-houseEmployeesNone (employment)The system and the capability to buildCore of the business, frequently revised after launchCannot start until people who can build it are assembled
Outsourced development to an AI development companyAI development companyContract for work (ukeoi) or quasi-mandate (jun'inin)Whatever the contract specifies will be handed overThings with easily defined specifications and few changes after launchEvery revision requires a new estimate and contract
Building together with a development company and moving in-houseDevelopment company and employeesMainly quasi-mandateThe system and the building capability for the transferred portionCore of the business, but no one in-house can build it yetOutsourcing drags on if transfer conditions are not set

In my view, systems using generative AI rarely end once built. Prompt tuning, adding evaluation data, and adapting to model updates continue after launch. So the core of the in-house versus outsourcing debate is not so much who builds it initially, but who keeps maintaining it after launch.

The third approach is not a compromise for when you cannot choose between in-house and outsourced development. It is an independent option: borrow building capability from outside while cultivating the capability to keep maintaining it in-house.

Five questions for deciding between in-house and a development company

The decision is made not at the company level but for each thing you build. Even within the same company, it is fine to outsource an AI that answers internal inquiries while building in-house an AI that supports decisions at the core of the business. Answering the following five questions reveals which way to lean. The table is my own framework.

QuestionAnswer leaning toward in-houseAnswer allowing outsourcing to a development company
1. Is it a source of competitive advantage for the business?Directly tied to how your company winsWork that would look the same at any company
2. How often will it be revised after launch?Revised continuously on a weekly or monthly basisLargely unchanged once built
3. Are there many exceptions and unwritten rules in the work?Many judgments known only to the people in chargeProcedures are documented
4. Does it use data that is hard to share externally?Hard to define what can be passed outsideThe scope of what is shared and its use can be set by contract
5. Are there people in-house who can build and maintain it?Yes, or there is a prospect of hiring themNo, and they cannot be hired right away

Questions 1 and 2 measure the reasons to lean toward in-house, and 5 measures whether in-housing is feasible. If 1 and 2 point toward in-house but the answer to 5 is "no," the third approach—building with a development company while moving it in-house—becomes a candidate.

Question 3 measures how hard it will be to convey the work when outsourcing. Work with many exceptions and unwritten rules cannot be fully captured in a requirements specification. If you outsource, you need a setup where the builders can talk directly with the people doing the work.

Question 4 does not directly decide between in-house and outsourcing. If you share externally, you can define the scope of use of the shared data in the contract. On February 18, 2025, METI (the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry) published the "Contract Checklist for AI Use and Development." It summarizes the points users of AI services should verify at contract time, and also explains the basic knowledge needed to consider the scope of use of provided data and the conditions for using AI-generated outputs.

Question 5 is precisely the personnel issue that ranked first among in-housing challenges in the IPA survey. If you decide on in-house development assuming people you do not yet have, nothing starts until the hiring is done.

In outsourced AI development, the substance differs depending on whether it is a contract for work or a quasi-mandate

Once you decide to hire an AI development company, the next question is the form of the contract. Under the Civil Code, a contract for work (ukeoi) is formed when "one party promises to complete a certain work, and the other party promises to pay remuneration in exchange for the results of that work" (Article 632). A quasi-mandate (jun'inin) is the delegation of affairs that are not legal acts (Article 656), and the party accepting it bears the duty to handle the affairs with the care of a good manager (Article 644). Under a contract for work, you pay for the completion of the work; under a quasi-mandate, you pay for the professional doing the work itself. However, even under a quasi-mandate, it is possible to agree to pay remuneration based on results (Article 648-2).

This distinction matters in AI development because it is hard to promise performance before building. The "Contract Guidelines on the Utilization of AI and Data" published by METI on June 15, 2018, recognizing that the content and performance of trained models often cannot be specified at the time of contracting, advocated "exploratory, phased" development divided into assessment, PoC, development, and additional training stages. In its model contracts, both the introduction verification contract for the PoC stage and the software development contract for the development stage are quasi-mandate type.

The "Information System Model Transaction and Contract (Agile Development Version)" published by IPA on March 31, 2020, is also premised on quasi-mandate rather than contract for work, so that it can flexibly accommodate added features and changed priorities.

METI's guidelines were written with machine learning in mind and do not directly anticipate today's development, where off-the-shelf large language models are incorporated into business operations. Even so, in my view, the points that outputs are not necessarily the same every time and that you cannot know whether they will reach the passing bar until you build are shared, so the idea of contracting in stages can be used as is.

StageWhat to verifyContract that fits easilyWhat the ordering side decides
Try (PoC)Whether it reaches the passing bar with your business dataQuasi-mandateThe passing bar and evaluation data, conditions for moving to the next stage
BuildWhether it works under production permissions and exception handlingQuasi-mandate; contract for work also possible for finalized specificationsWhat you take over, frequency of progress reports
Keep fixingWhether output quality is maintainedQuasi-mandateWho evaluates and when to review

The table is my own organization. Since the form of contract varies with individual circumstances, please verify the actual contract with your legal staff or a lawyer.

What to decide before requesting an AI development company

Before requesting an AI development company, there are things the ordering side must decide. If you request with these left blank, the development company will fill them in as placeholders, and those placeholders become the requirements. The table is my own organization.

Write what you take over into the contract. METI's model contracts also show a framework for adjusting the interests of the ordering side and the development side by specifying not only the attribution of rights to deliverables but also detailed terms of use. Questions to check when choosing an AI development company

"AI development company" covers a range: companies that broadly take on system development, development companies specializing in AI, and the implementation departments of companies selling AI products, each with different strengths. Rather than choosing by company type, it is faster to judge by how they answer the following questions. The table is my own organization.

QuestionWhat you want to verify
How far into the building process is the person who made the proposal involvedWhether information is lost between proposal and implementation
What is the longest-running thing in production, not a prototype, and for how many monthsWhether they have experience running things in production
How and when do you verify output qualityWhether they assume building an evaluation mechanism
Will data entrusted to you be used for other purposes or AI trainingWhether the scope of data use is set by contract
What will you hand over when the contract endsWhether things can be fixed without the development company
If transferring in-house, in what order will the primary responsibility be transferredWhether they have a plan for internalization

For every question, look at whether the answer is concrete. If a question only gets answers like "We will respond flexibly to your needs," that is a point to nail down before the contract. Criteria for comparing companies that call themselves FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers) who build on-site are covered in more detail in How to Choose an FDE Support Company.

If aiming for internalization, write the transfer conditions into the first contract

According to an IPA survey, 19.5% of Japanese companies pursuing internalization cited "difficulty migrating externally developed systems to internal development" as a challenge. Moving something built by an outside party into the company later is not easy. So for things you will eventually want to internalize, build the premise of transfer into the first contract.

In my view, the transfer is easier to manage if divided into the following three stages.

  • The development company builds, and employees participate through reviews and providing business knowledge. Employees handle evaluation data creation from the start.
  • Build together. Transfer primary implementation responsibility to employees part by part, and place the evaluation and operation mechanisms in-house.
  • Employees build, and the development company steps back to reviews and support on difficult parts. Expansion to new business operations starts in-house.

Set the conditions for advancing stages by state, not by duration. For example, make "employees alone can update evaluation data and deploy prompt changes to production" the condition for ending stage two. If this condition is not written into the contract or plan, the support meant to be joint building becomes merely prolonged outsourcing.

The form of support where external experts build together with the in-house team and leave behind a state that runs in-house afterward is summarized in What Is AI Accompaniment Support, and how to develop in-house AI talent in Developing AI Talent.

If you are torn between internal development and hiring an AI development company, first pick one thing you want to build and try answering the five questions. Where your answers split is what to nail down in a consultation.
