Many people confuse these three. Let's start by clearly defining "what each one refers to."

MVP (Minimum Viable Product) refers to "a product with the minimum features needed to deliver value to customers." In Japanese, it is translated as "practically minimal product."

The person who popularized the MVP concept is Eric Ries, author of "The Lean Startup." He defines MVP as "a product that allows one full cycle of the Build-Measure-Learn feedback loop with minimal effort."

The essence of MVP is not "creating a perfect product" but "validating hypotheses as quickly as possible." It is a means of testing on a small scale "whether there is actually a need for this product" before investing significant cost and time into a product that doesn't yet exist in the market.

MVP is often confused with "prototypes," but the two are fundamentally different (explained in detail in section 5-1).

Agile Development is a general term for "development methods and philosophies that repeat small development cycles at high speed, unit by unit of functionality." "Agile" in English means "nimble" or "quick."

The origin of this philosophy lies in the "Agile Manifesto," published in 2001. This manifesto presents the following four values:

  • Value individuals and interactions over processes and tools
  • Value working software over comprehensive documentation
  • Value customer collaboration over contract negotiation
  • Value responding to change over following a plan

Agile development is not "a single methodology" but a general term for methodologies based on these values. Representative methods include Scrum, XP (Extreme Programming), and Kanban.

Related article: What's the Difference Between Agile Development and Scrum Development? 5 Benefits and Development Steps Explained

Lean Startup is a management methodology that grows a product while minimizing waste by repeating the cycle of "hypothesis → build → measure → learn" in small, fast iterations. Eric Ries systematized it in his 2011 book.

"Lean" means "without excess fat, streamlined," and its origins lie in the Toyota Production System (Lean Production System). It aims to thoroughly eliminate waste and generate maximum value with the minimum necessary resources.

The core of Lean Startup is the feedback loop of "Build-Measure-Learn."

  • Build: Create an MVP based on a hypothesis
  • Measure: Measure user behavior data and reactions
  • Learn: Learn from the data whether the hypothesis was correct
  • Pivot or Persevere: Decide the next action based on what was learned

Running this cycle at high speed is the essence of Lean Startup.

While the three concepts are interrelated, each operates at a different "layer." The following comparison table organizes this.

The key takeaway from this table is that "the three are not competing or alternative approaches; because they operate at different layers, they can (and indeed should) be used together."

With the definitions in place, the next step is to understand how the three relate to each other and work in combination.

The simplest way to represent the relationship among the three is as the following hierarchical (layered) structure.

[Strategy Layer] Lean Startup

↓ Decides "which hypothesis to validate"

[Tactics Layer] Agile Development

↓ Decides "how to develop at high speed"

[Means Layer] MVP

↓ Concretizes "what to build and validate"

  • Lean Startup (Strategy): Determines the overall direction of the business and product, such as "what should be validated now" and "which hypothesis is most important."
  • Agile Development (Tactics): Following that direction, it determines how development proceeds, such as "what to build in which sprint" and "which team to work with."
  • MVP (Means): Concretely defines "what to build" within a sprint and delivers it to users as fast as possible.

When these three layers mesh together, the ideal form of development—building "the right thing, the right way, fast"—is achieved.

The Lean Startup's "Build-Measure-Learn cycle" and Agile's "sprint" differ in cycle granularity and purpose, but they are connected as follows.

Sprint (Agile's tactical cycle)

  • Duration: 1–4 weeks
  • Purpose: Complete a feature-level product in a working state
  • Output: Working software (a releasable increment)

Build-Measure-Learn Cycle (Lean's strategic cycle)

  • Duration: Several sprints to several months
  • Purpose: Validating business hypotheses and learning (Pivot or Persevere decisions)
  • Output: Validation data and the next hypothesis

In other words, it is a nested structure in which multiple sprints accumulate to complete one Build-Measure-Learn cycle. You assemble the MVP in a sprint, release it, collect data, and use the Lean cycle to judge "is this product headed in the right direction?"—this flow is the ideal way to combine them.

In modern DX and new business development, a four-layer model that adds Design Thinking to the three above is put into practice.

LayerFrameworkQuestion
User understanding layerDesign Thinking"What are users really struggling with?"
Strategy layerLean Startup"Which hypothesis should be validated?"
Tactics layerAgile Development"How can we develop at high speed?"
Means layerMVP"What should we build minimally?"

Design Thinking functions upstream of the Lean Startup's "hypothesis setting" phase. It uncovers "essential pain points (problems)" through user interviews and observation, on top of which Lean Startup hypotheses are formulated and MVPs are developed with Agile—this flow is the most reliable approach for reducing failure rates in the field.

Now that the theory is understood, let's bring it into the context of DX initiatives and walk through the practical steps.

The DX promotion advocated by METI (the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry) calls for "transforming existing operations and business models through digital technology to create new value." A traditional waterfall approach fails here for the following reasons.

  • Requirements keep changing: DX projects have no visible "correct answer" from the start
  • Technology evolves rapidly: There is a risk that a system built over three years becomes obsolete by the time it is released
  • Market response is unpredictable: You cannot know users' real needs until they actually use the product

The combination of MVP × Agile × Lean Startup is a powerful weapon for dealing with this "uncertainty." Build small, measure, and learn—this cycle is the compass for DX projects.

Let's walk through a practical approach for DX promotion projects, step by step.

Step 1: Setting the Problem Hypothesis (Design Thinking × Lean)

  • Identify pain points (problems) through user interviews and field observation
  • Define "whose problem, what problem, and how to solve it" in a single sentence
  • Visualize the entire business model on one page using a Lean Canvas

Step 2: Defining the MVP (Lean × MVP)

  • Re-examine "what is the minimum set of features needed to validate this hypothesis?"
  • Create a feature list and strip away all "nice-to-have" features
  • Determine the minimum unit that answers the question "will users feel value from just this?"

Step 3: Developing the MVP in Sprints (Agile × MVP)

  • Divide development into 1–2 week sprints to build the MVP
  • Demo the working product at the end of each sprint and review it as a team
  • Conduct early user testing (alpha testing) to obtain real feedback

Step 4: Measurement and Learning (Lean)

  • Define KPIs in advance (e.g., retention rate, task completion rate, NPS) and measure the data
  • Analyze "was the hypothesis correct?" and "which pain points were truly serious?"
  • Decide whether to pivot (change direction) or persevere (continue)

Step 5: On to the Next Sprint (Agile × Lean)

  • Reflect learning results back into the backlog
  • Re-prioritize and enter the next sprint
  • By continuing this cycle, the product achieves market fit

Airbnb

Before developing the service, Airbnb's founders validated their market hypothesis with an extremely rough MVP: "Let strangers stay in our apartment and charge them for it." Before building a full-scale system, they confirmed demand through a manually operated MVP.

Dropbox

Before actually developing the service, Dropbox used a working demo video (a promotional video) as an MVP to validate the hypothesis that "it would be convenient to sync files in the cloud." With just a single video, they gained tens of thousands of waitlist sign-ups, justifying the development investment.

Domestic Case: DX at a Housing-Related Manufacturer

A housing-related parts manufacturer carried out a DX project to replace its traditional CD-based parts catalog with a web system. Using agile development, they split the project into a first phase for basic features and a second phase for extended features, achieving service launch in just six months.

What this change means is that "hypothesis-testing cycles can now run 2 to 5 times faster than before." Even with one-week agile sprints, we are now in an era where multiple MVPs can be tried within a single sprint.

Generative AI is also helping resolve "PoC fatigue" (the problem of PoCs being mass-produced but never going to production), which is common among Japanese companies. By leveraging generative AI, the cost of moving from PoC to production has dropped, making it realistic to deliver "something that works for now" to users early.

Beyond "developing with AI," cases of "developing products with AI features as an MVP" are rapidly increasing. In such cases, there are points to note that differ from ordinary MVP development.

Point 1: Prioritize "UX" over "accuracy"

An MVP of a generative AI product can succeed even if the AI's answer accuracy is not 100%. UX design that makes users feel it is "easy to use" and "valuable" is more important in early validation. Improving accuracy is work that should be done after achieving PMF (product-market fit).

Point 2: Design for hallucination (misinformation generation) risk

Generative AI can produce misinformation (hallucinations). Even at the MVP stage, risk design is needed, such as "clearly indicating that responses come from AI" and "designing so that AI output is not used directly for important decisions."

Point 3: Cost structure changes with scale

LLM APIs are billed by token. While this is no problem with few users at the MVP stage, cost design for scaling is necessary. We recommend measuring "API cost per user" from the early MVP stage.

Point 4: Privacy issues with training data

If user data is used for AI model training, you must verify compliance with personal information protection laws and GDPR. Incorporating legal perspectives into the initial MVP design minimizes later risks.

Even if you understand the framework, various pitfalls await in real-world practice. This section explains common failure patterns and how to address them.

One of the biggest causes of MVP development failure in the field is "confusing an MVP with a prototype." The two are fundamentally different.

Comparison axisMVPPrototype
PurposeValidating and learning from market hypothesesConfirming technical feasibility and demos
Target usersActual target usersInternal teams, investors, stakeholders
Level of completionActually usable (minimal features)For demo purposes (may not work in production)
What is measuredUser behavior and business metricsTechnical feasibility
Next actionPivot or Persevere (business decision)Proceed to full development or give up

An MVP must be "something users can actually use." Even with few features, the goal is to obtain real feedback from actual users feeling (or not feeling) value. A prototype is for internal verification and cannot substitute for an MVP.

In lean startup, the most important and difficult decision is whether to "Pivot or Persevere."

Signals that you should consider a Pivot

  • Key KPIs do not improve even after multiple sprints
  • User interviews consistently reveal "reasons they don't want to keep using it"
  • Acquisition cost (CAC) significantly exceeds LTV (customer lifetime value)
  • Users different from the core target segment are finding value

Signals that you should choose Persevere

  • User retention is above a certain level and core fans exist
  • KPIs improve with each sprint
  • Multiple users give feedback that they "would be in trouble without it"
  • There are signs of approaching PMF (product-market fit)

Pattern 1: "Playing at Agile"

This is a case where only the forms of Scrum (sprints, daily scrums, etc.) are adopted without the philosophy taking root. It becomes "Agile for the sake of daily meetings," resulting in the contradiction of effectively proceeding as waterfall.

How to address it: It is important to instill the WHY—"why Agile is needed"—throughout the organization before the framework's forms.

Pattern 2: Insufficient authority of the PO (Product Owner)

In Scrum development, the PO is the role with final authority to decide priorities of "what to build." However, in Japanese companies, the PO often exists only nominally, with actual decisions requiring approval from superiors, causing sprints to stall.

How to address it: It is essential to grant the PO real decision-making authority and establish a system where management supports it.

Pattern 3: Divergent interpretations of "minimal" in MVP

Interpretations of "minimal" differ from person to person, causing the MVP to balloon. Engineers define it as "the minimal with assured quality," while the business side defines it as "the minimal that can compete with rivals," resulting in something no different from full-feature development.

How to address this: It is important to start development only after everyone can answer the question, "What hypothesis do we want to validate with this MVP?" When the hypothesis is clear, it becomes easier to judge whether "this feature is necessary for the MVP."

The ultimate goal of MVP development, Agile, and Lean Startup is achieving PMF (Product-Market Fit). PMF refers to "a state in which the product strongly matches market needs," a concept proposed by Marc Andreessen, founder of Y Combinator-backed Netscape... actually, a concept advocated by Marc Andreessen.

Signs of PMF Achievement (Sean Ellis Test)

Sean Ellis (former growth marketer at Dropbox) proposed measuring PMF with the following question.

If 40% or more of users answer "very disappointed" to the question "How would you feel if you could no longer use this product?", it is considered a benchmark for achieving PMF.

Until PMF is reached, you repeat the cycle of MVP development → measurement → Pivot or Persevere. After achieving PMF, you shift Agile sprints into a growth phase and aim to scale up.

Practical Actions for Winning High-Rate Projects

① "Articulating" Skills and "Visualizing" Track Record

For MVP development, Agile, and Lean Startup experience, it is important to articulate it not as "I have used it" but in the form of "what hypothesis I validated, how I validated it with an MVP, and what results I achieved." Keep a concrete track record in your portfolio and on GitHub.

② Establishing a "T-Shaped" Specialization

By understanding the entire modern DX development flow of "MVP × Agile × Generative AI" as the horizontal axis (breadth), while deepening a specific domain (fintech, healthcare, SaaS, etc.) or technology (backend, ML, etc.) as the vertical axis (depth), you can position yourself as a "rare specialist."

③ Registering with Agencies with Shallow Contract Layers

To achieve high rates as a freelancer, it is important to secure projects with contract layers close to end companies or prime contractors. If you end up in a multi-tier subcontracting structure, your rates may be driven down despite your skills. Registering with agencies that have many direct transactions with end clients is an effective strategy.

Let's review what this article has covered.

MVP development, Agile, and Lean Startup are not independent frameworks; they combine in a layered structure of "strategy (Lean) → tactics (Agile) → means (MVP)" to deliver maximum effect.

From 2025 onward, the addition of generative AI has dramatically accelerated MVP speed and further sped up the hypothesis validation cycle. Engineers who master tools like Cursor and Dify, and PMs who can design MVPs for generative AI products, are in high demand in the market.

Practicing these frameworks in DX promotion and new business development, and experiencing rapid development combined with generative AI, is the shortest path to increasing your market value as a next-generation engineer or PM.

Moreover, armed with upstream process experience and an understanding of these frameworks, going independent as a freelancer has become a more realistic and lucrative career path than ever before. By shifting the practical experience accumulated as a full-time employee to a freelance working style, it is not uncommon for PMs and engineers to achieve monthly compensation of ¥800,000–1,200,000 or more.
