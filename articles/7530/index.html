Chapter 9 · What Really Changed Is the Delivery Contract

Original title: 第 9 章 · 真正改变的是交付契约

FDE 的来路 · 2023 · 6 min read

Summary

This chapter argues that the real shift in forward deployed engineering is the delivery contract: completion is defined by production adoption and business outcomes, not by launch. The author contrasts proofs of concept with minimum viable deployments, which must touch real data and workflows with pre-agreed metrics. It separates system, work, and business metrics, urges FDEs to sometimes declare a project not worth doing, and favors layered commitments over pure pay-for-performance. A finished engagement should leave observable customer change, reusable capabilities, and a data-backed relationship.

Key points

  • Delivery contracts shift from functional acceptance to adoption and business results
  • Minimum viable deployments need real data, real users, and agreed metrics
  • System, work, and business metrics cannot substitute for each other
  • FDEs should be willing to say a project is not worth doing
  • Accountability means layered commitments, not owning all business risk

What changes with the FDE is not just a job description, but a delivery contract that shifts from functional acceptance to production adoption and business outcomes, along with the team responsibilities that come with it.

Software projects have a very ceremonial moment: go-live.

Congratulations flood the group chat, the project team takes a group photo, and leadership thanks both teams. The people who have been busy for months can finally breathe a sigh of relief.

Go-live matters, of course. Until you're in production, everything before is just preparation.

But from the customer's value perspective, go-live is only the moment the system first becomes eligible to be tested.

Will users actually use it? Will processes change? Can the model still work with real data? Who picks up the pieces when something goes wrong? Each of these only reveals its true face after go-live.

Traditional projects treat go-live as the endpoint, and there are contractual and organizational reasons for that. If the FDE merely pushes the endpoint back a few weeks without changing how success is measured, it's just a longer delivery.

The new contract has to answer: what exactly has to happen for the project to be considered complete?

From "It Runs" to "People Use It"

The easiest thing for a prototype to prove is that it runs.

Feed it a set of sample data, and the model identifies the fields; type in a question, and the system generates an answer; click a button, and the workflow completes. Customers get excited when they see it, because an abstract capability finally has a picture.

The problem is, every step of the demo is being watched over by someone.

The data has been cleaned in advance, exceptions never appear at the same time, permissions are already set up, and if the model answers wrong you can just try again. In production, users don't live by the demo script.

The first gate isn't accuracy—it's adoption.

Is the operator willing to hand work over to the system? Do they still need to maintain the old process alongside it? Will a ten-second delay make them give up? When an error occurs, do they know who to turn to?

OpenAI's FDE job description writes "production adoption" and "measurable workflow impact" directly into its success criteria. Rather than how many models were deployed, it cares more about whether the workflow actually changed.

If a system is opened a hundred times a day but only lets users copy results back into Excel, the adoption may still be fake. Usage only proves that someone is clicking; it can't, by itself, prove value.

The Minimum Viable Deployment Is Not a Prettier POC

A proof of concept answers "is this technically possible?"

A minimum viable deployment answers "in the real environment, is this worth continuing?"

Both can look small; the difference lies in the constraints.

A minimum viable deployment must at least touch real—or close enough to real—data, enter an actual workflow, have real users, and agree in advance on an outcome metric. It doesn't have to carry full production traffic, but it can't be a performance staged only in a conference room.

Palantir once described its AIP Bootcamp as going from zero to a use case in five days or less. The speed is attractive, but "five days" isn't magic. The value of a short cycle is that it forces both sides to pick just one sufficiently concrete problem and test it on the spot with real data and workflows, leaving no room for a grand platform project to stretch on indefinitely.

Speed isn't about writing less code—it's about getting objections sooner.

The user says this step would never be done that way; the security team says this permission can't be opened; the moment the data is connected, three systems turn out to contradict each other. The earlier these "bad news" items surface, the more money the project saves.

Agree on the Referee Before the Game Starts

Enterprise AI projects are most prone to vague goals:

"Improve efficiency, enhance experience, empower decision-making, increase intelligence levels."

These phrases are fine for project proposals, but useless for accepting value.

A concrete project should at least separate three types of metrics.

The first type is system metrics: accuracy, latency, stability, cost per call.

The second type is work metrics: processing time, share of manual review, backlog size, first-pass rate.

The third type is business outcomes: how much loss avoided, how much extra revenue, how much risk reduced, whether the customer renews.

None of the three can substitute for the others.

Higher model accuracy doesn't guarantee shorter work time; shorter work time doesn't guarantee the company saves money. If a role saves two hours a day but the person still has to be on standby the whole time, the savings may not show up in the financials right away.

So before the project starts, agree on the referee: which numbers to look at, what the baseline is, how long the evaluation period is, and who confirms the results.

A pilot without a referee is the most likely to stay forever in the state of "just a bit more optimization and it'll be valuable."

Dare to Say "Not Worth Doing"

Traditional vendors find it hard to refuse a request that comes with a budget.

If the customer is willing to pay, the project brings in revenue. Whether the benefits exceed the costs is usually left to the customer's own business judgment.

If the FDE claims to be accountable for outcomes, it can't hide entirely behind that boundary.

Some processes happen only a few dozen times a year, and the cost of automating them will never be recovered; some tasks are time-consuming but require humans to carry relationships and judgment, and forcing AI in only adds review overhead; some problems are rooted in broken systems, and the model can only make them execute faster.

In these cases, the most valuable deliverable may be a single sentence: "Not worth doing."

Saying this isn't romantic. It may shrink the contract, or even stop the project. But if the FDE is only there to prove that AI can do everything, we end up back at selling features.

Judging whether something is worth doing requires putting the technical ledger and the business ledger side by side:

How much does it cost to build, how much to run, how large is the cost of errors, whether the savings actually reach the financial statements, and whether the growth is actually driven by the system.

Technology is the entry ticket; the math decides whether there's a next round.

Accountability for Outcomes Doesn't Mean Promising Every Outcome

"Pay for performance" sounds most in line with the FDE spirit.

The system recovers an extra million yuan, and the service provider takes a share; each customer session resolved is billed per case. Price is tied directly to value, and the two sides' goals seem perfectly aligned.

In reality, outcomes are usually determined by many factors together.

Sales growth may come from market conditions; falling bad debt may be due to a changed customer mix; customer service satisfaction is also affected by logistics and product quality. If the service provider can't control the key variables but promises the final outcome, the contract becomes an argument over attribution.

So being accountable for outcomes doesn't mean taking over all of the enterprise's business risk.

A more workable approach is layered commitments.

Take clear responsibility for the system and workflow metrics you can control; set joint goals for business outcomes and track attribution together; and when conditions are ripe, tie part of the fees to results.

Chinese enterprises' procurement habits, budget categories, and acceptance processes also mean not every customer can jump straight to pure outcome-based pricing. Staged acceptance and value metrics can coexist; there's no need to reject project management just to look advanced.

When Delivery Ends, Three Things Should Be Left Behind

When an FDE project ends, it should leave behind at least three types of results.

On the customer side, work has changed in observable ways. Users have genuinely adopted it, risk boundaries are clear, and business metrics have before-and-after comparisons.

On the product side, field experience has been distilled into reusable capabilities. Connectors, eval sets, business objects, control rules, or delivery playbooks—things that can be used next time.

On the relationship side, both sides know whether to expand, maintain, or stop next. Not based on "the customer feels good about it," but backed by data and shared experience.

If only code is left behind, the project remains fragile.

If only the customer relationship is left behind, the service depends on individuals.

If there's only a set of pretty metrics but no reusable capability, the team starts from scratch at the next customer.

The FDE's delivery contract is heavier than a traditional project's precisely because it touches all three of these sides at once.

At this point in Part Three, the answer can be stated more clearly.

What's new about AI is not just that it can generate content, but that it has begun to take action; what's new about the FDE is not just the blending of capabilities, but that responsibility no longer stops easily at go-live; what's new about delivery is not just a shorter cycle, but that value must be proven through production adoption, workflow impact, and reusable feedback.

But once you're ready to do that, how much of your past experience with data modeling and governance still helps?
