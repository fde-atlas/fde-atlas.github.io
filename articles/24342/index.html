Summary

This Japanese guide explains the distinction between requirements definition (user requirements, driven by the client to articulate business problems, producing an RFP) and system requirements definition (driven by the vendor to translate those needs into verifiable specifications). Citing ISO/IEC 29148 and IPA's non-functional requirements framework, it lists what a requirements document should contain, describes five common failure patterns with countermeasures, and argues that engineers who can lead upstream processes command premium freelance rates of 1–1.5 million yen per month.

Key points

  • Requirements definition answers 'What', led by the client side.
  • System requirements definition answers 'How', led by the vendor.
  • Non-functional requirements must be quantified, per IPA's six categories.
  • Common failures include deferred non-functional specs and scope creep.
  • Upstream skills can lift freelance rates to ¥1–1.5M monthly.

In system development projects, "requirements definition" and "demands definition" are frequently used terms. However, surprisingly few engineers and PMs in the field can clearly distinguish between the two. It is not uncommon to find cases where they are "used with a vague sense that they mean something similar" or "treated collectively as part of the upstream process."

However, proceeding with development while confusing the two carries risks: major rework may occur in later stages, or misalignment with the client's understanding may directly lead to quality problems in the system. Especially for those in upstream roles such as PMs or tech leads, accurately understanding the conceptual differences and being able to properly document them as definition documents is an essential skill.

In this article, we systematically organize the definitions, roles, and differences between demands definition and requirements definition, and then explain in detail the specific items that should be included in each definition document, common failure patterns, and countermeasures. In the latter half of the article, we also introduce how upstream process skills directly impact your career as a freelance engineer.

Demands definition (Requirements Elicitation / User Requirements Definition) is the process by which the party ordering the system or service (the client or user) articulates and organizes "what they want to achieve" and "what problems they want to solve."

At this stage, system specifications and implementation methods have not yet been determined; the primary purpose is to articulate business objectives, operational challenges, and expected outcomes.

Examples of what is clarified during demands definition include the following.

  • "Currently, order processing takes an average of 30 minutes per order, and work concentrates at month-end, making overtime the norm. We want to resolve this through systemization."
  • "We want sales representatives to be able to check inventory in real time from the field."
  • "Our EC site has a high cart abandonment rate. We want to improve the payment flow and increase the purchase rate by 10%."

In this way, demands definition is the process of organizing business requirements and user requirements, starting from users' "voices" and "pain points."

In ISO/IEC 29148 (an international standard for system and software engineering), demands definition is defined as "the process of collecting, analyzing, and building consensus on stakeholder requirements."

Requirements definition (System Requirements Definition) is the process of converting the business and user requirements clarified during demands definition into concrete specifications as the "functions, performance, and constraints" that the system should fulfill.

Requirements definition is carried out primarily by the development vendor side (IT engineers, system architects, PMs, etc.). They receive users' requests of "we want to do this" and translate them into "this system will have these functions and meet these non-functional conditions."

Examples of what is defined during requirements definition include the following.

  • "Order data will be entered via a web form and automatically registered in the database after entry. Processing time shall be within 3 seconds per order."
  • "The inventory inquiry function shall be accessible from smartphone browsers, with screen navigation completed within 3 taps."
  • "The payment screen shall use a one-page checkout format, supporting three payment methods: credit card, convenience store payment, and PayPay."

The deliverable of requirements definition is the "requirements definition document," and design, development, and testing proceed based on this document. It is no exaggeration to say that the quality of the requirements definition document determines the quality of the entire project.

As this table shows, requirements definition (yōkyū teigi) is "the phase where the business side takes the lead in articulating issues and goals," while requirements specification (yōken teigi) is "the phase where the engineers take the lead in concretizing them as system specifications." In terms of the input-output relationship, the output of requirements definition (the requirements definition document and RFP) becomes the input of requirements specification.

The three main reasons these two are easily confused are as follows.

① Boundaries tend to be ambiguous in Japan's SI industry In large companies with an in-house IT department, that IT department may handle both requirements definition and requirements specification. Also, when an SIer takes on a project as a single contract, the process from interviews to creating the requirements specification document is handled as one continuous flow, so the two phases may not be distinguished and both may be called "requirements specification" (yōken teigi).

② Term definitions vary by context Both "yōkyū" (requirements) and "yōken" (specifications) correspond to the English word "requirement," making them prone to translation inconsistencies. Books and companies may also use different names such as "requirements specification document," "specification requirements document," or "user requirements document."

③ Definitions differ depending on the standard followed, such as PMBOK or ISO PMBOK organizes these from the perspectives of "scope definition" and "collect requirements," while IPA (Information-technology Promotion Agency, Japan) sometimes defines "requirements specification" (yōken teigi) as including the confirmation of user requirements.

In practice, rather than strict distinction, it is important to be mindful of the two-step thought process of "organizing business requirements early in the project (requirements definition) and then concretizing them as system specifications (requirements specification)."

In system development, requirements definition and requirements specification are positioned in the "upstream processes." Upstream processes refer to the design and planning phases before coding begins, and they greatly influence the quality and success of the project.

The typical flow of upstream processes in waterfall development is as follows.

Requirements definition → Requirements specification → Basic design (external design) → Detailed design (internal design) → Development/implementation → Testing → Release

The "V-model" is a framework that visualizes the correspondence between upstream and downstream processes. The left side of the V (descending part) corresponds to the design and development phases, and the right side (ascending part) corresponds to the testing phases, showing which test process verifies each design deliverable.

Design phase (left side)Corresponding test phase (right side)
Requirements specificationSystem testing (acceptance testing)
Basic designIntegration testing
Detailed designUnit testing

The importance of requirements specification in the V-model lies in the fact that "the content described in the requirements specification document becomes the criteria ultimately verified in system testing." If the requirements specification is ambiguous, it becomes difficult to judge pass/fail in acceptance testing, leading to problems such as "it was built to spec, but it's unusable."

In the requirements definition phase, the client and user company take the lead in advancing the following processes.

① Identifying stakeholders Identify all stakeholders involved in the project (executives, business staff, IT department, end users, etc.). Since priorities and directions of requirements may differ by stakeholder, it is important to understand the overall picture of the parties involved.

② Understanding current operations and As-Is analysis Organize the current business flows, tools and systems in use, and existing issues and bottlenecks. By digging down to the root cause of "why the issue is occurring," the problems that truly need to be solved through systemization become clear.

③ Designing the To-Be (ideal state) Based on the As-Is analysis, design the ideal business flow after system implementation. Separate "operations to be automated by the system" and "operations handled by people," and document what is expected of the system.

④ Prioritization and scope definition Since it is not realistic to realize all requirements at once, determine the scope of implementation considering business priorities, development costs, timelines, etc. Frameworks such as MoSCoW analysis (Must/Should/Could/Won't) are effective.

⑤ Creating the RFP (Request for Proposal) As the deliverable of requirements definition, create an RFP (Request for Proposal) to request proposals from vendors. The RFP should include the project's purpose, background, outline of requirements, schedule, budget estimates, and so on.

In the requirements specification phase, the vendor's PM, architects, and business analysts take the lead in advancing the following.

① Interviewing and analyzing requirements Based on the RFP and requirements definition document, conduct detailed interviews with the client and user representatives. Dig deeper into the intent behind the requirements, such as "why is this feature needed," "how often will it be used," and "are there any exception cases."

② Reorganizing business flows and defining system boundaries Based on the interview results, clarify the scope of operations handled by the system and the system boundaries (what is handled by the system and what by manual work). Using DFDs (data flow diagrams) and use case diagrams makes it easier to align understanding with stakeholders.

③ Organizing functional and non-functional requirements Classify and define user requirements into "functional requirements" and "non-functional requirements." (Details are explained in Chapter 3.)

④ Creating and reviewing the requirements specification document Document the organized content as a requirements specification document and have both the client and development team review it. Resolve ambiguous descriptions and discrepancies in understanding, and finalize it as the "agreed specifications."

⑤ Reaching agreement and sign-off Finally, obtain formal approval (sign-off) from the client to complete the requirements specification phase. Since this approval functions as the "contractual finalization of specifications," rushing approval while things remain ambiguous can cause problems later.

In waterfall development, the principle is to fully finalize the requirements specification at once (front-loading), but agile development takes a different approach.

In agile development, requirements are managed as a "product backlog," and high-priority items are implemented and verified sequentially in each sprint. Rather than fully finalizing requirements from the start, the basic approach is "rolling wave planning," where requirements are continuously refined based on feedback.

However, even in agile development, defining "what to build (the product goal)" and "what value it provides to whom (user stories)" is important. If development proceeds while these parts remain vague, it easily leads to a state of "fast sprint speed, but misaligned direction."

A requirements specification is a document that describes "the conditions the system to be built in the project must satisfy" and serves as the standard for development. This section explains in detail the main items that should be included in a requirements specification.

Business requirements are the items that define the purpose, background, and target business processes of systemization. Before describing technical specifications, they clarify "why this system is being built."

Main items to include:

  • Project purpose and background: Why the system is being developed and what the current issues are
  • Scope of target business: The boundary between the business processes the system covers and those it does not
  • Current business flow (As-Is): A description of the current business flow in diagrams or text
  • Future business flow (To-Be): The ideal business flow after system implementation
  • Related systems and external services: Existing systems, external APIs, and databases that require integration
  • Glossary: Definitions of project-specific terms and abbreviations (essential for preventing misunderstandings)

Using UML (Unified Modeling Language) activity diagrams or BPMN (Business Process Model and Notation) to describe business flows makes it easier to align understanding among stakeholders.

Functional requirements are concrete definitions of the functions and behaviors the system should provide. They cover both the parts users interact with directly (screens and operations) and the processes running in the backend (batch processing, API integrations, etc.).

Main categories of functional requirements and their contents:

① Screen/UI requirements

  • Screen list (screen IDs, screen names, and descriptions)
  • Screen transition diagrams
  • Input fields and validation conditions for each screen
  • Error message specifications
  • Whether responsive design is supported and supported devices

② Data requirements

  • Data subject to create, read, update, and delete (CRUD) operations and operation permissions
  • Data retention periods and backup requirements
  • How master data is managed

③ Reports/output requirements

  • List of reports and documents to be output
  • Format, output timing, and output destination (print/PDF/Excel, etc.) for each report

④ Batch processing requirements

  • List of batch processes (process names, execution timing, and process contents)
  • Error handling and recovery procedures

⑤ External integration requirements

  • List of systems and services to integrate with
  • Integration methods (API/file transfer/direct database integration)
  • Data formats (JSON/XML/CSV, etc.) and communication protocols

When describing functional requirements, it is important to use expressions like "shall be able to..." and write them in a verifiable form. Subjective expressions such as "make the screen easy to use" should be avoided because they do not allow pass/fail judgments during testing.

Non-functional requirements define conditions regarding quality, performance, and operations rather than the system's functions themselves. They are often treated as an afterthought, but deficiencies in non-functional requirements frequently cause system failures and security incidents, making them one of the most important items in a requirements specification.

The "Non-Functional Requirements Grades" published by IPA (Information-technology Promotion Agency, Japan) organizes non-functional requirements into the following six major categories.

① Availability: The ability of the system to remain operational. Defined quantitatively as an SLA (Service Level Agreement).

  • Service operating hours (e.g., 24/7/365 or weekdays 9:00-18:00)
  • Target uptime rate (e.g., 99.9% or higher)
  • Target recovery time (RTO) and recovery point (RPO) for unplanned outages
  • Maintenance window settings

② Performance and scalability: The ability of the system to respond appropriately to the required load.

  • Response times (e.g., within 3 seconds normally, within 5 seconds even at peak times)
  • Number of concurrent users (e.g., support for up to 500 simultaneous users)
  • Throughput (e.g., processing 100 transactions per second)
  • Scalability policy for increasing data volumes

③ Operability and maintainability: Conditions for operating and maintaining the system stably.

  • Monitoring and alerting mechanisms (e.g., Slack notifications to the person in charge when a failure occurs)
  • Log types, retention periods, and log formats
  • Backup frequency, number of generations, and restoration procedures
  • Procedures and frequency for version upgrades and patch application

④ Portability: Conditions related to data migration and environment migration from the current system to the new system.

  • Types, quantities, and formats of data to be migrated
  • Policy for data cleansing (deletion and correction of unnecessary data)
  • Migration schedule and phased release plan
  • Period of parallel operation with the current system during migration

⑤ Security: Requirements for preventing unauthorized access, information leaks, tampering, etc.

  • Authentication methods (e.g., multi-factor authentication and SSO support)
  • Access permission management (role and permission design)
  • Communication encryption (e.g., use of TLS 1.2 or higher)
  • Requirements for vulnerability assessments and penetration testing
  • Policy for handling personal information (compliance with GDPR and personal information protection laws)
  • Security standards to comply with (ISO 27001, SOC 2, etc.)

⑥ System environment and ecology: Infrastructure and environmental conditions under which the system operates.

  • Choice of cloud/on-premises and platform to use (AWS/Azure/GCP, etc.)
  • Supported browser and OS versions
  • Network configuration and bandwidth requirements
  • Hardware specification standards (for on-premises environments)
  • Future scalability and cloud-native strategy

It is important to describe non-functional requirements with quantitative figures. Instead of "runs fast," define it in a measurable and verifiable form, such as "response time must be within 3 seconds"; instead of "security must be considered," specify "communications must be encrypted with TLS 1.3 or higher, and multi-factor authentication is mandatory."

The requirements definition document and the requirements specification document are separate documents. Below is a summary of the main differences in their contents.

ItemRequirements Definition DocumentRequirements Specification Document
Project overviewPurpose, background, and business challengesScope of systemization and prerequisites
Users and stakeholdersList of parties involved, roles, and requestsUser types and access permission design
Business processesAs-Is (current) business flowTo-Be (future) business flow
Description of functionsRequests at the "we want to..." levelSpecifications at the screen, function, and processing level

Since the requirements definition document is primarily read by business stakeholders, it should be written clearly while avoiding technical jargon. On the other hand, since the requirements specification document is a technical document read by the development and testing teams, it requires precise, unambiguous expression.

This section explains common failure patterns in requirements definition and how to address them.

Failure #1: Non-functional requirements are postponed, and problems are discovered just before release

"The features were complete, but performance testing revealed that the system was significantly slow in the production environment, resulting in additional costs for infrastructure expansion."

Countermeasure: Define performance and security requirements from the early stages of requirements definition and incorporate them into the design. Use a non-functional requirements checklist.

Failure #2: Scope creep due to missing or overlooked requirements

"Additional requirements kept surfacing during detailed design, extending the development period by two months."

Countermeasure: Use user story mapping and business flow diagrams to cover user operation flows without gaps. Once requirements are finalized, establish a change management process, and accept additional requirements only through a formal change procedure.

Failure #3: Discrepancies in interpretation between the client and the developers

"The document said 'a feature that allows administrators to view data,' but the developers implemented it as read-only, while the client expected editing capabilities as well."

Countermeasure: Write requirements in "shall" form, and always attach use cases and screen mockups to share a concrete image. During reviews, verify behavior with a prototype together with the user representatives.

Failure #4: Priorities are undefined, and everything becomes "top priority"

"All features were defined as Must, but it was impossible to deliver them within the schedule."

Countermeasure: Use MoSCoW analysis to prioritize requirements and set the scope realistically. Considering phased releases is also effective.

Failure #5: The requirements specification document is not updated, leaving outdated information

"There were specification changes during development, but the requirements specification document was not updated, so members who joined later implemented based on the old specifications."

Countermeasure: Enforce version control of the requirements specification document and record change history. Clearly state the document's last updated date, version number, and approver.

Engineer skills can be broadly divided into "implementation skills" and "upstream process skills," but their supply-demand balance in the market differs greatly. While the number of engineers with coding skills is increasing year by year, engineers and PMs who can lead requirements definition from a business perspective are chronically in short supply.

The reasons are as follows.

  • Requires practical experience to acquire: Requirements definition demands "the ability to draw out users' true challenges through dialogue," "broad knowledge spanning both business and systems," and "documentation skills," which are difficult to acquire without hands-on experience
  • High responsibility for failures: Since the quality of requirements definition determines the success or failure of the entire project, it is not work that can be casually delegated; experience and a proven track record are required
  • Communication skills are also necessary: Beyond technical knowledge, facilitation skills are needed to bridge the three parties: business stakeholders, executives, and the development team

For this reason, engineers, tech leads, and PMs who can lead requirements definition remain highly sought after in both the hiring market and the project market.

The market rates for freelance engineers vary greatly depending on their skill set. While engineers focused mainly on implementation and coding earn around 600,000 to 800,000 yen per month, for PMs, tech leads, and architects who can lead the requirements definition through design phases, projects paying 1,000,000 to 1,500,000 yen or more per month are not uncommon.

The reasons are clear.

1. Hard to replace: Even as offshore development and AI-driven code generation advance, the process of "accurately articulating the client's challenges and building consensus with the development team" is still something only humans can do. Requirements definition skills are among the least likely to be replaced in the AI era.

2. High impact on the entire project: From the perspective of end clients and businesses, "someone who can accurately define what to build" is often more valuable than implementation itself. Freelancers involved in upstream processes tend to build trust with clients more easily, which often leads to long-term and repeat engagements.

3. Stability even after going independent: In the early stages of independence as a freelance engineer, some struggle to secure projects. However, with requirements definition skills, it is easier to establish a position in the project market through SES agencies or direct contracts, which also provides an advantage in rate negotiations.

For engineers who want to develop requirements definition skills, here is an example career path.

Step 1: Be mindful of the shift from downstream to upstream (while an implementation engineer) While you are still in the position of reading design documents and requirement specifications, make a habit of actively asking questions and gaining an understanding of "why this specification was decided" and "what interviews led to its definition."

Step 2: Take on some requirements definition work (as a lead engineer or sub-PM) As a support role to the PM or senior engineers, build experience through accompanying interviews, creating meeting minutes, drafting requirement definition documents, and so on.

Step 3: Lead requirements definition (as a PM or tech lead) Lead stakeholder interviews and take end-to-end responsibility for creating, reviewing, and building consensus on the requirement definition document. At this stage, learning knowledge frameworks such as PMBOK and IPA's Non-Functional Requirements Grades will help systematize your skills.

Step 4: Go independent as a freelancer and move into high-rate projects Once you have acquired upstream process skills, consider transitioning to freelancing, which positions you to target high-rate projects in the range of 800,000 to 1.5 million yen per month. In particular, in today's projects that demand both speed and precision—such as AI development, DX promotion, and MVP development for new businesses—demand continues to grow for freelance PMs and engineers who can define requirements quickly and accurately.

The main items that should be included in a specifications definition document are as follows.

  • Business requirements (objectives, background, business processes)
  • Functional requirements (screens, reports, batch processing, external integrations)
  • Non-functional requirements (availability, performance, operability and maintainability, migration, security, system environment)

Engineers and PMs who can lead these upstream processes are extremely rare and highly valuable in terms of market worth, both in the current development market and in the freelance project market. Building on implementation skills by accumulating knowledge and practical experience in requirements definition and specifications definition can be said to be one of the most reliable career strategies for continuously securing high-rate projects as a freelancer.
