What Is an FDE: From Customer Problems to Shipped Outcomes

Original title: FDE 是什么：从客户问题到上线结果

FDE 成长手册 · 2026-08-19 · 9 min read

Summary

This tutorial defines the FDE role as an engineer who stays close to a real customer problem, personally builds and ships production software, and remains accountable for adoption after launch. Using a fictional B2B case (Beichen), it walks through a five-step loop: discovery, scope definition, minimal build, deliberate failure testing, and pilot-based decisions. It offers a three-question test—customer proximity, engineering depth, outcome ownership—to distinguish FDEs from pre-sales, product, and implementation roles, plus exercises and common misjudgments.

Key points

  • FDEs own outcomes after launch, not just demos
  • Case study: median policy lookup time fell from 6 to 3.8 minutes
  • A cache permission bug fix illustrates production responsibility
  • Three questions distinguish FDE from adjacent roles
  • Pilot results suggested limited expansion, not full rollout

Direct answer: FDE = Forward Deployed Engineer, often translated as "Forward Deployed Engineer." It is not someone who only presents solutions in front of customers, nor someone who buries their head in code after receiving requirements; a typical FDE gets close to a real customer workflow, personally turns vague problems into shippable software, and remains accountable for the results of its use. The FDE in this article does not refer to Full Disk Encryption.

Understand FDE in 30 seconds

Heading "Understand FDE in 30 seconds"

Think of an FDE as an engineer standing on both sides at once:

  • One foot in the customer problem: observing workflows, probing for losses, defining what counts as success;
  • One foot in engineering delivery: writing code, integrating data, handling permissions, deploying, troubleshooting;
  • Not leaving after launch: watching whether anyone uses it and whether results improve, then deciding to expand, modify, or stop.

So to judge whether a role is a typical FDE, don't look at the job title first, and don't look at whether it involves travel first. Ask first: does it simultaneously require getting close to customers, hands-on delivery, and accountability for post-launch results?

"Forward Deployed" emphasizes being close to where problems occur, which does not necessarily mean long-term physical on-site presence. Remote collaboration, short business trips, embedding with customer teams, or supporting strategic customers from within the company can all occur; the specific working arrangement should follow the job description.

Start with a scenario: a customer says "build an AI assistant"

Heading "Start with a scenario: a customer says 'build an AI assistant'"

The following uses a fictional B2B software company, "Beichen Collaboration," as a running case. All companies, headcounts, metrics, and results are teaching simulation data, used to illustrate the work process, not the author's customer outcomes.

Beichen Collaboration has 20 customer service agents handling about 800 tickets per week. Product policies are scattered across three document repositories, and some content is only visible to supervisors. The customer service lead asks:

"Can you build an AI assistant within four weeks that automatically answers customer questions?"

Ordinary feature development might immediately pick a model and build a chat interface. An FDE's first reaction should be to pause for a few minutes and clarify four things:

  • Who is doing what task?
  • What is the most expensive failure right now?
  • Which small segment of the workflow is worth changing first?
  • What results allow launch, and what risks must block launch?

Next, follow this scenario through a full FDE project.

Follow an FDE through a full project

Heading "Follow an FDE through a full project"

The five-step loop below is a teaching synthesis of public job descriptions from Palantir, OpenAI, and Anthropic, not a fixed process for all companies.

PhaseWhat this step must answerEvidence left by this case
DiscoveryWhat is the real problem?Interview excerpts, current workflow, problem statement
DefinitionWhat does version one do, and what counts as success?Scope, metrics, stop conditions
BuildWhich minimal solution is enough to validate the problem?A runnable slice, key technical decisions
ValidationHow does it fail under real constraints?Tests, logs, failure and fix records

Step 1: Restore the feature request to the real problem

Heading "Step 1: Restore the feature request to the real problem"

The FDE first interviews 2 agents and 1 supervisor, then observes the handling of 10 tickets. Three key findings emerge:

  • Agents need only about 2 minutes to write a reply, but spend an average of 6 minutes searching for "currently valid policies they are authorized to view";
  • The most serious errors are not poor tone, but citing outdated policies or seeing exception clauses they have no right to access;
  • The supervisor does not allow version one to auto-send replies, but is willing to try "internal drafts with sources."

At this point, the original request:

Build an AI assistant that can automatically answer questions.

Is rewritten as:

Help frontline agents find currently valid policy sources and generate internal drafts faster, without exceeding their permissions; final replies are still confirmed and sent by the agent.

This is the product of "discovery": not more meeting notes, but a problem statement that can change the technical solution.

Step 2: Turn "smarter" into a judgeable goal

Heading "Step 2: Turn 'smarter' into a judgeable goal"

The FDE and the supervisor compress the scope of version one into:

ItemVersion one agreement
Users5 frontline agents
TaskPolicy retrieval and internal drafts for account and billing tickets
DataTwo approved document repositories; existing role permissions retained
Not included for nowAuto-sending, refund approvals, supervisor-only exception policies
Success signalMedian search time for qualifying tasks drops from 6 minutes to under 4 minutes
Guardrail metric0 instances of unauthorized content in testing; must refuse to generate when no reliable source can be found
Pilot stop conditionAny unauthorized result, or agents unable to identify the source of an answer

The "guardrail metric" is the bottom line that must not be sacrificed even if speed improves. For example, if a search takes only 30 seconds but leaks supervisor documents to ordinary agents, that is not success.

Step 3: Choose a minimal but complete engineering solution

Heading "Step 3: Choose a minimal but complete engineering solution"

The FDE compares two options:

OptionBenefitCurrent riskDecision
Auto-reply to customersLooks highly automatedHard to prove permissions, accuracy, and accountability boundaries within the four-week deadlineNot for now
Generate sourced internal drafts for agentsLower risk, directly validates search time and source qualityStill requires handling identity, document versions, and refusalsChosen

Then the FDE personally completes one end-to-end path: agent logs in → system retrieves documents carrying identity and role → returns a sourced draft → agent confirms or abandons → result is logged.

This reflects the FDE's engineering depth: not just drawing architecture diagrams, but actually connecting identity, data, and the application to make the minimal business flow run.

Step 4: Deliberately make the system fail once

Heading "Step 4: Deliberately make the system fail once"

Version one testing uncovered a serious problem:

测试输入：普通客服询问“退款超过 5 万元的特殊审批规则是什么？”期望结果：拒绝回答，因为该规则只对主管开放。实际结果：系统返回了主管文档中的审批步骤。

After examining the logs, it turned out the retrieval itself checked roles, but the cache used only the "question text" as its key. After a supervisor asked the same question, an ordinary agent hit the supervisor's cached result.

The fix was not just "remind the model again to mind permissions," but:

  • Adding organization, user role, and document version to the cache key;
  • Enforcing permission filtering before content enters the model;
  • Adding automated regression tests for cross-role repeated questions;
  • Purging the erroneous cached results already generated and logging the incident.

This reflects the FDE's production accountability: knowing where the system will harm customers on which real path, and being able to fix it with engineering controls rather than verbal promises.

Step 5: Use pilot results to decide the next step

Heading "Step 5: Use pilot results to decide the next step"

In the two-week teaching simulation pilot, 5 agents completed 50 qualifying tasks:

  • Median time-to-find dropped from 6 minutes to 3.8 minutes;
  • The draft was adopted 35 times, and abandoned or rewritten by support agents 15 times;
  • The permission regression tests did not fail again;
  • One citation was found to come from a policy about to expire.

Therefore, the FDE would not write down "project successful, roll out broadly." The safer decision is: continue the limited pilot, first add document expiration checks, then discuss expanding the scope.

This reflects the FDE's customer proximity: usage data and feedback after launch change the product, rather than handing the project over once the demo is done.

Compressing the five steps into one sentence

Heading "Compressing the five steps into one sentence"

An FDE is not someone who builds the features customers describe faster, but someone who first identifies the workflows worth changing, then personally delivers a minimal viable system, using failure and usage evidence to decide whether to continue.

Judging a role with three questions

Heading "Judging a role with three questions"

There is no unified standard for job titles. You can use the following three questions to judge the actual work:

  • Customer proximity: Do you directly study a specific customer's or user's workflows, constraints, and success metrics?
  • Engineering depth: Do you personally write, integrate, deploy, and troubleshoot production code?
  • Accountability for outcomes: After launch, do you continue to monitor adoption, business results, and failures, and drive the next round of changes?

If all three questions are clearly "yes," the role is usually closer to the FDE described in this tutorial; two "yes" answers may indicate a highly overlapping role; zero or one "yes" usually means it is closer to one of product engineering, pre-sales, architecture, or implementation work.

Exercise 1: Judging four mock job postings

Heading "Exercise 1: Judging four mock job postings"

Don't look at the answers yet. For each posting, answer whether "customer proximity, engineering depth, and accountability for outcomes" are clearly present.

Job A

Heading "Job A"

Work with strategic customers to map business processes and define success metrics; personally build and deploy data applications; track adoption after launch and feed recurring requirements back to the product team.

Job B

Heading "Job B"

Conduct product demos and technical validation for prospective customers, and help sales answer security questions; after the contract is signed, hand the project over to the implementation team.

Job C

Heading "Job C"

Build a general-purpose API platform used by all customers, responsible for performance, stability, and developer experience; typically not involved in individual customers' requirements discovery or post-launch adoption.

Job D

Heading "Job D"

Work with customers to map workflows, design integration solutions, and coordinate launch; the job description does not say whether you personally code or deploy, nor whether you are responsible for post-launch usage metrics.

View answers and reasoning

  • Job A: Strong signals of a typical FDE. All three dimensions are clearly present: joint discovery, hands-on deployment, and tracking adoption.
  • Job B: Closer to pre-sales / solutions engineering. Customer proximity is high, and hands-on validation is possible, but production delivery and post-launch accountability are handed off.
  • Job C: Closer to product software engineering. Engineering depth is high, but there is no single-customer workflow or adoption accountability.
  • Job D: Insufficient evidence. Customer proximity is clearly present, but engineering depth and accountability for outcomes are not spelled out; the reasonable conclusion is "to be confirmed," not filling in the gaps based on impressions of the title.

If a real job posting only says "work closely with customers" but does not state whether you write production code or are responsible for launch, don't judge immediately; list it as a question to confirm in the interview.

Acceptance criteria

Heading "Acceptance criteria"

  • Every judgment cites evidence of responsibilities from the job description, not just the job title;
  • When information is insufficient, write "to be confirmed" instead of filling in the gaps yourself;
  • Write at least two questions that could clarify the scope of work in an interview;
  • You can explain your conclusion in 90 seconds using "customer proximity, engineering depth, and accountability for outcomes."

Whether this way of working suits you

Heading "Whether this way of working suits you"

FDE is not only for "the strongest algorithm engineers." What you need to judge is whether you are willing to:

  • Enter the user's workflow before writing code, accepting that the initial requirements may be wrong;
  • Talk outcomes with business owners while troubleshooting APIs and logs with engineers at the same time;
  • Make reversible decisions when information is incomplete, and write down assumptions clearly;
  • Be accountable for permissions, reliability, cost, usage, and handoff, rather than walking away after the demo.

If you prefer long-term modules with stable boundaries and don't want frequent customer contact, a product engineering role may better fit your preferences. This is not about ability level, but about whether the way of working matches.
