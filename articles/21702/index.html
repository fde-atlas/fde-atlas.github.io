FDE vs Technical Account Manager: roles, incidents, careers

FDE Times · 2026-11-04 · 6 min read

Summary

This article contrasts the Forward Deployed Engineer and Technical Account Manager roles. FDEs write and ship production code daily, while TAMs create value through client relationships and coordination, as seen in AWS's Enterprise Support plan. Using a scenario where an order system fails before a client's biggest sale, it proposes a split: the FDE fixes the fault and sends short periodic technical updates, while the TAM briefs client leadership and pulls in specialists. Examples include Palantir's Forward Deployed Enablement Engineer, on call 24/7 for critical incidents. The author recommends assigning system and communication owners in advance, agreeing on update cadence, and submitting joint feedback to the product team. For TAMs seeking FDE roles, the usual gap is proof of shipping code to production themselves.

Key points

  • FDEs ship production code; TAMs rely on relationships and coordination.
  • During incidents, the FDE fixes the fault while the TAM communicates with the client.
  • Palantir's Forward Deployed Enablement Engineer is on call 24/7 for critical incidents.
  • AWS provides a designated TAM through its paid Enterprise Support plan.
  • TAMs moving to FDE work usually need proof of shipping production code.

In brief

  • FDEs build and ship code to production every day; TAMs read code but create value through relationships and coordination.
  • A workable split during an incident: the FDE fixes the fault while the TAM brings in specialists and keeps the client informed. At Palantir, one FDE variant is on call 24/7.
  • The two roles overlap in handling escalations and feeding input back to the product team, so they need to agree who sends which feedback.

On the eve of a client’s biggest sale of the year, the order-processing system you deployed for them starts returning errors. Two people from the vendor side are in the incident channel. One opens a terminal; the other opens a contact list to call the client’s head of operations.

If both open a terminal, nobody tells the client what is going on. If both pick up the phone, nobody fixes the fault. The line between a Forward Deployed Engineer and a Technical Account Manager (TAM) sounds like a matter of job titles, but the difference shows most clearly on nights like that one.

For a working FDE, understanding the TAM role means knowing when to hand the phone to someone else and get back to the code. For a TAM who wants to move into FDE work, it means knowing exactly which skills are still missing.

Where do the two roles differ?

A role comparison on fde.academy puts it briefly: FDEs build and ship, TAMs coordinate and advise. More concretely, a TAM can read code but rarely writes production code, while an FDE writes production code every day.

According to the same blog, a TAM’s strength comes from the depth of the client relationship and the ability to coordinate across departments, not from delivering the technical product themselves.

The rhythm differs too. A TAM stays with a client for the long term, with quarterly business reviews. An FDE works in deployments that last from a few weeks to a few months.

Christian & Timbers, an executive search firm, stresses that an FDE’s responsibility does not end when the client signs the contract; it runs until the system is in production and delivering measurable results.

AWS offers an easy example of the TAM role. It lists a designated TAM as a benefit of its Enterprise Support plan. The TAM comes with a paid support plan rather than as an engineer working inside the client’s systems.

The line blurs most during an incident

The two roles share one important thing: both handle client escalations and feed input back to the product team. That makes it easy, when something breaks, for both to pile onto the same task.

AWS describes fairly clearly what a TAM does around a critical business event. Beforehand, the TAM works with the client to check whether the system is ready and can handle the expected load. During the event, the TAM supports the client.

According to the Enterprise Support page, the TAM coordinates AWS specialists, keeps track of the overall picture and leads a post-event review to find what can be improved. The AWS Countdown program calls this kind of support “TAM-led”.

On the FDE side, there is an example from Palantir, where the role originated. The company has a variant called Forward Deployed Enablement Engineer, which sits in a centralized customer success team.

The job posting asks this person to act as first responder when incidents occur and to be on call 24/7 for critical client incidents. In other words, their job is to keep the client’s system running.

Worked example: one sale, two task lists

Back to the sale night from the opening. Say you are the FDE who deployed an agent that automatically processes return requests for a retail chain, and your TAM colleague has managed this client for years. The table below suggests how the two could split the work.

PhaseFDE doesTAM does
Two weeks beforeRuns load tests at expected traffic, fixes bottlenecks, writes a runbookChecks readiness and expected load with the client, confirms who on the client side has decision rights
During the incidentResponds first: reads logs, rolls back or patches, writes technical updatesBrings in internal specialists when the FDE needs them, briefs the client’s leadership
After the eventWrites the root-cause analysis, ships the long-term fix to productionLeads the post-event review, gathers feedback for the product team

The most fragile part is the handoff of information between the two during the incident. One effective approach is for the FDE to send the TAM a three-line update at fixed intervals, for example:

Broken: 30% of return requests are timing out at the inventory API call.
Doing: rolled back this afternoon's deploy, monitoring the error rate.
Next update: 23:30.

The TAM turns those three lines into what the client’s leadership needs to hear: the impact on shoppers and when the next update will come. The FDE does not have to leave the terminal to join calls, and the TAM does not have to promise a timeline without asking the person doing the fix.

Do it yourself: split the roles before trouble starts

Start while things are calm. Write down who owns the system and who owns communication with each person on the client side, then send it to both teams. If your company has no TAM, the communication owner can be an account executive or your team lead. What matters is that a named person holds the role.

Next, agree on the update cadence and message template before the event, not in the middle of an incident. Before each of the client’s peak periods, schedule a readiness review. The FDE brings the load-test numbers; the TAM brings the list of client stakeholders who need to be notified.

After the event, sit down together before sending anything to the product team. Since both roles feed input back to the product, one joint analysis carries more weight than two separate emails describing the same bug in two different ways.

Common mistakes

An easy trap for an FDE is to take on the TAM’s role as well: sitting in meetings with the client’s leadership while the fault is still unfixed. The TAM’s mirror-image mistake is promising the client a resolution time without asking the person reading the logs. Both mistakes come from not assigning roles in advance.

The third mistake is treating a TAM as a lighter version of an FDE. The two roles rest on different strengths, relationships and coordination on one side, production code on the other. Someone good at one is not necessarily good at the other.

How to read job descriptions and write a CV

When reading job postings, watch the phrasing. Terms such as “quarterly business review”, “designated” or “relationship” suggest a role leaning towards TAM. “Production code”, “on-call” or “first responder” suggest a role leaning towards FDE, even when it sits in a customer success team, as in Palantir’s case.

On a CV, do not just write “worked with clients”. Describe an incident you handled directly: what you fixed, how long the system took to recover, and which long-term fix reached production.

If you are a TAM aiming for FDE work, the missing piece is usually proof that you have shipped code to production yourself, so take on a small patch during the next escalation instead of passing all of it to the engineering team.

A good vendor needs both roles on the night of an incident. As long as each person knows their job in advance, the client sees the system restored and hears from the vendor when promised.
