OpenAI FDE Interview: Semantic Search Assignment and 60-Minute Review

Original title: OpenAI FDE 面试：语义搜索作业与 60 分钟评审

FDE Times · 2026-10-21 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide breaks down OpenAI's FDE take-home assignment and review process, using a semantic search system over Amazon product data as an example. Sources disagree on the time limit—roughly five hours per some guides, about a week per others—so candidates should confirm scope with recruiters first. The core advice: treat the assignment as a deliverable for a real customer, since evaluation weighs whether you can explain decisions and tie them to user needs. It also covers a 60-minute review, decision logs, and AI-allowed coding rounds.

Key Takeaways

  • Sources disagree on the take-home's duration: one candidate and Aced's guide say about a week, while Aced's overview, Gaijineer, and The Forward Deployed say about five hours.
  • Aced describes a 60-minute review: first presenting design choices, then answering questions on RAG, the trade-offs between fine-tuning and prompting, guardrails, and evals.
  • AI is allowed in the coding round. Aced's guide recommends sharing your screen and narrating your thinking as you write.

You open the recruiter's email, and the prompt is: build a semantic search system over Amazon product data that ChatGPT can call. One OpenAI Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) candidate recalled on Aced that this was exactly the assignment they received, with a week to build something that actually runs.

This is one person's experience, not a standard question, and accounts of the process don't fully agree. But there's one thing worth remembering from Aced's (formerly Exponent) guide: treat the take-home as a product delivered to a real customer.

The assignment is a small contract, not an exam

First, clarify the time limit, since sources differ. Aced's guide says you have about a week to submit working code, a running application, and a video walkthrough. Gaijineer and The Forward Deployed describe a roughly five-hour assignment: build something real and usable with OpenAI's API, plus a video.

Even Aced itself gives two numbers: its FDE interview overview (the source of the 60-minute review below) says the assignment takes about five hours, while its OpenAI-specific guide says about a week. The process may have changed, or different teams may do things differently, so don't treat either number as fixed.

So the practical advice is: ask the recruiter directly for the deadline and required deliverables, just as you'd confirm scope with a client before starting work. Final Round AI says the video should present the application as if you were facing a customer.

The grading criteria matter more than any technical trick. The candidate who got the Amazon prompt said grading weighs whether you can explain your decisions and tie them to customer needs.

Final Round AI puts it more bluntly: engineers who can't explain what they built don't pass this stage. No amount of clever retrieval saves a submission that doesn't run or can't be understood.

Start from what users type, not from the architecture diagram

Perspective AI recommends starting from the customer's job-to-be-done rather than from a reference architecture. Suppose you got the Amazon prompt. Before opening a notebook, write down three queries a ChatGPT user might type: "noise-canceling headphones under $100 for frequent flyers," "a gift for a 5-year-old who loves dinosaurs," "an iPhone 15 case that doesn't yellow."

These three queries immediately reveal two kinds of signals. The semantic parts ("frequent flyers," "loves dinosaurs") suit embeddings. Hard constraints ("under $100," "iPhone 15") should go through metadata filtering, because embeddings are bad at comparing numbers. This is exactly the kind of decision you'll need to explain later.

Next is how to represent products. A product record typically has a title, brand, category, a few lines of selling points, and possibly reviews. The simplest defensible approach: one short text per product, with price and category kept as filter fields:

def product_to_text(p):
    # 每个商品一个文档；价格保持结构化，用于过滤
    lines = [p["title"], f"Brand: {p['brand']}", f"Category: {p['category']}"]
    lines += p.get("bullets", [])[:5]
    return "\n".join(lines)

def search(query, max_price=None, k=5):
    q_vec = embed(query)
    candidates = [p for p in products if max_price is None or p["price"] <= max_price]
    return top_k_by_cosine(q_vec, candidates, k)

This is where chunking strategy matters. If you split long descriptions into multiple chunks, you have to merge results back to the product level. Without merging, a single product could occupy three of the top five slots.

Adding reviews brings in everyday phrasing like "my ears didn't hurt even on a whole flight," but also noise. Either choice is fine, as long as you can justify it.

Finally, measurement. Suppose you write 30 queries yourself, each pre-labeled with which products count as correct. If 21 queries have at least one correct product in the top five results, hit@5 is 70%. That makes a great opener for the review: "Version one hit 70%. Here's how much price filtering improved it, and why."

Bring a decision log into the review

Aced describes a 60-minute review combining a product demo with deep technical questioning. You first present your design choices, then answer questions about RAG, the trade-offs between fine-tuning and prompting, guardrails, and evals.

Aced's guide also notes that interviewers check whether the design starts from the customer's real needs.

According to Aced, common questions in this stage are: why did you choose this approach, and where did you decide not to take another path? The best preparation is a decision log recording which user need drove each technical choice. A template might look like:

DecisionRejected alternativeRationale (from the user's needs)When to change
One document per productSplit descriptions into chunksUsers want a set of distinct products, not text passagesWhen long descriptions dilute the embedding
Price as a hard filterPut price into the embedding text"Under $100" is a constraint, not a hintAlmost never
Skip reviews for nowAppend reviews to the documentKeep version one simple; measure first, then addWhen evals show everyday-language queries are missed

The last column is the most valuable. When asked "what if the customer has ten million products?", you don't have to improvise, because the conditions for changing your mind are already written down. Add a few more rows covering the topics Aced lists, such as why fine-tuning isn't needed yet, and which guardrail blocks results with mismatched prices.

The AI-allowed coding round: what's tested is how you use tools

The candidate who got the Amazon prompt only briefly mentioned that even the coding screen allows AI, without detailing the rules. Aced's guide is more explicit: AI tools are allowed, so share your screen and narrate your thinking as you write.

When every candidate has the same tools, the only thing an interviewer can observe is how you think. Narrating as you write might sound like:

"Before having the AI write anything, let me confirm one thing: could the input be an empty array? Now I'll have it write a first version... this line uses sort, so it's n log n, acceptable for this problem. Let me add a test for duplicate elements—that case is easy to miss."

The advice here: show the interviewer that you carefully read AI-generated code, write your own tests, and catch bugs before running. Pasting output and silently waiting for results wastes the chance to demonstrate your thought process.

Mistakes that get good work rejected

A common mistake is optimizing in the wrong direction: spending three days on an elaborate reranker with no README, no one-line command to run it, and no eval set. Per Aced's advice to treat it as a product delivered to a customer, such a submission loses to a simple system with tests and measurements.

Another mistake is opening the video walkthrough with the architecture diagram. Show the user's queries and results first, then explain the implementation behind them. Never answer "why" with "because that's how the tutorial did it." The whole point of the decision log is that you never have to.

Even if you never interview at OpenAI, this preparation won't go to waste. A search project with evals, decision logs, and a five-minute video belongs in the portfolio for any FDE role.

When writing it into your resume, use one line to state the numbers and a hypothetical customer, for example "built semantic search for 200 products; adding price filtering improved hit@5 from 70% to X%", rather than just listing "RAG, embeddings".

Practice this week: redo the Amazon problem in about five hours. That's the benchmark given by the overviews from Gaijineer, The Forward Deployed, and Aced, not the one week the candidate had. Then have a friend play the customer and grill you with follow-up questions for a full 60 minutes. Any question you can't answer goes straight into the "when to change" column.
