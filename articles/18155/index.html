In the era of AI writing code, what value does a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) still have?

Original title: AI 写代码时代，前线部署工程师（FDE）还有什么价值

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 5 min read

Summary

The article argues that as AI mass-produces code, the value of forward deployed engineers shifts from writing code to owning outcomes. Citing a Salesforce job posting that holds FDEs accountable even for AI-generated code, and OpenAI's Colin Jarvis, who estimates model capability explains only about 20% of the gap between demos and production, it shows most deployment work lies in organization, data, permissions, and evals. Responding to criticism that FDEs are a stopgap, it contends FDEs who leave capabilities with customers and feed insights back to product teams will remain in demand, and offers practical advice on resumes, interviews, and portfolio projects.

Key points

  • Salesforce requires FDEs to own quality of AI-generated code.
  • Jarvis estimates model capability is ~20% of the deployment gap.
  • Most deployment effort goes to data, permissions, and evals.
  • FDEs should transfer capability to customers, not create dependency.
  • Developers should demonstrate accountability, not code output.

Key Takeaways

  • Salesforce's FDE job posting requires FDEs to be accountable for delivery quality, whether the code comes from AI or from the engineer themselves.
  • OpenAI's Colin Jarvis believes model capability accounts for only about 20% or less of the deployment gap; most of the rest lies in organization, data, permissions, and evals.
  • Some argue FDEs are just a stopgap. To avoid being replaced, FDEs must leave capabilities with customers and bring frontline feedback back to product teams.

In mid-June 2026, Salesforce posted an FDE job opening for Agentforce and Data Cloud, with an unusual detail: whether the code was generated by a colleague using AI tools or written by the FDE themselves, the FDE must be accountable for delivery quality.

For this employer, what matters is no longer who typed the code, but who is accountable for it.

This is not an isolated case at one company. Infosys's CEO has said his team has already generated over 28 million lines of code with AI. At that scale of code output, the ability to write code is no longer scarce. Anyone who wants to become a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) must answer a very practical question: what reason is left to hire you?

Read the job postings from Salesforce and Anthropic, and listen to what OpenAI's head of FDEs says, and the answers are much the same. The FDE's value today lies in: being accountable for outcomes, taking on the parts of a project that don't belong to the model, and helping customers run the system themselves.

If you have two to eight years of experience, your resume needs to prove exactly these things. Commit counts won't prove them.

The cheaper code gets, the more valuable the accountable engineer becomes

Salesforce describes the senior FDE as the highest-level technical authority on the customer site, responsible for ensuring deliverables meet the highest technical standards.

Right after that, the posting adds two short sentences: technical depth is required, and so is architectural judgment. Combined with the requirement to be accountable "even for AI-generated code," technical depth today is used not only to write your own code, but also to review code written by others or by machines.

10Clouds uses exactly this criterion to distinguish FDEs from platform engineers: FDEs write code on customers' systems, are often on-site, and are accountable for deployment outcomes. AI doesn't blur that responsibility. When AI generates code faster than humans can read it, that responsibility becomes heavier, not lighter.

Imagine a scenario: an AI tool writes a connector that hooks an agent into a customer's CRM in 20 minutes, and all tests pass.

But the connector pulls every data field into the agent's context, including end customers' phone numbers and personal notes. The model won't spot this problem on its own.

The one who catches it must be the FDE on site.

The model is only a small part of the problem

Colin Jarvis, OpenAI's head of forward deployed engineering, estimates that between a demo and a system running in production, model capability accounts for "about 20% or less" of the gap. The rest, in his view, is about organization, data, permissions, and evals. The person saying this leads FDE teams at a lab that builds models.

Try applying that number to a hypothetical project that has been stalled for 10 weeks. This is only a rough illustration: Jarvis was talking about shares of the deployment gap, not offering any way to divide up time.

Roughly converted into time, the model part is at most about two weeks; the other eight weeks go into requesting data access, waiting for security approvals, cleaning dirty tables, and building eval sets that earn customer trust.

AI coding tools shorten the code-writing step. Most of the time, however, is spent in that other eight weeks.

Anthropic's Applied AI FDE job posting makes this point clearly: FDEs work inside customers' systems, take Claude-based applications to production, and deliver MCP servers, subagents, and agent skills.

Every one of these involves organizational and permission decisions. An MCP server determines which tools and data an agent can access. So the permission issues Jarvis mentions won't stay in meeting notes; they will materialize as line after line of concrete configuration in the servers you deliver.

The bulk of an MCP server's code can be handed to AI. What AI can't do for you is decide what the server opens and closes, and what evidence proves the agent behaves correctly. That is the work you must be able to explain clearly and defend in front of the customer.

Is the FDE just a temporary patch?

The weightiest counterargument comes from Larry Dignan of Constellation Research: FDEs exist because the products aren't mature yet. There's merit to this view. If platforms could handle data, permissions, and evals on their own, the person in the middle would be far less necessary.

OpenAI itself doesn't want FDEs to become a long-term crutch. Jarvis puts it bluntly: "We don't want to create dependency on FDEs." Anthropic emphasizes something else: FDEs must bring what they learn on the front lines back to product and engineering teams, so every customer's experience flows back and makes the product better.

So Dignan's critique may only apply to one kind of FDE: the kind whose value comes from the customer being unable to do without them. Once products mature, that kind of FDE loses their place. The other kind of FDE leaves capabilities with customers, and every deployment makes the product better.

The latter kind of FDE will still have work to do at the next customer, facing even harder problems. Gergely Orosz of The Pragmatic Engineer notes that demand for this role at Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic is extremely strong.

What developers need to prove

The first step is reworking your resume. Claims like "developed 15 APIs" carry little weight these days, because AI can do that too. Write from the angle of responsibility: what you were accountable for in a production deployment, which risks you caught in AI-generated code, and what the customer could run on their own after you left.

When reading job descriptions, watch for phrases like "owns the outcome," "architectural judgment," "customer systems," and "MCP servers." They tell you whether the company is hiring someone accountable, or just another pair of hands to write code.

Before interviews, prepare a real story about a time you rejected code written by AI: what was wrong, how you caught it, and what would have happened if it had shipped.

For practice, a worthwhile personal project is building an MCP server for a messy internal system. Pair it with a one-page access scope document, a small eval set, and a runbook complete enough that a colleague could follow it and run things without you. This project exercises all three things Jarvis mentioned: data, permissions, and evals.

Finally, there are the non-technical skills 10Clouds lists: product intuition, and composure in front of customers. If you do outsourcing work and frequently deal with overseas clients, you already have a place to hone both. Practice saying "no" to requirements with technical justification while preserving the customer's trust.

When a company already has machines writing more than 28 million lines of code, employers are unlikely to ask what you can write. What they want to know is which code you're willing to be accountable for.
