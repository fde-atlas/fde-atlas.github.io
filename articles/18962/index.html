How to do the FDE interview take-home assignment

Original title: FDE 面试带回家作业怎么做

FDE Times · 2026-11-11 · 5 min read

Summary

Drawing on guides from Aced (formerly Exponent) and techinterview.org, the article examines how FDE take-home assignments at Cognition, OpenAI, and Palantir have shifted away from pure coding. Cognition's assignment runs inside Devin with little code; OpenAI's week-long case requires a working app plus a video where each design decision maps to a customer need; Palantir's problem-decomposition round involves no coding at all. The argument: AI-generated submissions look alike, so evaluators now score structured decomposition, end-user empathy, data-driven reasoning, and scoping. The author recommends keeping a decision log and documenting customer-facing blockers.

Key points

  • Cognition's FDE take-home is done in Devin with minimal coding.
  • OpenAI's week-long assignment requires a runnable app and a video walkthrough.
  • Palantir's decomposition round scores four criteria, none related to syntax.
  • AI-generated polish makes submissions indistinguishable, so reasoning matters most.
  • Practical tips: keep a decision log and submit a list of customer blockers.

Key Takeaways

  • Cognition's FDE take-home assignment is completed in Devin with almost no coding; OpenAI's assignment requires submitting a working application and a video walkthrough.
  • The reason is AI: submissions increasingly look alike, and the thinking behind decisions is what separates excellent candidates from people who are good at writing prompts.
  • Reviewers want to see how you break down problems, how you understand end users, how you reason with data, and how you scope sensibly.

According to a guide from Aced (formerly Exponent), Cognition's FDE take-home assignment is completed in Devin, with coding making up only a small part. The exercise isn't meant to test how fast you can write algorithms, but to check whether you understand how customers use the product.

Cognition is not alone. Also according to Aced, OpenAI's take-home case study lasts about a week. Candidates must submit working code, a runnable application, and a video walkthrough; slides are optional. The advice given to candidates: in the video, every design decision should map to a specific customer need.

If the way you prepare for FDE interviews is just grinding more LeetCode or polishing your assignment repo, you're likely still blank on the part that actually decides the offer.

The round that decides the offer often involves no code

An analysis on techinterview.org points out that the most heavily weighted round in FDE interviews often involves no coding at all. The familiar example is Palantir's problem decomposition round: candidates are given a vague real-world problem, break it into smaller pieces, and propose solutions, with almost no coding.

The scoring criteria are written plainly: structured decomposition, empathy for end users, data-based reasoning, and the ability to scope. None of the four has anything to do with syntax. They describe exactly what an FDE does in their first week on-site with a customer, when nobody yet knows what the final system will look like.

Take-home assignments are converging toward the decomposition round. Put the three formats in one table and you'll find they ask almost the same question, just phrased differently.

RoundFormatAmount of codeThe real question
Cognition take-homeHands-on work in DevinVery littleCan you build the product and run through workflows like a real customer?
OpenAI take-homeAbout a week: code, a runnable application, a video walkthroughYes, scored together with how you scope the problem around the customerWhich customer need does each of your decisions serve?
Palantir problem decompositionDecompose a vague real-world problemVery little or noneHow do you break down the problem, choose scope, and consider end users?

The key is the last column. Three companies used three formats, but all want to know: when you stand between the customer and the product, how do you think.

Why code is no longer a sufficient signal

Karat, a company that provides technical interviewing services, believes that coding tests and take-home assignments now provide less reliable hiring signals than before. Karat also claims that live interviews with both humans and AI involved provide the strongest signal. Take this claim with a grain of salt, since it's exactly the format Karat sells.

techinterview.org looks at technical hiring as a whole, not just FDE roles, and reached the same conclusion: when AI can generate a polished take-home assignment, the submission no longer shows who made it, and the assignment loses its only purpose—separating excellent candidates from people who are good at writing prompts.

As a result, many teams have kept the take-home assignment but added a live presentation round where candidates defend their decisions.

One part is hard to hand off to AI: knowing why something should be done a certain way, and for which customer. A model can generate a complete application but doesn't know at which step the customer's operator would give up. OpenAI's video walkthrough and Cognition's hands-on exercise in Devin both pull the focus back to this point.

The same application, two presentations

Suppose the prompt asks you to build a ticket triage tool for a logistics company. Candidate A submits a solution with clean architecture and complete tests, and uses the video walkthrough to introduce frameworks, prompts, and latency. Everything runs, everything is correct.

Candidate B submits an almost identical application, but opens differently. B says that night-shift staff need to know which tickets must be handled before the morning shift starts, and every subsequent decision flows from that need.

B uses four labels instead of twelve, because when the classifier makes a mistake, the person on duty has to fix it manually, and fewer labels mean faster corrections. B keeps an "uncertain" label to prevent ambiguous tickets from sliding into the low-priority queue. B also deliberately cut the dashboard, because the primary users have no use for it while on duty.

Score both assignments against the four criteria of the decomposition round, and B scores on all four: structured decomposition, understanding of night-shift staff, reasoning about mislabeled data, and deliberate scoping. A mainly demonstrates build quality, which in this example is precisely the part a candidate skilled with AI could also produce.

This doesn't mean B's code can be sloppy. Aced notes that at OpenAI, interviewers check whether the design starts from customer needs and use cases, weighing this alongside build quality rather than replacing it. The best assignments have both.

The first thing to do after receiving the prompt is to build a decision log before opening the editor. Each row has four fields: the decision, the customer need it serves, what you're trading off, and questions to confirm with the customer.

When it's time to record the video, just walk through this document in order, and the live defense afterward will rarely catch you off guard.

Document pain points like a customer

According to Aced's guide, the goal of Cognition's take-home assignment is to prove you can set up Devin and run through a real workflow. This claim comes from candidates' accounts, not an official company description, but it still shows that reviewers focus on how you set up and run workflows.

This skill is exactly the same as your first day on-site with a customer: install, integrate with existing systems, run a real workflow, and note where things get stuck. Newcomers often skip the documentation step, but it's precisely this list of pain points that shows you're looking at the product through the customer's eyes.

So when doing this type of assignment, submit the pain-point list along with how you would explain each item to the customer. After the interview, this document is also worth adding to your portfolio.

Your resume should speak the language of the decomposition round

A resume that only lists tech stacks tells readers what you can do, but not who you did it for. A better formulation: what you chose, because which user needed what, and what the result was. No matter where you apply, every project should have at least one bullet point mentioning the user.

Job postings also tell you where to focus your effort. If they emphasize working directly with customers or handling ambiguous problems, set aside as much prep time for problem decomposition and presentation practice as for algorithms.

In today's FDE take-home assignments, what truly sets you apart is being able to articulate who will use the product on Monday morning, and why they need exactly this design.
