What to Do When a Customer Demo Goes Wrong: A Demo Recovery Guide for FDEs

Original title: 客户演示出错怎么办：FDE 演示补救指南

FDE Times · 2026-11-02 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide from FDE Times explains how forward deployed engineers should recover when a live customer demo breaks. Key advice: acknowledge the error once and move on, never push fixes on stage, and classify failures into three levels (partial, core-flow, total) with pre-prepared fallbacks like recorded videos, screenshots, and click-through demos. It recommends designating a wing person, setting a 60-second switch deadline, and following up with a short video after the meeting. It also lists five common mistakes that amplify small errors, and suggests rehearsing deliberate failures before real demos.

Key points

  • Acknowledge the error once, then keep presenting.
  • Never push code or change the environment live.
  • Classify failures into three levels with matching fallbacks.
  • Assign a wing person and set a 60-second switch deadline.
  • Follow up with a short video showing what failed.

Key Takeaways

  • Acknowledge the error only once, don't apologize repeatedly, and move on before silence turns into doubt.
  • Don't push code or change the environment to save face; use a pre-prepared backup plan instead.
  • If the demo can't be saved, wrap up and propose next steps, then send a short video afterward showing the part you couldn't demo.

You click "Run." The progress bar freezes, then a red error message appears on the projector, and everyone on the customer's side sees it.

Reprise's blog describes the instinctive reaction to this moment precisely: panic, followed by a string of apologies. But Reprise also points out that once the presenter shows panic, the customer becomes uncomfortable and stops focusing on what you're saying. A technical error costs you one demo workflow; panic can cost you the entire meeting.

If you're a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), treat a failed demo as a rehearsal: once the system is actually live, you may face similar incidents. What needs to be done is the same in both situations: tell the truth, make quick decisions, and keep your promises.

Do only three things in the first minute

The first is to name the problem. MangoApps recommends acknowledging the issue once and moving on, before silence turns into doubt. The key word is "once": a third apology adds no information; it just keeps everyone fixated on the error.

The second is not to fix it on stage. An article on the Atomic Object blog puts it bluntly: during a demo, you shouldn't push code or make risky changes to the environment to save face. A patch hastily written while the whole room waits can turn one bug into two.

The third is to classify the failure and choose a path, because each severity level calls for a different response. Once you know which level you're at, you don't have to improvise when your mind is under the most pressure.

Core flow brokenThe core step won't runSwitch to video or screenshots, and clearly state it's a recordingVideo, screenshots, text output

A 45-minute meeting, error at minute 18

Suppose you're demoing an invoice reconciliation AI agent to a logistics company. The workflow has three steps: upload a batch of invoices, have the agent match them against orders one by one, and finally export an exception report. At minute 18, the matching step returns a 500 error. This is the core flow, so you're at level two.

Say it once. When the cause is still unclear, don't blame the Wi-Fi or the customer's infrastructure:

"The matching step just threw an error. I don't know the cause yet. My colleague will investigate separately, and I'll keep going so you can still see the final result."

Atomic Object calls this colleague the wing person: while the presenter keeps talking, this person stands ready to investigate or prepare data. Their advice is clear: don't count on someone happening to step in; arrange it in advance. In the meeting room, this person digs through the logs while you open the recorded video.

"This is a video I recorded before the meeting, of the same workflow run on sample data. Please pay attention to the exception report—I'll pause there."

Be explicit that it's a recording. If the customer thinks they're watching a live system and later discovers the truth, what you lose is no longer just a demo workflow—it's their trust.

Then the finance lead asks: "What happens if this error occurs in production?" Reprise advises that when you're stumped, calmly say you'll follow up later. The honest answer here is to give a deadline, not a guarantee:

"I don't know the cause yet, so I can't give you a definitive answer. I'll email you this afternoon with the cause and how we'll handle it."

Small errors can reveal real needs

A few minutes later, the wing person sends a private message: the demo file is missing the supplier code column—a requirement the presenter overlooked. Atomic Object describes a similar case: what looked like a bug was actually the user forgetting a requirement; the issue was recorded, the team gained useful information, and the demo proceeded as planned.

Don't stop at the correction. Own your part of the mistake first, then turn it into a question that helps you understand the customer's needs:

"Found the cause: the file I used was missing the supplier code—that was my oversight in preparation. But let me ask: do your real invoices ever lack this field too?"

In this scenario, suppose finance answers that invoices from small suppliers often lack this field. You've just uncovered a data processing requirement that might never have surfaced if the demo had gone smoothly.

Now do the math. The error happened at minute 18, leaving 27 minutes. Spend 1 minute acknowledging and pivoting, play a 3-minute video, and you still have 23 minutes to discuss the customer's problems. If you spend 12 minutes debugging in front of the projector, only 15 remain—and the customer has already sat through those 12 minutes doing nothing.

After the meeting, honor the deadline you promised. Reprise recommends recording a short video focused specifically on the feature or issue you couldn't demo, and sending it along with the cause and how you handled it. A 5-minute video showing exactly what the customer missed is more useful than a long apology email.

Backup plans must be prepared the day before

No one can come up with a good backup plan in a panic, so composure in the meeting room is largely the product of advance preparation. The ORACLE-BASE blog recommends preparing text output and screenshots of the demo, and ideally a video. Reprise adds the click-through demo for the rare case of total failure.

Before every demo, prepare in this order. Re-run the entire workflow in the actual environment you'll be using, and record a video during that run. Write a short checklist of input requirements—for example, required data columns and accounts that need to be logged in beforehand—so errors like a missing field get caught before the meeting.

Next, pick a tested alternative workflow and prepare a clean dataset. Designate a wing person and agree on a private messaging channel and a code phrase for passing messages. Finally, set yourself a time limit, say 60 seconds: if there's no news by then, switch to the backup plan and stop waiting.

If every backup plan fails, don't force it. MangoApps recommends simply wrapping up and proposing the most appropriate next step, rather than slogging through a broken workflow. It can be as simple as: "I'd like to schedule a short separate session to demo the part that didn't run. For the rest of today, I'd like to hear more about the problems you're facing."

Five ways to turn a small error into a big one

The first mistake is apologizing repeatedly, because every apology pulls attention back to the failure. A close cousin is guessing wildly at the cause: first blaming the network, then discovering minutes later it was your own fault—a correction that costs more credibility than the original bug.

The third mistake is bluffing when asked a hard question. A confident but wrong answer can follow you all the way to signing, while "I'll email you the answer this afternoon" takes only one email.

The fourth is promising and then forgetting: once the deadline passes with no word, all the effort spent staying calm is wasted.

The last one is subtler: leaving the presenter to debug alone. One person presenting and reading logs at the same time does both poorly, and the customer can see it on screen.

Turn failure into proof of capability

This skill is hard to convey with a single line of tech stack on your resume, so demonstrate it through action. Instead of writing "strong communication skills," describe how you built a demo process with backup plans and support roles, and give a concrete example of a requirement you discovered from an incident.

When interviewing for an FDE role, prepare a real story of a demo that failed: what you said in the first minute, which route you chose, and what you sent afterward. If you've never given a demo to a customer, internal presentations are a good starting point.

Exercise: rehearse a deliberately staged failure

This week, pick one of your existing demos and ask a colleague to observe. Have them break something at a moment you don't know in advance—shut down a service, or rename a column in the input file—then play the customer and ask a tough question the moment the error appears.

Note how long it took from the error appearing to your first sentence, and how long until you switched to your backup plan. Then count how many times you apologized. The number may shock you the first time, but it tells you exactly what to practice before a real demo—because by then, errors won't announce themselves in advance.
