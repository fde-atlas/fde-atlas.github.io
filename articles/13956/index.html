How many years of experience does an FDE need? Hiring requirements at Palantir, Anthropic, and OpenAI

Original title: FDE 要几年经验？Palantir、Anthropic、OpenAI 招聘门槛

FDE Times · 2026-10-15 · 6 min read

Summary

Comparing three FDE job postings, this article shows wide experience gaps under the same title: Palantir asks for 1 year post-graduation, Anthropic 4 years of customer-facing technical work, and OpenAI 5 years for a financial services role. The real differentiator is not programming languages—Python and JavaScript appear in both Palantir and OpenAI listings—but customer-facing experience and deploying LLMs to production. The author advises engineers to identify their level, spend roughly twelve weeks building an evaluated agent wrapped as an MCP server to generate verifiable evidence, and apply to matching tiers. Travel requirements of 25-50% deserve early clarification in interviews.

Key points

  • Experience requirements differ sharply: 1 year (Palantir), 4 (Anthropic), 5 (OpenAI).
  • The gap lies in customer work and production LLM deployment, not languages.
  • On-call and urgent integration stories show potential but don't replace required years.
  • A twelve-week plan: build an evaluated agent, wrap it as an MCP server, write it up.
  • Travel up to 50% is mandatory at OpenAI; ask actual ratios in first interviews.

Key takeaways

  • The same FDE title comes with very different experience requirements: Palantir asks for 1+ years, Anthropic asks for 4+ years of customer-facing experience, and OpenAI asks for 5+ years.
  • What separates the levels is whether you've worked with customers and shipped large language models to production, not which programming languages you know.
  • Use a few weeks of study to produce verifiable results and case studies, then apply to the level that matches your current years of experience.

All three job postings carry the same title, Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), but the numbers differ. Palantir requires at least 1 year of experience after graduation. Anthropic requires 4+ years in technical, customer-facing roles; an OpenAI FDE role focused on financial services requires 5+ years.

So "how long does it take to become an FDE?" can't be answered in weeks. No eight-week course turns 2 years of experience into 4. What a few extra weeks can buy you is something else: evidence for the specific requirements hiring managers will scrutinize.

If you've been a software engineer for 2 to 8 years, you likely already qualify for at least one of these levels. The task is to figure out which level you're at, see what separates you from the next one, and spend your evenings and weekends precisely on closing that gap.

One title, three different jobs

Put the three postings side by side and the differences go beyond years of experience. Each one reveals what the company will hand you from week one.

One limitation should be stated up front: there is only one posting per company here, Anthropic's role is in Munich, and OpenAI's is focused on financial services. The "levels" below are an interpretation of these three specific postings, not a universal rule for all FDE roles at AI labs.

Palantir Anthropic（慕尼黑） OpenAI（金融服务）
Minimum experience1+ years after graduation4+ years in technical, customer-facing roles5+ years in engineering or deployment, partly customer-facing
Programming abilityPython, Java, C++, TypeScript/JavaScript, or similarProduction LLM experience: prompt engineering, agents, evalsProduction-grade full-stack code in Python, JavaScript, or similar
DeliverablesEngineering work combined with direct stakeholder engagementDelivering MCP servers, subagents, and agent skills inside customer systemsEnd-to-end ownership from first prototype to stable production, across multiple deployments
TravelUp to 25%, flexible based on personal preference25-50%Up to 50%, a hard requirement
What you must proveWriting good code and working with non-technical peopleHaving deployed LLMs to production and measured qualityPersonally driving multiple customer projects to production

The Palantir column is the entry level. This posting emphasizes direct contact with customer stakeholders, from technical teams to management, and the ability to collaborate well in mixed teams of technical and non-technical people. Beyond code, they want to see that you're comfortable sitting at the same table as people who don't read code.

The other two columns are the AI lab level, at least based on these two postings. Here the years of experience come with a very specific condition: they must involve customers or production environments.

Anthropic names the deliverables outright: MCP servers, subagents, agent skills. OpenAI states that you'll own things from the first prototype all the way to a stable running system, and that you'll do this across multiple deployments.

The gap between levels isn't programming languages

In the programming ability row, the three companies differ little. Python and JavaScript appear in both the Palantir and OpenAI postings. An engineer with 3 years of backend experience will most likely clear this bar.

What really separates candidates is the other two rows: customer-facing experience and production LLM experience. The hiring platform Paraform writes in its FDE role guide that technical depth alone isn't enough to sustain you long-term. In its view, stakeholder management and adaptability matter just as much.

Paraform also makes a point worth noting for anyone considering a switch. It believes background matters less than temperament, and consulting experience isn't required. Engineers who have done hackathons, worked at early-stage startups, or carried an on-call pager tend to adapt fastest.

So some of your customer skills may already be hiding in your current job, just unspoken. Explaining to the ops team at 2 a.m. why the payment system went down is also an experience of working with stakeholders under pressure.

How much do your current years count?

Imagine a backend engineer with 4 years of experience whose title has never contained the word "customer." Read literally, Anthropic's posting would exclude this person, because it explicitly requires those years to come from customer-facing roles.

But if during those 4 years he carried an on-call pager, scrambled with the sales team on an urgent integration, or gave demos to partners, all of that is strong material for a resume and interviews.

Just don't assume hiring managers will count those years as equivalent to years in a truly customer-facing role. They demonstrate potential, but they don't substitute for the requirement.

The task is to describe the experience clearly, not to embellish it or change your title.

For each such experience, write down: who needed the solution, what they didn't understand, how you turned a vague requirement into a working system, and what the final result was.

If you can't articulate a result, the case study isn't ready yet.

Likewise, an engineer with only 2 years of experience shouldn't force their way into OpenAI's 5-year role. What fits them is a posting like Palantir's: the bar is 1 year, and the test is good code plus collaboration skills.

Applying to the right level lets you accumulate years of working with real customers, and it's precisely those years that will later open the door to the AI lab level.

Outside of work, build evidence in twelve weeks

The plan below is advice for people working full-time. Each phase produces one type of evidence, matching exactly the gaps shown in the table above.

Weeks 1 and 2: read job postings the way you'd read a technical spec. Pick three postings across the two levels and sort every requirement into three buckets: have evidence, have it but it's not on your resume, don't have it. The third bucket is your scope of work for the next ten weeks.

Weeks 3 to 6: build a small agent with evals, because this is what Anthropic's posting names most explicitly: prompt engineering, agent development, eval frameworks.

Pick a real workflow at your company, such as ticket triage, and write test cases first, then prompts. An agent that produces a quality scorecard across versions beats ten demos that measure nothing.

Weeks 7 to 9: wrap that agent as an MCP server so other tools can call it, then hand it to a real user who isn't an engineer.

This phase maps to OpenAI's "own it from prototype to production" requirement, just at a smaller scale. The hardest part will be the moment the user asks for something different from what you built, and those are precisely the stories most worth bringing into an interview.

Weeks 10 to 12 are for packaging. Write a short essay that clearly explains the problem, the users, the evals, and what you changed based on feedback. Then rewrite your resume so every bullet point starts with a specific person or team you helped, rather than with the name of a technology.

Travel is part of the job

All three job postings contain a line that career changers often skim right past: travel. Palantir says up to 25%, with flexibility based on personal preference. Anthropic says 25-50%, in order to build directly alongside customers. OpenAI says up to 50%, and notes that it is a hard requirement.

This number says more about the nature of the work than any other line in the job description. According to OpenAI's posting, you might spend one week out of every two at a customer's office. If your family situation doesn't allow for that, it's wiser to choose a role with more travel flexibility, even if the experience requirements are lower.

So in the first round of interviews, ask directly about the team's actual travel percentage. Stating your constraints early is far better than accepting the job only to discover you can't manage the schedule.

The right unit of measurement is number of deployments

Back to the original question. For someone with 1 year of experience, good coding skills, and a willingness to collaborate with non-technical people, the main requirements in Palantir's posting are already within reach. At this point, the primary purpose of the twelve weeks is to turn those abilities into evidence that hiring managers can see; it doesn't guarantee an offer.

For those aiming at the AI lab tier, distance should be measured a different way: how many times have you put a system into the hands of real users and taken responsibility for it.

Twelve weeks can't replace four years of experience. But it's enough to complete your first deployment, and every deployment after that counts toward your track record.
