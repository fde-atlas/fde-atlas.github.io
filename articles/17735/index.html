A Day in the Life of a Forward Deployed Engineer: Customer Stand-ups and Issue Triage

Original title: 前线部署工程师的一天：客户站会与问题分拣

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide argues that a forward deployed engineer's real day starts at the customer's standup, not the internal one, citing Paraform, FDE Academy, PostHog, Blockchain Council, and a Palantir job posting requiring 4-5 days on-site weekly. It walks through a Monday scenario: triaging overnight Slack and Jira items before 9:00, listening for blockers at the customer standup, and converting vague requests into concrete tasks with owners and acceptance criteria. It also covers the internal standup's changed role, five common mistakes, and how to reflect this work on a resume.

Key points

  • FDEs work on the customer's rhythm; daily priorities are set at the customer's standup.
  • Triaging overnight requests before the workday starts best distinguishes strong FDEs.
  • At standups, probe vague requests until they become tasks with owners and criteria.
  • Internal standups shift to relaying customer blockers and requesting product support.
  • Common mistakes include attending passively, triaging by urgency of nagging, and skipping written confirmation.

Key takeaways

  • Paraform notes that most FDEs work on the customer's rhythm. FDE Academy writes that the day's priorities are set at the customer's standup.
  • Customers often report issues asynchronously, so FDEs must finish triage before the workday starts. This skill best distinguishes excellent FDEs.
  • The main job at the standup is to listen for blockers, turn vague requirements into concrete tasks, and only then start building.

Picture this scenario: Monday, 8:40 AM, you've just sat down at your desk in the customer's office. There are four messages in the customer's Slack sent Sunday evening, and a new ticket in Jira. At 9:00 the customer's engineering team holds its standup; your own company's standup doesn't start until 10:00.

Engineers used to building products tend to treat the 9:00 meeting as "the customer's business" and the 10:00 meeting as the real work. Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) think the opposite: Paraform observes that most FDEs work on the customer's rhythm, not their own company's.

FDE Academy also writes that FDEs embedded with customer teams attend the customer's standup too, and that the day's true priorities are clarified in that meeting.

If you're a developer looking to transition into an FDE role, this may be the hardest habit to change, because it inverts how you decide what to do first. Just sitting in the right meetings isn't enough. What needs practice are three small habits: triage before the standup, listen for blockers during it, then turn what the customer says into tasks.

The gap to fill hides in the customer's schedule

PostHog defines the FDE as someone embedded in a customer team to bridge the gap between what the product can do and what the customer actually needs, sometimes working directly in the customer's office. This gap rarely shows up in your company's backlog.

It usually surfaces at the customer's standup. For example, a data engineer complains that a data pipeline is stuck because no one granted read access to a certain table.

You also spend more time on the customer's side than you'd expect. A Forward Deployed Software Engineer job posting for Palantir's US government business requires candidates to be on-site with customers 4-5 days a week, and describes the job as working directly with customers, often on-site, to understand and solve their problems.

PostHog says its FDEs spend a lot of time in meetings, do most of their work directly with customers, and often travel 20-50% of the time. Spending most of the week in the customer's building, the customer's rhythm becomes your rhythm.

Paraform's description of a typical week makes this clear: on Monday, the FDE attends the customer's standup and sets the week's priorities around where the customer is stuck; only on Tuesday do they focus on building in the customer's environment, with real data, and under the customer's deployment constraints.

Before 9:00: walk into the meeting with answers

FDE Academy points out that customers often report issues asynchronously, so triage should be completed before the workday starts, and considers triage the best way to tell which FDEs are stronger. If you handle several customers at once, it's even harder, because you have to decide yourself which one to prioritize.

The purpose of triage is to walk into the standup able to say "I've looked at it, the hypothesis is this, here's when we expect a fix," rather than asking "What happened?". Here's a hypothetical example from a logistics customer, starting with the most urgent item:

分拣 — 周一，客户站会（9:00）之前
来源：客户 Slack（4 条），客户 Jira（1 个）

[P0] 今早的库存报表是空的
  影响：运营团队今早无法确认订单
  假设：昨晚的 ETL 任务读取新表时失败
  下一步：查看任务日志，在站会上给出预计时间

The other two items are lighter, plus one that needs the customer to make a call:

[P1] 希望在看板上加一列“发货仓库”
  影响：更方便，但不会卡住任何人
  下一步：在站会上问清谁在用、用来做什么决定
[P2] 询问如何导出 CSV
  下一步：发文档链接，不必在站会上提

需要客户决定：谁来审批新表的读权限？

Prioritize by three questions: Is anyone blocked, who is blocked, and whose decision is needed to unblock them. The order messages arrived, or how urgent the sender's tone was, are not prioritization criteria.

At the standup: translate what the customer says into tasks

According to Blockchain Council, in meetings with customers, the FDE's job is to turn requests like "we need better visibility into risk" into concrete technical tasks. Standups are where such statements appear most often, often sandwiched between two progress updates and easy to miss.

Continuing the hypothetical example above, the conversation might go like this:

运营负责人：这周运营需要更清楚地看到延迟交付的风险。FDE：现在你们在哪里跟踪风险？运营负责人：一个 Excel 文件，每周五下午有人手动更新。FDE：如果能更早看到，你们会做什么不一样的事：换车、提醒客户，还是换仓库？运营负责人：换发货仓库。

FDE：要及时换仓库，你们需要提前多久知道？运营负责人：至少 24 小时。

→ 任务：至少提前 24 小时预警可能延迟的订单，按仓库分组
→ 接收人：各仓库的班组长
→ 完成标准：班组长在订单截止时间前收到预警

The whole exchange takes about a minute. You don't design solutions at the standup; you only ask enough to know who will act, what they'll do, and how much lead time is needed. The rest goes into the 15-minute follow-up right after the meeting, then you write the tasks up and send them back to the customer for confirmation.

Internal standups still matter, just with a changed role

Putting the customer's rhythm first doesn't mean skipping your own company's standup. In internal meetings, you bring back the customer's blockers and make clear what you need from the product or platform teams.

Blockchain Council describes the FDE's working rhythm as a cycle: triage, requirements discovery, building, testing, training, writing documentation, then repeat. Mapped onto a day, the cycle starts with triage notes before 9:00, moves into requirements discovery at the standup, and only then comes opening the editor.

You can practice this sequence in your current job. Every morning, read all requests received since the previous evening and write a three-level triage. In meetings, lead with the P0s, with hypotheses and estimated timelines attached, then watch for vague statements and keep asking until you know who will do what differently.

After the meeting, write the tasks up and send them out. Before the end of the day, check that what you did maps back to the morning's blockers.

Five common mistakes

First, attending the customer's standup as a bystander: listening, taking notes, but picking nothing up. Customers will quickly treat you as an outsider. Second, bringing your own company's agenda into the customer's meeting, like reporting internal sprint progress, when the customer just wants to know when the inventory report will run again.

Third, triaging in the order messages arrived, or handling whoever pushed hardest first. Fourth, debugging at the standup, dragging a 15-minute meeting to 45 minutes and wasting the whole customer team's time.

The fifth is the hardest to spot: you understood the requirement in your head but didn't write it down and send it to the customer for confirmation, only to discover on Friday that the two sides understood it differently.

How to show this on your resume

When reading FDE job descriptions, watch for phrases like "on-site," "% travel," and "embedded with customer teams." They tell you whose schedule you'll live by. The number of on-site customer days stated in the JD is the fastest way to imagine what a real week in the role looks like.

On your resume, instead of writing "collaborated with stakeholders," be specific: which customer team or internal users you met with daily, what kinds of requests you triaged, and which vague requirement you turned into which feature. Expect to be grilled on this in interviews, so pick a story where you can explain every detail.

Anyone can attend a meeting. What's worth practicing is leaving the customer's meeting holding exactly the right work.
