How to Identify and Manage Scope Creep in FDE Projects

Original title: FDE 项目范围蔓延怎么判断和管理

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide addresses how FDEs should distinguish scope creep from legitimate requirement discovery. Drawing on Tandem, TimeTackle, Baytech Consulting, and FDE Academy, it argues that neither freezing the SOW with paid change orders nor accepting every request works. Instead, it proposes three tests—goal, adjustment, and documentation—applied to any request over a day of work, plus same-day written records of each decision (approve, reject, or defer), to protect margins and prevent tech debt.

Key points

  • Neither freezing the SOW nor accepting everything serves FDE work.
  • Three tests: goal, adjustment, and documentation, for requests over a day.
  • A good SOW states exclusions and objective completion criteria upfront.
  • Every major change gets a same-day written note and one of three decisions.
  • Silently absorbed scope is a common source of tech debt.

Say yes to everything and your margins get eaten alive; say no to everything and customers have no reason not to buy the standard product.

Key Takeaways

  • Freezing the statement of work (SOW) and charging for every change doesn't fit the way FDEs work; but saying yes to everything erodes margins and delays launch.
  • Don't ask whether requirements have changed; ask instead: is it clarifying an outcome the customer has already paid for, or adding a new one? Does it change timeline and resources? Is there documentation to check it against?
  • For every major change, write a brief written summary the same day, ending in one of three decisions: approve, reject, or defer.

Imagine you're in week three of a project. The invoice-recognition AI agent you deployed for the client is running on real data, and their operations lead is very happy with it. Then she makes another request: "Could you also have it cross-check against our supplier contracts while you're at it? It shouldn't take much time."

The success or failure of many Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) projects hinges on exactly this kind of question. Say yes, and you may have just taken on two weeks of unpaid work. Say no, and you may have just closed a door—a door the customer opened precisely so you could understand their problems more deeply.

This chapter covers how to answer this question with sound reasoning, rather than gut feeling or social pressure.

Why Can't FDEs Just Freeze the SOW?

Tandem describes in its blog that FDEs spend roughly half their time with the customer understanding the problem, and the other half building a bespoke solution for that customer. In this model, requirements changing mid-project is the norm, because sitting next to the customer inherently generates new understanding.

That's also why, according to Tandem, the standard practice in professional services—freezing the SOW and charging for every change—doesn't suit FDE work. Swinging to the other extreme is just as dangerous, and both mistakes have very concrete consequences.

If you classify every change as "requirements discovery," the project gradually swallows chunks of work nobody ever priced, until margins are exhausted and the launch date keeps slipping. If you treat every change as scope creep, you turn the customer into a user of a standardized product—when they chose you precisely because you would tailor things to them.

Replace Intuition with Three Questions

Tandem proposes a set of tests to replace the question "have requirements changed?" The following three are enough to handle the example in this chapter.

The outcome test asks: is the new request clarifying an outcome the customer has already paid for, or adding a new outcome? The adjustment test asks: does the request change timeline, cost, or resources without a corresponding adjustment?

The documentation test asks: can you point to what the originally agreed scope was, what was said, and what was delivered?

Tandem recommends running every request that requires more than a day of work through these tests. That threshold is sensible: small tasks get done directly to keep the collaboration smooth; large ones must go through a filter.

Good Scope Is Written Before Day One

The documentation test only works if there's something to check against. So preventing scope creep starts when you write the scope, not when a new request arrives.

An article from TimeTackle emphasizes that a good SOW must clearly state what you will not do, without softening it with diplomatic language. The "not doing" list is often just as important as the deliverables list. This scope should be signed off by all relevant parties before work begins.

Baytech Consulting adds another point: every feature and every milestone needs objective criteria for confirming it is "done."

Using the invoice project from earlier as an example, you could write: "The agent can correctly extract amounts, dates, and vendor codes from the sample invoice set provided by the customer; out of scope: contract cross-checking, payment approval, handwritten invoices."

A Complete Example

Suppose you receive two requests in the same week. Request A: "The agent misreads skewed invoices when scanning; please fix it." Request B: the contract cross-check mentioned at the start of this chapter.

For Request A, the outcome test's answer is clear: skewed scans still fall within the invoice set the customer provided, so this is clarifying an already-paid-for outcome. It may take a few days, but it's still within the plan for meeting the signed "done" criteria, so the adjustment test doesn't trigger either.

The documentation test also passes, because the scope document explicitly states which dataset is covered. This is requirements discovery—go do it.

Request B is a different story. Contract cross-checking is a new outcome, sits right on the "not doing" list, and unless time or headcount is added, it will delay the launch. This is scope creep—but that doesn't mean it must be rejected. It needs to be put on the table and decided openly.

The change control process TimeTackle describes requires analyzing the impact on timeline, budget, and technical risk before making a decision. Baytech has every request go through a standard form and records the result: approve, reject, or defer to the next phase. Combining the two, your change log might look like this:

FieldRequest ARequest B
DescriptionRecognize skewed scanned invoicesCross-check against supplier contracts
Outcome testClarifies an already-paid-for outcomeAdds a new outcome
Adjustment testWithin the plan for meeting "done" criteriaDelays launch unless resources are added
Documentation testConsistent with the signed datasetOn the "not doing" list
ImpactA few days, low riskTimeline, budget, and technical risk need separate assessment
DecisionApproved, start immediatelyDeferred to phase two, pending customer confirmation

Send this table, and you're no longer telling the operations lead "no"—you're saying "yes, here's the cost, here's the timeline."

Write It Down the Same Day, Even If It's Just a Message

According to FDE Academy, strong FDEs turn every major scope change into a brief written summary the same day, even if it's just an informal note.

A message like this is enough: "Summary of this afternoon's meeting: contract cross-checking moved to phase two; we'll send an estimate on Monday—does that work for you?"

The reason to write it immediately is technical debt. FDE Academy also points out that technical debt often arises when expanded scope is quietly absorbed rather than formally acknowledged. The reason is easy to understand: if the extra work is never acknowledged, the original deadline doesn't change, and what usually gets sacrificed in the end is code quality.

When you have to take a shortcut, write one sentence on the spot explaining what was skipped and why. For example: "Skipped timeout retries for the accounting API because the customer needs a demo on Friday; must be added back before launch." That one sentence can save whoever picks up the work—and sometimes yourself three months later.

Common Mistakes

The most common mistake is running the tests in your head without writing anything down, leaving you with no evidence when a dispute arises. The second is listing only the work to be done while leaving the "not doing" list blank, so every new request looks "probably in scope."

The third is treating every change as an enemy. Request A in the example is exactly the kind of feedback that makes the product better; rejecting it means throwing away the very reason the customer hired an FDE. The last is leaving decisions in limbo: every request must have one and only one status—approved, rejected, or deferred.

Where to Practice This Skill, and How to Show It

Practicing doesn't require the FDE title. If you work at an outsourcing firm or on a product team, every ticket from a PM or customer asking "could you also add this?" is a chance to practice the three tests.

When reading job descriptions for FDE or Solutions Engineer roles, look for content about working directly with customers or managing change requests; that's where a well-prepared relevant story is most useful.

On your resume, don't write "collaborated with customers"—write a sentence with a result, such as how you moved an unplanned request to a later phase while still launching on schedule.

In an interview, tell a story about a time you told a customer "not now" and the relationship actually got better. That story is more persuasive than any self-assessment like "strong communication skills."

Customers will always ask for more, and a good FDE project should naturally attract those requests. Experts don't avoid them: they respond quickly, put things in writing, and each time accurately point out which outcome the customer actually bought.
