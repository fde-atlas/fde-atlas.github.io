How to Implement Generative AI | 5 Steps to Move Beyond PoC Stalls and the Right Order to Avoid Failure

Original title: 生成AI導入の進め方｜PoC止まりを越える5つのステップと失敗しない順序

FDX株式会社 · 2026-06-05 · 24 min read

Summary

This FDX article argues that most generative AI initiatives fail not technically but procedurally, getting stuck at the proof-of-concept stage. Citing MIT NANDA research that only about 5% of enterprise GenAI projects reach production, it identifies four structural gaps—missing workflow integration, retrofitted governance, unusable tools, and no internal owner. The authors propose a five-step sequence: narrow the scope, define success criteria and ROI upfront, redesign the workflow and deploy to production, embed and internalize operations, then scale horizontally. Each step has a quantified gate, and skipping steps guarantees failure. A self-diagnostic method and FDX's AX Factory methodology are also introduced.

Key points

  • PoC stagnation is a process problem, not a technology problem.
  • Five ordered steps: Scope, Define, Deploy First, Embed, Scale.
  • Each step has a numeric gate; skipping steps causes failure.
  • MIT NANDA: only ~5% of GenAI projects reach production.
  • Diagnose by tracing back to which gate was missed.

Why so many generative AI adoptions get stuck at "PoC"

The most common failure in generative AI adoption is not a technical one. It is "PoC stagnation": confirming at the PoC (proof of concept) stage that "this looks usable" and then stopping without ever reaching production.

Surveys show that only a small fraction of enterprise generative AI projects pass PoC and reach results in production (MIT NANDA's "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025" reports about 5% reach production, among other industry surveys). What the chart shows is a structure where many projects reach the validation stage, but the success rate plunges at the production deployment stage. This article calls this plunge zone the "PoC valley."

Why does the valley arise? Because PoC and production operations look at fundamentally different things. Let's go through them in order.

PoC asks "can it be done?" Production asks "will it keep being used?"

The purpose of a PoC is to verify technical feasibility. You test under limited conditions whether "generative AI can summarize meeting minutes" or "can provide first-line responses to inquiries." This usually works. Today's generative AI is smart enough.

But production operations ask something different: "Will frontline staff actually keep using it every day within their own work?" Who uses it and when, how quality is assured, what the fallback is when it fails, and who drives improvement. A PoC never asks these questions. So even when the PoC succeeds, the design for production operations remains blank. This blank is the true nature of the PoC valley.

Four structural gaps that block production

When projects that passed PoC stall at production, the reasons almost always fall into the following four. None are technical; they are gaps in design.

  • No design for embedding into business processes: The PoC only checked "does it look usable," without designing how it integrates into workflows—who uses it, when, for what purpose, and how to fall back when it fails.
  • Permissions, auditing, and quality assurance bolted on afterward: The access controls, audit logs, and output quality checks needed for production are worked out only after the PoC ends, causing rework.
  • Frontline users can't or won't use it: The builders can run it, but frontline staff walk away saying "this isn't it" or "I don't know how to use it." Training and operational documentation were never part of the design.
  • No decision on who will operate and improve it next: After the staff or vendors who ran the PoC leave, no one takes over operations, and within a few months it is abandoned.

What these four share is that all of them "could have been prevented by designing for production from the start." The moment you start a PoC with a "let's just try it" mindset and postpone production design, the trajectory into the valley is set.

The "add a tool" mindset mass-produces PoC stagnation

There is a deeper cause. Many companies try to simply add generative AI to existing work as a "convenient tool." They contract for a chat tool, distribute it to employees saying "give it a try," and change nothing about business processes. With this approach, the benefits stay confined to individuals' working hours, and after confirming "looks usable" at PoC, the effort runs out of steam. Why adding a tool alone is not enough is covered in detail in "What is AX (AI Transformation)?." This article focuses on what comes after: "so how do we proceed?"

In short, PoC stagnation is not a matter of luck or technical skill; it is a matter of approach. With the wrong order, even an excellent team falls into the valley; with the right order and gates, the valley can be structurally avoided. The next chapter makes that order concrete.

The 5 steps of generative AI adoption

To turn generative AI adoption into results, proceed through the following five steps, in this order. The "gates" (criteria for moving to the next step) placed at each step are critical. If you move on without meeting a gate, you will inevitably fall into the valley somewhere further along.

The 5 steps proceed as follows: narrow the scope (Scope) → define success criteria and ROI (Define) → rebuild each workflow and deploy to production (Deploy First) → embed it in operations and internalize it (Embed) → roll out horizontally (Scale). Each step can only be started after passing the gate of the previous step.

Step 1: Narrow the Scope (Scope)

The first thing to do is narrow the target down to a single business area. The posture of "using generative AI across the whole company" is a classic recipe for failure. It becomes scattershot, no one takes responsibility, results cannot be measured, and the champions drift away and the project stalls.

The criteria for narrowing down are clear. Choose business processes that are routine, high-volume, have relatively clear decision criteria, and whose impact can be measured quantitatively. Specific candidates include first-line inquiry handling, meeting minutes creation and CRM updates, quote preparation, document and draft generation, and data aggregation and classification. These are areas where generative AI tends to deliver results and where outcomes are easy to see in numbers, making them well suited to creating the first success experience.

Conversely, avoid starting with processes where decision criteria are vague, work is highly dependent on individuals, and impact is hard to show in numbers. Even if you achieve results there, no evidence remains to convince the company that "it worked," and no momentum for the next step is generated.

Gate (criteria for moving forward): Is the chosen business area a process whose impact can be measured quantitatively? Pass if results can be expressed in numbers such as "minutes saved," "cases processed," or "percentage reduction in quality errors." If not, reselect the area.

The stumbling point is being greedy and trying to roll out company-wide all at once. The wider the scope, the heavier the consensus-building and design become, and the first step never gets taken. The iron rule of generative AI adoption is to start with a small success in one area and expand from there.

Step 2: Define Success Criteria and ROI First (Define)

Once the area is narrowed, define success criteria and return on investment (ROI) before production deployment. The order is critically important. Do not measure impact after building; decide in numbers "what counts as success" before building.

Two things must be defined. One is business KPIs: metrics suited to the target work, such as time savings, quality improvement, number of cases processed, cost reduction, and decision-making speed. The other is the current value (baseline) of those metrics. Unless you measure the current state, such as "first-line inquiry handling currently takes an average of 15 minutes per case," claiming "it got faster" after adoption lacks persuasiveness.

At the same time, decide in advance the criteria for withdrawing and expanding. Hold "if we don't exceed this number in three months, we withdraw" and "if we reach this level, we expand to the next area" before starting. With these in place, the project can be driven by numbers rather than emotions or politics.

Gate (criteria for moving forward): Are the criteria for judging withdrawal or expansion expressed as concrete numbers? Pass if you can say "expand if the KPI is X or above, withdraw if below Y" rather than "keep going if it goes well."

The stumbling point is putting off the design of impact measurement. If you proceed with "let's get it running first and think about impact later," you miss the baseline and become unable to prove results afterward. Without baseline figures, you cannot speak to the magnitude of improvement or secure additional budget. Define is an unglamorous step, but it matters most for crossing the PoC valley.

Step 3: Rebuild Each Workflow and Deploy to Production (Deploy First)

This is the core of the 5 steps and where the PoC valley gapes open. What you must do is not stop at PoC, but rebuild the business process itself and put it into production operation.

"Rebuilding each workflow" means not adding tools to existing work, but restructuring the work with AI as the premise. If AI handles first-line response, design the response flow, escalation criteria, quality checks, exception handling, permission management, and audit logs as one integrated whole, so that the front line can actually run it within daily operations. Working backward from production deployment, launch business redesign, AI capabilities, data, and talent simultaneously.

The "Deploy First" posture of getting things running in the field first is important. In the fast-moving field of generative AI, it is more rational to deploy to production small and fast and improve while running than to spend six months building a perfect plan. Only by running in real operations do improvement requests that never emerge from desk studies surface from the field. The standard practice is to leave existing core systems intact and insert the AI layer non-destructively. Assuming a large-scale overhaul pushes production deployment years into the future and delays results.

Gate (criteria for moving forward): Is the front line actually using it within daily operations? Pass if it is used daily as part of real work, not just demos or trials, and business KPIs have started to move. ★This is the PoC valley, the point where the most projects fall away.

The stumbling point is being satisfied once the PoC confirms "it seems usable" and stopping without production operation design. There is a deep chasm between a successful technical validation and continued use in the field. Whether this chasm can be fully bridged through business redesign and operational design determines the success or failure of generative AI adoption.

Step 4: Embed in the Field and Internalize (Embed)

Even after production deployment, it is not over. The next task is to make the workflow stick in the field and reach a state where internal staff alone can operate and improve it.

Embedding has two layers. One is that frontline users can keep using it without confusion: provide training, operational documentation, and troubleshooting procedures. The other is internalization: making it so that internal staff can operate, improve, and if necessary rebuild the AI-embedded workflow themselves. Rather than simply increasing the number of "people who can use the tool," cultivate "people who can redesign, operate, and improve workflows with AI" within the company. This is decisively important.

By incorporating internal capability building into success metrics, you can structurally break the dependence on external support. The difference between a project that stops the moment the vendor leaves and one that keeps running is determined by whether this step was designed in from the start.

Gate (criteria for moving to the next step): Can operation and improvement run with internal staff alone, without external vendors? Pass if business does not stop even when the vendor leaves and improvement cycles are running with field-led ownership.

The pitfall is treating implementation as the finish line and letting improvement stop. Generative AI, models, and business processes all keep changing. The moment improvement stops, the AI you worked to embed starts becoming obsolete. Embed is the step that transforms generative AI adoption from a one-off project into a sustained internal capability.

Step 5: Scale across the organization (Scale)

Once production operation and internal capability building are established in one area, the final step is to spread that pattern to other departments and business processes. This is where generative AI adoption shifts from "a one-off success" to "a mechanism that can be replicated in-house."

The key to scaling is to turn the business process redesign, AI implementation, and training methods gained in the first area into templates, leaving behind a reusable "playbook" that can be reproduced in other roles and business units. If you redesign the rollout from scratch every time, neither speed nor cost will work out. Use the playbook established in the first area, with internal champions leading the transfer to the second and third areas.

Gate (criteria for moving to the next step): Is there a "playbook" that can be reproduced in other departments? Pass if the approach from the first area has been articulated as procedures and templates, and another person can reproduce it following the same steps.

The pitfall is that the initial success remains person-dependent and things stall at one area. "It worked because that person was there" does not scale. Consciously leaving behind a reusable playbook from the first area onward—asking "can this be reproduced in the second area?"—is what makes Scale possible. Only when you reach organization-wide scaling does generative AI adoption translate into company-wide competitive advantage.

What happens when you skip steps

The five steps have meaning precisely in their order. Each step builds on the gate of the previous one, and skipping any one of them will inevitably cause a breakdown downstream. Here is what happens when each step is skipped.

  • If you skip Step 1 (Scope): The initiative launches company-wide without narrowing down targets and becomes scattered. No one takes ownership, effects cannot be measured, and champions drift away until the project stalls. This is the most typical entry point to failure.
  • If you skip Step 2 (Define): You move to production with neither success criteria nor a baseline. Even if it works, you can only evaluate it as "it feels faster," and cannot prove results. Additional budget cannot be secured, and scaling cannot be justified.
  • If you skip Step 3 (Deploy First): You stop at the point where a PoC confirms it "seems usable." This is the PoC valley itself—without business process redesign or operational design, it never goes to production and is left abandoned.
  • If you skip Step 4 (Embed): You can deploy to production, but everything stops the moment the vendor or a specific person leaves. Improvement stalls, and the implemented processes gradually become obsolete. Adoption without internal capability building eventually returns you to square one.
  • If you skip Step 5 (Scale): The success in one area remains a one-off. It never reaches company-wide impact commensurate with the investment, and ends as "that one department did well."

What deserves particular caution is the pattern of skipping Steps 1 and 2 and jumping straight into Step 3 (production deployment). This happens frequently in the field. When you rush to production with a "just build it and run it" mindset, you start running without target focus or success criteria, and ultimately fall to the bottom of the PoC valley. Even if it seems like a detour, carefully working through Scope → Define is, as a result, the fastest path to production operation.

The importance of maintaining the order is reflected in the fact that each step's gate presupposes "the deliverables of the previous step." The Define gate (quantitative decision criteria) cannot be established unless the scope has been narrowed in Scope. The Deploy First gate (is the field actually using it?) cannot be judged unless success criteria were set in Define. The gates are not independent; they are chained together. That is exactly why skipping does not work.

Where is your company stuck? | Diagnostic perspectives

When generative AI adoption is not going well, the first thing needed is to identify "which of the five steps your company is stuck at." Misjudging your current position leads to investing in the wrong remedies. For example, if you are stuck at Step 3, adding a new tool will not get you over the valley. Use the following questions to self-diagnose your company's current position.

  • Signs of being stuck at Step 1: The order has been given to "adopt generative AI company-wide," but no decision has been made on which business processes to start with. Multiple departments are experimenting individually, but there is no organization-wide definition of targets.
  • Signs of being stuck at Step 2: The target business process has been decided, but "what counts as success" is not defined numerically. The current baseline has not been measured, and there is no means to prove post-adoption effects.
  • Signs of being stuck at Step 3: PoCs and demos have been run many times, but the field has not reached a state of using it in daily operations. It remains stuck at "seems usable," with production operation design (permissions, quality assurance, exception handling) still blank. This is the most common and the deepest valley.
  • Signs of being stuck at Step 4: It is running in production, but only with a specific vendor or person involved. Improvement has stalled—the system was adopted but has not been maintained since.
  • Signs of being stuck at Step 5: Success was achieved in one area, but expansion to other departments is not progressing. The success is person-dependent and has not been articulated as a reproducible playbook.

Many companies believe they are at Step 3, when in fact they skipped Steps 1 and 2. It is not uncommon for the true reason production deployment stalls to be insufficient scoping or missing success criteria. That is why you need to diagnose by tracing back to "which gate was failed," rather than looking at the superficial "where things stopped."

There are two common pitfalls when diagnosing on your own. One is assuming that a lack of technology or budget is the root cause. People look for causes externally—"if only we had a more powerful model" or "if only we had more budget"—but the real cause is often a design problem, such as missing business process redesign or absent success criteria. The other is taking frontline complaints of "it's hard to use" at face value. Behind that difficulty often lie structural gaps, such as the tool not being integrated into business workflows in the first place, or no quality assurance mechanism. The key to correct diagnosis is to see through the surface of the complaints and identify the gate that was never passed.

A practical tip for improving diagnostic accuracy is to walk through the five steps in reverse order. Start with "Have you expanded horizontally to other departments? (Step 5)," and if not, ask "Have you completed in-housing in one area? (Step 4)"; if that is also doubtful, ask "Are frontline staff using it in daily work? (Step 3)," tracing the gates upstream to find the first point where the answer is "No." That is where your company is truly stuck. Working backward from downstream helps you avoid misjudging your current position due to assumptions.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1. How do you move from PoC to production?

A. The only solution is to design for production operation from the PoC stage onward. Simply confirming "it seems usable" in a PoC cannot cross the valley. Integration into business processes—who uses it, when, for what purpose, and how to substitute when it fails; permissions, auditing, and quality assurance; frontline training; and an owner for operations and improvement—must be included in the design from the start. By carefully working through Step 2 (defining success criteria and ROI) and Step 3 (production deployment with processes rebuilt around AI) in this article, the gap between PoC and production can be structurally closed. For projects already stuck at the PoC stage, the standard approach is to start with re-diagnosing the baseline and rebuild the plan with production deployment as the premise.

Q2. Which business processes should you start with?

A. It is easier to succeed by starting with processes that are standardized, high in volume, have relatively clear decision criteria, and whose effects can be measured quantitatively. Specifically, candidates include first-line inquiry handling, meeting minutes creation and CRM updates, document and draft generation, quote preparation, and data aggregation and classification. These are areas where generative AI tends to deliver results and where outcomes are easy to show in numbers, making it easy to create an initial success story. Conversely, if you start with processes that have ambiguous decision criteria, are highly dependent on individuals, and whose effects are hard to express numerically, then even if you achieve results, you will have no evidence left to convince the rest of the company that it "worked." The standard approach is to establish a success pattern in one area and then roll it out horizontally.

Q3. Which is better: in-house development or outsourcing?

A. A hybrid approach of "launch with outsourcing, run with in-house capabilities" tends to produce the best results. If you try to do everything in-house from the start, the launch takes time and you lack the know-how. On the other hand, if you hand everything over to an external vendor, everything stops the moment the vendor leaves. The ideal model is for external supporters to lead the business redesign and advanced implementation, while in-house internalization candidates work in parallel and accumulate know-how, with in-house staff becoming the operational leaders six months to a year later. It is important to include "development of internalization candidates" and "handover completion criteria" as success metrics at the contract stage. For criteria on deciding between outsourcing and in-house development, see "Using an external FDE vs. developing in-house | 5 criteria for management decisions".

Q4. What are the typical implementation period and costs?

A. As a guideline, for each business area, expect roughly 3 to 6 months to reach production deployment and see initial results. The breakdown is: a few weeks to one month for narrowing down the area and defining success criteria (Steps 1-2), 2 to 3 months for production deployment (Step 3), and one to several months for consolidation and internalization (Step 4), confirming improvement in business KPIs for that area. Costs vary depending on the complexity of the target business, the scope of integration with existing systems, and the depth of internalization support, so they cannot be stated uniformly. What matters is not comparing unit prices, but evaluating return on investment in terms of both business impact and the capabilities left behind in-house (internalization). Showing early results in one area, without waiting for company-wide transformation to be complete, is a realistic path that keeps costs down while gaining internal buy-in.

Q5. What scale does "starting small" refer to?

A. The guideline is a scope that can be completed within a single business process and a single team. For example, something like "first-line inquiry handling for the sales department" or "meeting minutes and CRM integration for a specific department" — a scale where the target business, target people, and success metrics fit on a single sheet of paper. Conversely, "company-wide business efficiency" or "cross-departmental transformation" is too large as a first step. The aim of starting small is to reach production deployment quickly, show results in numbers, and gain momentum for the next step. The smaller the scale, the faster the gate decisions become, allowing quicker decisions on whether to pull back or expand. Succeeding in one area and then rolling out that model horizontally ultimately becomes the fastest path to spreading it across the whole company.

Q6. What is the difference between generative AI adoption and AX (AI Transformation)?

A. Generative AI adoption refers to the concrete initiative of executing the 5 steps in this article within a single business area. AX refers to a larger management transformation: expanding that across multiple areas and rebuilding the business, organization, and decision-making itself around AI as the default. In terms of their relationship, repeatedly running the 5 steps of generative AI adoption (especially the horizontal rollout in Step 5) eventually gives rise to AX as a company-wide state. This article focuses on "how to succeed in one area"; the broader company-wide vision is covered in "What is AX (AI Transformation)?".
