How to Write an FDE Resume: Present Deployment Results with Baseline Numbers

Original title: FDE 简历怎么写：用带基线的数字写部署成果

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 6 min read

Summary

This article argues that FDE resumes should present verifiable, baseline-anchored numbers about customer outcomes rather than lists of tools and frameworks. Drawing on hiring guidance from Aced, Palantir, Paraform, and recruiter Christian & Timbers, plus Laszlo Bock's X-Y-Z achievement formula, the author rewrites a tool-list bullet into a concrete result with baseline, measurement window, and confirmation, and warns that every number will be probed in interviews.

Key points

  • Every resume bullet should end in a number, like cutting deployment from 6 weeks to 9 days.
  • Palantir treats languages as baseline requirements and values end-to-end ownership instead.
  • Christian & Timbers demands a starting baseline and measurement period for claims.
  • Interviewers probe where numbers came from, who confirmed them, and what you built.
  • Record baselines in week one of a project, or you cannot quantify impact later.

Recruiters want verifiable deployment results, like "six weeks down to nine days," not a string of tool names.

Key Takeaways

  • Aced's FDE resume guide requires every bullet point to have a number, such as cutting deployment time from 6 weeks to 9 days.
  • Palantir treats Python, Java, and C++ as baseline requirements; what it's really looking for is someone who can drive important projects end to end.
  • Big numbers will always be verified: Christian & Timbers requires candidates to provide the starting baseline and measurement period.

"Cut deployment time from 6 weeks to 9 days." Aced's (formerly Exponent) FDE resume guide uses this sentence as an example, contrasted with vague statements like "improved efficiency." Its advice is straightforward: every bullet point should end with a number.

This sounds like tired resume advice, but it actually reveals how recruiters for Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) read resumes. They're not looking for the person who has used the most tools, but the person who got systems actually running at customer sites and can prove it made a difference.

Many developers, including many in Vietnam, still organize their resumes around tech stacks: what projects they've done, whether they used Spring or FastAPI, Kafka or RabbitMQ. For backend roles, this passes keyword screening; for FDE roles, it hides exactly what recruiters most need to see.

Frameworks Are Just the Minimum Bar

Palantir's Forward Deployed Software Engineer job posting makes its priorities clear. Programming languages are listed under basic requirements: solid coding ability and proficiency in languages such as Python, Java, and C++.

Yet the job description itself is about something else: working directly with customers and driving the execution of important projects from start to finish.

Palantir also lists Ownership as a standalone value, defined as seeing a project through from start to finish, overcoming every obstacle along the way. A list of frameworks proves none of this. It only shows you've touched these tools, not that you finished any work.

Aced makes the same point: FDE recruiters value ownership and customer impact over pure technical complexity.

So in the Skills section, list only skills relevant to the role that you can speak about confidently, not everything you've ever used.

Listing too many backfires in interviews: interviewers will pick exactly the name you know least well to ask about.

Google's Formula Still Works

This approach didn't originate in the FDE world. In 2015, Laszlo Bock, then Google's head of People Operations, said in a Reddit AMA that every accomplishment should be written as "accomplished X, as measured by Y, by doing Z." His overall advice: make the impact of your work clear.

Applied to FDE work, the three parts become more specific. X is the customer-side change: faster processes, fewer errors, or earlier decisions. Y is what you personally built or led. Z is the number—in FDE work there is almost always a "before" and an "after."

Aced's example bullets all follow this template. One bullet about a data pipeline reads: time to first insight reduced from 3 months to 3 weeks. Others measure latency of RAG systems or hours saved by reusable toolkits. Not one starts with a library name.

Rewriting a Bullet Point Step by Step

Imagine a developer at an outsourcing company in Hanoi who just finished a project for a logistics client. His current bullet reads:

"Built a RAG system using LangChain, Pinecone, and FastAPI."

Three tool names, no customer, no result. Now apply Bock's formula. X: the customer support team can look up shipping policies faster. Y: he sat with the team to collect real questions, built the retrieval pipeline, and integrated it into the tools the team uses every day.

Z: suppose each query originally took 4 minutes, and after deployment it takes 40 seconds, with a measurement period of the first 30 days after going to production. Putting the three parts together, the new bullet is:

"Reduced the customer support team's time to look up shipping policies from 4 minutes to 40 seconds per query by collecting the team's real questions, building a retrieval pipeline, and integrating it into their daily tools (measured over the first 30 days after going to production)."

Put the two sentences side by side and the difference is the subject. The old one is about tools; the new one is about the customer's operations team. The 4 minutes, 40 seconds, and 30 days here are purely illustrative—real numbers must come from your own project's logs, tickets, or reports.

LangChain or Pinecone can still stay in the Skills section, as long as you can explain why you chose them. They just aren't the core of this line anymore.

Numbers Will Be Questioned

Many people go wrong here: they assume adding numbers automatically makes a resume more weighty. Numbers help you get the interview, but once you're in the room, they become claims you must defend. The recruiting platform Paraform states plainly that a resume doesn't truly reflect whether someone is fit for an FDE role, so interviewers will dig into every number.

Imagine an interviewer reading the logistics bullet above and pausing at "4 minutes." The first question is almost certainly: where was the baseline measured? Then: who confirmed the post-deployment number? Finally: which parts of this work did you do yourself?

A solid answer might go like this: "The baseline comes from the support team's ticketing tool logs for the two weeks before deployment.

The post-deployment number comes from the same log source, covering the first 30 days after going to production, and the client team lead signed off on the report. I built the retrieval pipeline and integrations myself; a colleague handled the interface."

This answer holds up because it contains exactly three things: the measurement source, the time period, and who confirmed it, plus a clear boundary of the candidate's own work. The executive search firm Christian & Timbers demands precisely this: the bigger the claim, the more it needs a starting baseline and a measurement period.

The firm raises the bar one level higher: an FDE's technical work must land in production workflows with measurable results, and companies should look for people who have deployed agentic AI multiple times, backed by documented financial outcomes. So the next question is likely: have you done this at a second client too?

Laying out each company's hiring criteria in a table shows what each bullet point must answer both on paper and in the interview:

These questions expose three common mistakes that can make a polished bullet collapse in an interview.

Inflated percentages. "300% efficiency improvement" sounds impressive, but one question—"300% relative to what?"—deflates it. Small, real numbers are far safer.

Claiming team results as your own. If you stammer when asked which part you did, the number loses its value. The ownership Palantir looks for means carrying your own work through to the end, not putting your name on collective achievements.

Numbers without baselines. "Reduced processing time by 70%" without saying what it started from or over what period cannot be verified, and the interviewer will treat that line as if it doesn't exist.

Where to start

A common obstacle for engineers at outsourcing companies is having no data at hand, because the data lives with the client. The solution lies in the work itself: in your next project, record the baseline in the first week—for example, how long the current process takes, how many errors it produces, and how many manual steps it requires. Without a "before," there will be no Z later.

When reading FDE job descriptions, watch for phrases like "end-to-end," "working directly with customers," and "production environments." When you see them, order your bullet points around customer outcomes rather than your tech stack. If the description is just a pile of framework names, it may be a backend role in disguise.

Before submitting, reread every number and run it through the three questions above: where was it measured, who confirmed it, and which part was yours. Delete any number you can't fully answer for, because FDE recruiters will keep probing until they get answers.

The best FDE resumes read like a series of delivery records: which client, what changed, how it was measured. The tech stack is something interviewers only ask about after they already want to hear more.
