FDE Customer Expansion: Finding the Second Contract from Delivered Projects

Original title: FDE 客户扩展：从已交付项目发现第二份合同

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide argues that an FDE's second contract is usually hiding inside the first delivered project. Firecrawl's job posting evaluates FDEs on adoption and expansion rather than closed tickets, and Palantir's bootcamp model—over 915 institutions attended—shows expansion opportunities emerge from engineers' workflows. The author proposes a repeatable method: deliver a narrow two-week slice, then track weekly adoption rates, customer workarounds, and reusable code components. These signals are compiled into a table and handed to revenue teams. The article also warns against four mistakes: pitching too early, promising scope yourself, expanding via copy-paste code, and talking only to end users.

Key points

  • Firecrawl evaluates FDEs on adoption and expansion, not closed tickets.
  • Palantir's bootcamps have drawn over 915 institutions and drive larger orders.
  • Three expansion signals: adoption rate, customer workarounds, reusable code.
  • Deliver a narrow two-week slice first; discuss expansion only after adoption stabilizes.
  • Avoid pitching early, promising scope yourself, copy-paste expansion, and user-only communication.

Week six on-site. The workflow you built is running smoothly, the final demo was a hit, and the account manager even thanked the whole team in the group chat. Then you roll off the project, and three months later the contract renews with the scope untouched. Sometimes it doesn't renew at all.

At some companies hiring Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs), this outcome means the job is only half done. Firecrawl's FDE job posting is blunt: the role is evaluated on adoption and expansion, not on the number of tickets closed.

The same posting also requires FDEs to work with the revenue team to turn technical wins into adoption and larger contracts. At such companies, delivering the first project is not the finish line.

If your goal is an FDE role, whether you can spot the second contract within the first project is exactly what separates you from a good outsourced engineer. This skill can be learned, and it starts with reading the data, not with being a smooth talker.

Why is "expansion" an engineer's job?

Palantir makes no secret of this strategy. Ryan Taylor, the company's Chief Revenue Officer, said on the Q1 2024 earnings call that the company would keep bringing on new customers and expand those relationships as the product gains traction.

The entry point is the bootcamp. According to Taylor, more than 915 organizations had participated by that point. Since late 2023, Palantir has described its AIP bootcamps as a way to get real workflows running on customer data in five days or less.

The company has also said that bootcamps lead to larger orders and shorten the time needed for contract conversion and subsequent expansion. If the entry point is a workflow running on real data, it stands to reason that expansion opportunities also emerge from that same workflow—that is, from the engineer's work.

Sales doesn't know that the warehouse team exports the same Excel file every Monday morning, or that the ERP (enterprise resource planning) connector you wrote could be used by three other departments. You know, because you're inside the customer's systems.

The order cannot be reversed. fde.academy's deployment handbook warns that trust lost in the first few weeks is extremely costly to rebuild. The handbook also recommends delivering a working narrow slice in about two weeks before broadening the scope. Nail one small thing first; talk about expansion later.

An example: from invoice reconciliation to procurement

Imagine a logistics company. Your first project is an AI agent: it reads supplier invoices, matches them against purchase orders in the ERP, and pushes discrepancies to finance for approval. The scope is deliberately narrow: only domestic freight invoices, serving only one finance team.

Four weeks after launch, the first thing to do is open the logs. Suppose 20 accountants have access and 15 use it weekly: that's 75% adoption. That base is solid enough to start discussing expansion. If only 4 of the 20 are using it, your job is to fix adoption first, not to sell more.

The second signal is workarounds. You notice that every Monday, the procurement lead has an accountant export the discrepancy list to negotiate with suppliers who always invoice incorrectly. This is a user the contract never envisioned, and he found the product on his own.

The third signal is in the code. The invoice parser and the ERP connector are already written. International freight invoices run through the same ERP; only the formats and currencies differ.

As a result, the second slice costs far less than the first. This is exactly the mechanism described in the fde.academy handbook: one-off custom work gradually solidifies into reusable functionality.

The next step is the review meeting with the customer, where how you ask matters enormously. Don't open with "we can do the international part too." Ask about the problem itself:

"I noticed you export the discrepancy file every week. What do you use it for? How long does it take to handle today?"

If the answer is that it takes two afternoons a week to manually piece the numbers together, you now have a quantifiable problem. Take it back to the revenue team rather than committing to scope on the spot.

Do it yourself: five steps

The first step happens during scoping: pick a narrow slice and write down the adjacent workflows you deliberately excluded. That list is your future expansion map, and it lets you refuse scope creep without damaging the relationship.

After launch, measure adoption weekly, defined as actual users divided by users granted access—not features delivered. Also record every workaround: exported files, manual copy-paste steps, and the people who frequently ask others to pull results for them.

When writing code, separate the generic parts (connectors, parsers, evals) from the parts specific to this customer. It's this investment that makes the second slice cheaper, and it's what the product team needs to standardize it into a feature.

Once adoption stabilizes, fill in the signal table, validate it line by line with questions about the problem itself, then hand it to the account manager or the revenue team. Budgets, contracts, and signatories are their responsibility. Yours is providing the technical evidence and effort estimates.

Finally, once the second slice is approved, repeat the same loop: narrow the scope, deliver fast, measure, and look for the next signal.

Mistakes that make opportunities disappear

The first is raising it too early. Pitching expansion when only 4 of 20 people are using the product just makes the customer feel you care more about selling than about them, and trust starts to erode.

The second is committing to scope yourself. A single "we can add that quickly" in a meeting can disrupt the revenue team's pricing strategy, and in the end your team ends up doing a pile of extra work nobody pays for.

The third is harder to spot: expanding by copy-pasting custom code. The second slice's cost doesn't drop at all, technical debt doubles, and by the third slice nobody wants to touch it anymore.

The last is talking only to the users. The accountants loving your product doesn't mean the procurement lead knows it exists. The person with the problem and the person with the budget are often not the same person.

Putting this skill on your resume

Demand is hot: Indeed data shows that Applied AI job postings grew more than 800% from January to September 2025. When reading job descriptions, watch for phrases like "adoption," "expansion," or "partner with revenue team"—they tell you what criteria the company will use to evaluate you.

On your resume, replace "built an invoice reconciliation pipeline" with a sentence that carries numbers: "Got 15 of 20 accountants using it weekly within four weeks; identified the procurement department's need and reused the existing connector to open a second slice." Whether you do contracting or internal product work, every project has usage logs and workarounds worth talking about.

Doing the first project well is necessary, but not sufficient. Starting with your next project, practice spotting the second project before the customer thinks of it.
