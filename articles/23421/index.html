How to Prove Your FDE Writing Skills: Interview and Portfolio Preparation

Original title: FDE 写作能力怎么证明：面试与作品集准备

FDE Times · 2026-11-18 · 5 min read

Summary

Key takeaways

  • Tungsten Automation's FDE job posting compresses writing ability into a single vague requirement, while the responsibilities section calls for writing custom technical documentation for customers.
  • According to Aced's guide, OpenAI's recruiter screen evaluates your ability to summarize complex work for a non-technical audience; FDE Academy quotes a saying: a 70% solution with good documentation beats a 100% solution without it.
  • The evidence you should present is writing that others can use, such as an incident report or a decision record, not an adjective on your resume.

Tungsten Automation's Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) job posting, published in July 2026, lumps writing together with verbal communication and presentations on one line, with just one word attached: "excellent." The responsibilities section of the same posting is far more specific: the FDE is to run train-the-trainer sessions, write custom technical documentation for customers, and share best practices.

This is just one job posting, but it reveals a gap worth watching for in any FDE hiring process: the requirements list gives you a single adjective, while the responsibilities describe an entire deliverable. The posting doesn't say how candidates should prove that adjective, so the burden of proof falls on you.

Interview Kickstart's FDE skills preparation roadmap states explicitly that your portfolio, resume, and interview answers must all contain concrete evidence of how you have applied a skill. A line on your resume saying "strong communication skills" can't be scored; a real document can.

One requirement, three jobs

The same Tungsten posting requires the FDE to explain complex technical concepts to frontline engineering teams and C-level executives. Put that together with the line about writing, verbal communication, and presentations, and "communication" here covers at least three tasks: explaining things to engineers, presenting to management, and writing documents that will outlast the conversation.

Only the third one leaves behind an artifact the hiring team can read before meeting you; the other two only surface during the interview. To find out how you'll be assessed, look at the interview process — the job posting won't tell you.

Which interview round listens to you explain?

According to Aced's (formerly Exponent) OpenAI FDE interview prep guide, the recruiter screen examines your motivation for doing forward deployed work and your hands-on experience building products with LLMs. Communication is one of the scoring criteria in this round: whether you can clearly summarize a complex piece of work for a non-technical audience.

This is a verbal exercise, but the difficulty is exactly the same as in writing: deciding which points to keep, which to drop, and in what order to present them. A summary written down before the call becomes the skeleton of your answer.

In an article analyzing why candidates fail FDE interviews, FDE Academy quotes a blunt observation: people who can write code but cannot explain their thinking to an outsider will struggle badly. The same article quotes another source: a 70% solution with good documentation beats a 100% solution without it.

That sounds counterintuitive, but it makes sense from the reader's perspective. With a documented 70%, the reader knows which parts work, which are missing, why you cut them, and what comes next. With an undocumented 100%, the reader can only guess; on a customer site, guessing means calling you back.

The handoff is complete only when a stranger can take over

PostHog's FDE handbook sets a clearer standard than any adjective: a deliverable is only done when a stranger on the customer's team can pick it up and use it immediately without anyone explaining it. At handoff, the FDE must write down what was done and why.

The handbook also gives a short example: post a weekly status note in a channel shared with the customer, outlining the plan for the coming week so that nobody is surprised on Monday morning. Interview Kickstart describes the same habit at the decision level: record the decision itself, the reasoning behind it, and the expected impact on the customer.

This is exactly the custom technical documentation Tungsten mentions, and PostHog adds a clear test for whether the documentation measures up. It follows that the strongest evidence is writing identical to the work you would do after joining.

What a good incident report looks like

Rootly's postmortem template is a good starting point. It has six sections: summary, timeline, impact, contributing factors, what went well, and follow-up actions. Rootly deliberately omits a separate root cause section, instead recording contributing conditions to avoid forcing every incident into a single cause.

A technical writing study guide for FDEs on Fatskills recommends a blameless postmortem, with the root cause stated in the opening paragraph. The two recommendations can be reconciled: the first sentence of the summary states the main cause in plain language, while the contributing factors section lists all the conditions that combined to cause the incident.

Here's a hypothetical example. After a server's time zone was changed, a nightly data sync job ran twice, duplicating records in the customer's reports. A note saying "fixed the duplicate bug" proves nothing about your writing ability.

Following Rootly's framework, the first sentence of the summary states: after the time zone change, the job schedule drifted, causing the job to run twice. The timeline records each point in time and the hypotheses you ruled out. The impact section states which reports were wrong, who was using them, and for how long.

The contributing factors section points to both the scheduling issue and the job's lack of protection against duplicate writes. The follow-up actions section states clearly who does what to ensure the failure doesn't recur. A manager on the customer side can understand it, and a customer-side engineer can handle a recurrence independently: PostHog's stranger test, passed.

How to put the evidence into your application

Start with your resume. Delete "excellent communication skills" and replace it with a sentence with a concrete outcome — for example, that you wrote handoff documentation for a system taken over by another team, and, if possible, attach a link to a redacted version.

In your portfolio, include at least one decision record per project, written in the three parts Interview Kickstart lists: the decision, the reasoning, and the expected impact. Add an incident report written in the six-section framework above, and whoever reviews your application can picture what you would leave behind for customers.

For an interview take-home, budget time for documentation from the start. If time runs short when most of the work is done, don't cram in the remaining parts; in the spirit of "70% beats 100%," write clearly what works, what's unfinished, and how you would finish it.

When reading a job posting, look at the responsibilities first, then the requirements. If the posting mentions custom documentation, train-the-trainer sessions, or best practices, as Tungsten's does, writing is part of the daily job, and you should prepare an interview story centered on a document you wrote that others actually used.

People hiring FDEs can't verify an adjective. A one-page incident report shows them what you leave behind after you leave the customer, which is exactly what they want to know before hiring you.
