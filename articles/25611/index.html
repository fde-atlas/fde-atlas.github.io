1. Surrounded by ads, but the ads aren't the point

Over the past few months, whether you're standing on Zhichun Road in Beijing or in Shenzhen's Hi-Tech Park, in the subway, in elevators, in your feeds—whether you're scrolling WeChat official accounts or Moments—it's all Tencent WorkBuddy ads.

Starting in May 2026, Tencent has been running a saturation ad campaign for this product, spreading from subways all the way to office buildings. There's even an internal saying that this is Tencent's third strategic-level product after QQ and WeChat.

Is that just hype? Look at the data. According to an Analysys report, in Q2 2026, WorkBuddy ranked first among domestic office agent platforms with 20.97 million monthly PC visits. Public reports put monthly active users at the 20 million level, and a Citi research report goes even further: 20 million MAU and 13 million DAU.

Pony Ma personally called it out on the earnings call, describing it as "the most widely used AI productivity agent service in China." The product's technical foundation comes from CodeBuddy, launched by Tencent Cloud in 2023—yes, that AI coding tool.

Competitors haven't been idle either. Alibaba merged QoderWork, Wukong, and MuleRun into a package called "Qwen Office," with DingTalk's new CEO Chen Yusen personally in charge.

360's Zhou Hongyi launched "Nano Work" on July 28. ByteDance has TRAE. The entire market for AI desktop office agents saw monthly visits surge from 20 million to 60 million in three months.

Giants fighting, ads everywhere—what does this have to do with us ordinary programmers? Honestly, at first I just thought it was something to watch for fun.

But today I discovered that what deserves programmers' attention more than the ads is a job posting Tencent put up.

The position pays 50-80K/month, with openings in Beijing, Shanghai, and Shenzhen. The employer is Tencent Technology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., the direction is WorkBuddy/CodeBuddy, and the role is called FDE.

These three letters may be the most valuable job abbreviation in the AI application layer for years to come.

2. What exactly is an FDE, and what are the prospects?

FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer.

The term was coined by Palantir. Founded in 2003, the company's early clients were the CIA and the military. These clients have a trait: they can't articulate their requirements clearly, and classified material can't be brought out for you to see.

Sales makes grand promises, but the product fails on-site—what then? Palantir simply stationed engineers at client sites, gathering requirements while improving the product. Internally, this group was called Delta.

Palantir early employee Bob McGrew gave a definition that I consider the most accurate to date: an FDE is "an engineer who sits at the client's site, filling the gap between what the product can do and what the client needs."

The traditional engineer's logic is to build one feature that serves all clients. The FDE works in reverse, assembling all the capabilities a single client needs. You're a developer, a pre-sales engineer, a delivery person, and the product manager's eyes and ears at the client—a hybrid role where one person does the work of four.

Sounds exhausting? It is. But you also get access to something ordinary programmers can't touch in ten years: how real businesses actually run, where the money goes, and where the pain points are.

Why is this role, which has existed since 2003, suddenly exploding now? Because AI implementation is stuck. MIT's research found that 95% of enterprise AI pilots produced no measurable financial return.

Model capabilities have long been sufficient. The bottleneck isn't the model—it's the last mile of implementation: enterprise data is a mess, internal systems are a patchwork, and nobody can deliver the results the boss wants.

I've seen plenty of these pilots: a flashy launch event, a big screen hung in the conference room, and three months later you ask finance: how many people did it save, how much extra revenue did it bring? Nobody can answer. The AI in the PowerPoint and the AI in the ledger are two different things.

So the big companies started grabbing talent. In May 2026, OpenAI established a separate deployment company valued at about $10 billion, staffed with roughly 150 experts. Anthropic and AWS quickly followed. Domestically, ByteDance, Alibaba, and Tencent are mainly building FDE teams and adding positions, with hiring concentrated in Q2-Q3 2026. FDE job postings grew 700% to 800% year-over-year in 2025—a growth rate few programmer roles have matched in recent years.

The pay is aggressive too. Senior FDEs at leading overseas labs earn total packages of $485,000, with Principal-level roles exceeding $1 million. Note that's overseas data; domestic big tech is just starting to compete in this space with no public figures, but a quick look shows Zhipu hiring, and Alibaba and ByteDance hiring too. Basic economics applies: when a new role has a supply-demand imbalance, early entrants reap the rewards.

But a word of caution. This role is not about quietly writing code. The JD states in black and white: you must accept business travel and on-site assignments, be able to converse with client executives, and carry business metrics—delivery cycle reduction, code adoption rate, office efficiency gains—all of which are your performance indicators. To put it bluntly, if the solution underperforms, the client comes to you first, possibly in the middle of the night. Think it through before you get on board.

3. Tech stack breakdown: explaining the Tencent JD line by line in plain language

The technical requirements in this JD are dense, so let me translate them for you.

Hard skill foundation: Python, TypeScript, and Java—at least two of the three. Why exactly these three? Python is the lingua franca of the AI ecosystem; you can't tune models or process data without it. TypeScript covers frontend and full-stack work—the interfaces, plugins, and IDE components delivered to clients depend on it. Java is the elder statesman of enterprise backends; clients' CRM and ERP systems are most likely Java-based. The three languages correspond to the AI ecosystem, delivery interfaces, and clients' legacy systems—you can't skip any of them.

Now let's decode the jargon in the JD: "context, RAG, MCP, SDD, Harness engineering." Breaking them down one by one:

Context engineering

It's not just about writing a clever prompt or two—it's about feeding the model the right material. What information goes into the context, in what order, taking up how many tokens, and when it's inserted directly determines output quality. Think of it like onboarding a new outsourced colleague: dumping a room full of documents on them to dig through themselves versus picking out the three most relevant pages and handing them over in order produces two very different qualities of work. Models are the same—the quality of what you feed them determines the quality of what they produce. So stop believing in "one magic prompt to rule them all"; out in the field with clients, this fundamental skill is what you compete on.

RAG

Retrieval-Augmented Generation. In plain terms, it's an open-book exam: first retrieve relevant material from the client's private knowledge base, then have the model answer based on it. You need to understand embeddings (converting text into vectors), vector databases, and retrieval tuning. The JD's "building RAG Q&A capabilities based on the client's private knowledge base" refers to exactly this.

MCP

Model Context Protocol. Think of it as the USB standard of the large model world: a unified protocol for connecting the client's internal systems (CRM, ERP, OA) to the model, so you don't have to write separate integration code for each system.

SDD and Harness engineering

SDD is Spec-Driven Development: write the spec clearly first, then let the agent do the work. Harness is the scaffolding plus exam room you build for the agent, letting it run reliably within constraints, with its work evaluable and replayable. Together they solve one core problem: the agent can't be hit-or-miss. "Harness engineering" has been getting hotter this year and has already become a hard requirement in hiring—technical buzzword inflation is rising much faster than salaries.

For engineering delivery, the JD's bonus points list full-stack development and cloud-native deployment (Docker/K8s/CI-CD). The logic is simple: the solution you build ultimately has to be deployed into the customer's own environment, so containerization and continuous integration are basic skills you can't avoid.

Don't dismiss soft skills. The fourth item in the JD requires you to track the customer's business metrics, "continuously tune to ensure the value is tangible," and distill frontline experience into reusable delivery assets and industry solution templates.

Translated: you not only need to know how to build, but also how to do the math, summarize, and replicate. Many engineers stumble here—the work is done beautifully, but when it comes time to report, all they can say is "the model performs well." The customer's boss doesn't understand, and the budget disappears.

Let me also translate the bonus points: Tencent Cloud TCA/TCP certifications, assistant-level and professional-level (renamed TCCA/TCCP in China in 2024); benchmark customer deployment cases in key industries; specialized research, technical blogs, or patents in AI code generation and large model applications. Note the last one—writing blogs counts too. This is a bonus point within reach of ordinary programmers.

IV. How to prepare for the interview: 4 hands-on projects plus a 30-day roadmap

The FDE interview tests real, hands-on delivery capability—you can't bluff your way through by memorizing canned answers. Here are 4 projects you can do at home:

Project 1: Enterprise knowledge base RAG Q&A. Find a public document set—a company's annual report or product manual works—and build a complete RAG pipeline: document parsing, chunking, embedding, vector store, hybrid retrieval, and answer citation tracing. Finishing isn't the end; you need to be able to explain how to tune recall and fix bad cases. When the interviewer asks "the customer says the answers are inaccurate, what do you do," you should be able to give a complete troubleshooting path.

Project 2: MCP connector in practice. Write an MCP Server that connects a real system to a large model. You can set up a fake CRM with local SQLite, or if you have the resources, go straight to Feishu or WeCom open APIs. Demonstrate a multi-step agent flow: look up customer info, generate follow-up notes, write back to the system. This maps exactly to the JD line about "connecting the customer's internal systems via MCP to enable multi-agent collaboration."

The interviewer will most likely follow up: how is this different from writing function calling directly? Don't panic—the answer lies in the pitfalls you've hit. Function calling is a dialect for each model; switch models and you have to rewrite everything. MCP unifies the dialects into a common language: if the customer connects ten systems, you only maintain one protocol layer. If they dig deeper, it's just about how to handle permissions and how to guard against mistakes in write operations. Explain clearly how you designed authentication in your project and how you added confirmation steps for dangerous operations, and you've nailed this question.

Project 3: AI development productivity in practice. Use tools like WorkBuddy or Cursor to make a real transformation to an open-source project, recording changes in code acceptance rate and delivery cycle throughout, and write it up as a data-driven technical blog. Two birds with one stone: you get project experience plus the "technical blog output" bonus point in the JD.

Project 4: AI solution for a specific industry (advanced). If you have industry background, I strongly recommend building a complete solution for a vertical scenario. For example: e-commerce—intelligent customer service plus return prediction system; finance—automated compliance document review; manufacturing—equipment failure prediction plus automatic work order dispatch.

It doesn't need to be perfect, but you must be able to explain clearly: what is the customer's pain point? Why is AI needed? How does your solution solve it? How to quantify the expected results. What FDE interviews value most is this closed-loop thinking of "from business to technology and back to business."

Anticipate four types of interview questions. Scenario questions: the customer says they want "AI to boost efficiency"—how do you break down the requirements? System design questions: how do you choose between RAG and agent architecture trade-offs? Metrics questions: how do you prove to the customer that your solution has value?

Behavioral questions: how do you feel about business travel and on-site assignments, and what do you do when you conflict with customer executives? Prepare a story for each type in advance. Especially for metrics questions—the FDE interviewer most likely comes from a delivery background. Saying "code acceptance rate" versus saying "improved user experience" gets completely different treatment.

Your resume needs a rewrite too. Stop listing "responsible for developing such-and-such system"—translate it into delivery language: who you served, what you deployed, and how much the metrics improved. Even for an internal company project, "cut average customer service ticket handling time by 30%" is worth ten times more than "developed a ticketing system."

For the 30-day schedule, I suggest this: week one, grind hard on the RAG project; week two, conquer MCP; week three, do the third project and write the blog along the way; week four, do mock interviews with friends. Three hours a day is enough. Don't overreach—three projects done thoroughly beats bookmarking a hundred tutorials. Your bookmarks folder won't pass the interview for you.

✦ A few final words ✦

The model race is a game for giants, burning money we can't even count. But the last mile of deployment is an opportunity within reach of ordinary programmers.

Code depreciates; experience doesn't. FDE is probably one of the few roles in this AI wave that becomes more valuable the longer you work, because every industry case you accumulate can't be taken away by others.
