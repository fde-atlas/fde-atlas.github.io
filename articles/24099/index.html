What Is AI Talent? Types, Skills, and Development Key Points—Plus a New Option That Handles Implementation and Adoption in the Field

Original title: AI人材とは？種類・スキル・育成のポイントと、現場で実装・定着まで担う新しい選択肢

AI Ops Manager · 2026-07-25 · 18 min read

Summary

This Japanese article argues that companies lack not AI researchers but people who can embed AI into actual workflows and carry implementation through to adoption. Citing Japan's 2026 Communications White Paper (86.4% of firms use generative AI, yet only 21.5% pursue company-wide business transformation) and IPA's DX Trends 2026 (69.6% of firms lack employees who can drive on-site AI adoption, versus 39.1% lacking AI researchers), it identifies an "execution gap" where PoCs stall. After reviewing limits of hiring, internal training, and outsourcing, the author proposes bringing in professional AI talent who work inside the company alongside staff until solutions take hold, with three engagement models depending on internal capability.

Key points

  • Scarce talent is on-site implementers, not AI researchers.
  • 86.4% of Japanese firms use generative AI; only 21.5% pursue company-wide transformation.
  • IPA survey: 69.6% lack on-site AI adoption staff; 39.1% lack researchers.
  • Hiring, training, and outsourcing each stall at the PoC stage.
  • Proposed fix: embedded AI professionals working alongside internal staff until adoption.

AI talent refers to people who redesign business processes using AI and make them stick in the workplace. Based on data, this article explains a map of definitions and roles, the five essential skills, what can be developed in-house, and the limits of hiring, training, and outsourcing—along with a new option.

Published date Last updated

"I was put in charge of AI initiatives, but I can't decide where to start" or "We tried it in some departments, but the results never spread company-wide." As AI adoption becomes a given in management, many companies find themselves in one of these two situations. That's when they search for the term "AI talent."

To conclude upfront: what most companies lack today is not "people who know AI." It's people who can get into the field, restructure operations together with internal staff, and carry things through to implementation and adoption. This article covers the basics—AI talent definitions, roles, and required skills—then organizes the background of the talent shortage and the limits of hiring, training, and outsourcing, before explaining the option beyond all that: "AI talent that handles implementation and adoption." Executives and AI officers considering securing AI talent should use this as material for decision-making.

What Is AI Talent?

AI talent is a general term for people who solve business and operational challenges using AI and produce results. The scope of this term has shifted significantly over just a few years. It once referred to engineers who design machine learning models, but what companies now seek are people who can restructure operations themselves with AI in mind and bring them to a working state in the field.

The reason is simple. High-performance models are now available to anyone via APIs or services. Scarcity has shifted from the ability to build models to the ability to embed them in a company's operations and keep them running. This article views AI talent through this lens.

Differences Between AI Talent, IT Talent, and DX Talent

These three terms are often confused, but they place their emphasis differently.

CategoryFocusMain Role
AI talentRedesigning operations themselves, assuming AI is availableImplementing AI into operations, adoption in the field, penetration throughout the company
IT talentKeeping information systems running stablyBuilding, operating, and maintaining systems and infrastructure
DX talentTransforming operations and business models with digital technologyDriving transformation of business processes and business models

One thing to avoid is positioning AI talent as "DX talent who happen to know AI well." While DX has been discussed as an extension of informatization and digitalization, what is demanded of AI talent is whether they can redraw operational procedures themselves, starting from what AI can do. Forcing them into existing boxes leads to misjudging the kind of talent actually needed.

Note that the "Digital Skills Standard" developed by METI and IPA also contains no category for AI talent. The "DX Promotion Skills Standard," which defines the main roles needed for DX promotion, consists of six categories: business architect, designer, data scientist, data management, software engineer, and cybersecurity*1. A supplement on generative AI was added in July 2024, but it does not stand as an independent job type*2.

Meanwhile, Japan's Artificial Intelligence Basic Plan, approved by the Cabinet in July 2026, organizes the people responsible for AI utilization and development into five talent profiles. At the core of efforts to review how work is done with AI in mind is the "AI implementation talent." The plan describes this talent as "those who provide AI solutions adapted to the field, rather than generic AI solutions," and requires "understanding both industrial and administrative operations and AI"*3.

From "Builders" to "Operators": The Role Map Has Changed

When AI talent is divided by role, it becomes clear where demand is currently concentrated.

RoleWhat They DoCommon Job Titles
People who embed AI into organizationsAnalyze field operations, redesign them with AI, and handle everything from implementation to adoption and trainingAI promotion officer, AI Operations Manager, AI Enablement Lead
Builders (applied)Build business systems and AI agents on top of existing AI modelsAI engineer, FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)
Builders (foundational)Research and develop models themselves, creating new methodsMachine learning engineer, research scientist

Generally, when people hear "AI talent," they think of the third type. But for most user companies, the first type is what's needed first. Research and development can draw on external resources, but the role of adapting AI to one's own operations, designing its use, and rooting it in the field cannot simply be outsourced for results. FDEs, in the second category, are professionals who enter customer sites carrying their own company's products and handle everything from problem discovery to implementation and adoption; they are covered in a separate article, "What Is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)? Salary, Skills, Careers, and How Companies Secure Them."

This shift is also reflected in hiring trends. In LinkedIn's "Jobs on the Rise" ranking of fast-growing US jobs published in January 2026, AI engineer ranked first and AI consultant second*4. Neither is a research role; both are jobs that embed AI into products and operations.

The expansion is also happening across business domains. One example is the "GTM engineer," who rebuilds GTM processes spanning sales, marketing, and customer success into a system through data integration and automation. Here too, the point is the same: rather than executing initiatives, this role designs and implements the system for execution itself. This job type is covered in a separate article, "What Is a GTM Engineer? Job Description, Differences from Sales Planning, and the Reality of 4 Domestic Job Openings."

The same movement is beginning in Japan. Loglass, Inc. has a position called "AI Operations Manager." A person who took on this role at the company explains that their mission is to redesign internal business processes with AI, deeply understanding operations across departments, identifying issues, and solving them with AI*5. Whether placed internally or brought in from outside, the work expected is the same.

This trend is also reflected in the number of job openings. In a previous article, "Why Is Demand for FDEs Surging? An In-Depth Analysis of Job Openings at 73 Japanese Companies," we checked job postings on companies' own recruitment pages in September 2026 and found 73 companies hiring for FDE roles. The largest group was domestic AI and software companies (40), but 7 companies were also recruiting FDEs to introduce AI into their own or their group's operations. Companies are beginning to place roles that work hands-on in the field not only on the side selling AI, but also on the side using it.

Why AI Talent Is in Short Supply

While demand is expanding rapidly, the supply of people who can fill these roles has not kept up. However, this is not simply a matter of "not enough experts"—what is lacking is not people who use AI, but people who drive business operations with AI. In Japan, the number of companies that have started using AI is growing rapidly, and the bottleneck lies in the steps beyond that.

Generative AI Has Spread. What Hasn't Changed Is the Work Itself

According to the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications' "2026 Edition Information and Communications White Paper" (published July 2026), 86.4% of companies in Japan reported using generative AI in some business operations (survey targeted companies with 10 or more employees that had begun digital transformation; Japan n=477). Companies with a proactive policy on adoption also rose sharply to 68.9%, up from 49.7% the previous year. In terms of usage rates, Japan still falls short of the US at 90.9% and Germany at 91.6%, but the gap narrowed from the previous year *6. Getting started is no longer a major barrier.

The problem is what lies beneath. When the same survey asked whether companies were "organizationally pursuing business transformation through generative AI," only 21.5% of companies were pursuing business transformation on a company-wide, cross-functional basis, while 27.0% answered that they had "no organizational initiatives." Among companies selecting the same option, the figures were just 1.4% in the US and 4.9% in Germany *6.

In other words, at many Japanese companies, the tools have arrived, but the way work is done remains unchanged. Progress stops at the stage where individuals speed up document creation, and the work itself has not been restructured. The role of moving things beyond this "using but not changing" state is now open.

What's Missing Is Not Researchers, but People Who Put AI to Work in the Field

Looking at the composition of the AI talent shortage, the nature of the gap becomes clear. IPA's "DX Trends 2026," published in July 2026, divides AI talent into seven categories and asks about their sufficiency. The categories Japanese companies most often said were "insufficient" were: personnel who can plan and promote data management (71.9%), employees who understand frontline operations and can drive AI adoption (69.6%), and employees who can analyze data with AI and apply it to the business (66.5%) *7.

Meanwhile, companies citing a shortage of AI researchers who develop cutting-edge algorithms stood at 39.1%, tied with management and leadership with AI understanding (39.0%) as the lowest level among the seven categories. Moreover, 56.7% of companies said AI researchers were "not necessary for our company" *7. Most companies are not stuck because they lack researchers. What they lack is people who can bring AI into frontline operations and make it work.

Development Takes Time, and the Workforce Itself Is Shrinking

There are also reasons why supply cannot increase quickly. The ability to apply AI to business operations cannot be acquired through classroom learning alone. It requires experience restructuring actual work on real projects, and at companies with no internal opportunities to gain that experience, development never even begins. A survey published by IPA in June 2025 also found that 19.4% of companies said they lacked personnel to drive transformation yet were taking no action to secure talent *8.

Furthermore, the base pool of workers itself is shrinking. Japan's population aged 15–64 stood at 73,728,000 as of October 2024, a decrease of 224,000 in one year *9. The current state of the hiring market is one where competition for talent intensifies even as the pool shrinks.

Skills Required of AI Talent

What is required of roles that introduce AI into operations and deliver results is a set of five capabilities for understanding and restructuring work. The emphasis differs between technical and promotion roles, but the foundational areas are common. What distinguishes this from the traditional IT talent profile is that the ability to understand and restructure work comes before expertise in programming or statistics.

Skill/KnowledgeDescriptionRepresentative Related Roles
Practical proficiency with AI toolsUsing generative AI effectively in real work and building systems by combining business automation tools. Keeping up with technology trends that change monthlyAI Promotion Officer, AI Engineer
Business process designAnalyzing workflows to identify bottlenecks and redesigning procedures with AI in mind. Demonstrating return on investment in numbersAI Promotion Officer, Business Reform Officer
Project advancementMoving stakeholders across multiple departments and driving projects through to implementation. Overseeing operations after rolloutAI Planner, Business Planning Officer
Education and adoptionExplaining things in terms non-engineers can understand and creating a state where frontline staff can use AI on their ownAI Enablement Lead, Internal Promotion Officer
Literacy in law, regulation, and ethicsJudging which data may be handled and how, based on laws such as the Personal Information Protection Act and the Copyright ActAI Promotion Lead, Product Manager

Expertise in machine learning and data science has not become unnecessary. It remains essential when developing models in-house. However, the challenge most companies face first is "which operations to restructure with AI and how," and without people who can take on that task, adoption will not advance no matter how many engineers are assembled.

Can AI Talent Be Developed In-House?

Half of it can. Of the five skills, business process design and education/adoption favor people who already know the company's operations. How many hours each step takes, who approves what, and why the procedure is what it is—this knowledge cannot be quickly acquired by someone coming from outside. Existing business improvement staff or experienced mid-level employees whom departments rely on are natural candidates.

The way to develop them is not classroom training but assigning them to real projects. Pick one target operation and drive it through to implementation. For example, if preparing quotations and internal approvals take one to two hours a day, redesign that entire flow with AI in mind and bring it to a state where frontline staff actually use it. People who experience this full cycle become able to choose the next operation themselves. Conversely, no amount of training alone will build the judgment needed for implementation.

National subsidies are now drawing the same line. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's Human Resources Development Subsidy Program (Business Expansion Reskilling Support Course) has, as of August 3, 2026, excluded from subsidy eligibility training that teaches generic prompt-writing techniques without application to specific work. Training directly tied to business operations, such as courses for sales staff creating product proposals with generative AI, remains eligible *10.

On the other hand, when it comes to practical skills and implementation of AI tools, self-study leads to a slow start. Since usable tools change on a monthly basis, the burden of keeping up is high, and if there is no one in-house who can teach others, the time spent on trial and error becomes a direct cost. Bringing this part in from outside and transferring the entire approach to your in-house team is the faster route.

In other words, in-house development is not a question of "whether to do it or not," but a matter of allocation: which parts to develop in-house and which to bring in from outside. Trying to do everything yourself leaves you short on time, while outsourcing everything leaves no know-how behind.

How to Secure AI Talent and Its Limits

The methods of securing talent can be organized into three: "hiring," "developing in-house," and "outsourcing to external parties." Each has its advantages, but it is rare for any single one to be sufficient, and a common barrier remains for many companies.

Method of securingMeritsLimits and issues that tend to remain
HiringCan bring in immediately productive talent from outsideCompetition for talent is fierce, and arranging compensation and onboarding systems takes time. Retention after joining is also an issue
Developing in-houseCan cultivate talent well-versed in your own operationsTakes time before results appear. A teaching structure and know-how on the instructor side are also essential
Outsourcing (training/consulting + contracted work)Can quickly incorporate external expertiseOften stops at advice or training and does not reach on-the-ground implementation. There are also cases where operations stall after delivery

As a result, many companies stall at the same place. They get as far as a pilot introduction (PoC) but cannot move on to company-wide rollout. They created an AI promotion office, but execution on the ground does not advance. Despite having exhausted the methods, there are not enough hands to execute—this state is the "execution gap." In the survey mentioned earlier, even among companies that do not ban generative AI, 27.0% answered that they have "no organizational initiatives" *6. The same gap lies behind this as well. How to proceed from taking inventory of operations to measuring effects after establishing a promotion office or responsible parties is organized in a separate article, "How to Advance AI Adoption | 5 Steps to Turn Individual AI Use into Organizational Results."

In other words, "AI talent" has so far been discussed in the context of securing people through hiring, training, and dispatch. But what is truly lacking is someone who can get into the field, work hands-on, and carry things through to implementation and establishment.

The Option of AI Talent Embedded In-House

One option to fill this gap is a form in which professional AI talent embeds themselves in the company and works as one with in-house talent to advance AI use across the organization. It is neither waiting on external experts nor waiting for in-house development to bear fruit. You welcome AI-savvy professionals into the company as if they were members of your team, and advance everything from conception to implementation and establishment together.

Advantages Compared to Conventional Methods

Compared to hiring, the speed is different. Without waiting months for selection, a professional who can execute can join and get started right away. Compared to outsourcing, what remains is different. It does not end with delivery; the approach and know-how transfer to in-house talent, with the goal of ultimately reaching a state where your company can run on its own.

The fact that uncertainty on the delegating side is small also matters in practice. If you know in advance what kind of talent will come, and the scope and duration of their engagement can be adjusted according to circumstances, you can suppress the anxieties typical of using external resources, such as "not knowing who will come" or "not being able to turn back once you start."

What to Delegate Depends on How Many In-House Practitioners You Have

Depending on the state of your in-house practitioners, the way to delegate falls into three patterns.

  • No one in-house can act yet: The professional takes the lead and drives the project
  • There are candidates to become practitioners: Form a team and proceed together while passing on the framework for promotion
  • People can act but lack knowledge: Focus primarily on transferring knowledge

Where to Embed Depends on Where the Organization Is Stuck

The way of embedding can also be chosen according to where things are stuck.

  • Need to support the judgment of executives or AI officers: As a right hand, accompany them from articulating the vision to technical validation
  • There are projects with defined tasks: Take charge of their implementation
  • Circumstances differ by department: Embed directly under each department's manager and support on-the-ground promotion

In any form, the point of embedding in-house and moving together remains the same.

Summary

When thinking about securing AI talent, the shortcut to results is not to end the discussion with merely choosing among hiring, development, and outsourcing. What needs to be designed is what comes after: who will implement the adopted AI in the field, and who will see it through to establishment. First, decide on one task and one issue in your company where AI should be implemented, and place someone who can execute there. Even a small experience of reaching implementation becomes the foundation for company-wide rollout.

Advance AI adoption not by delegating it outside and waiting, but by getting inside and moving together. Just by setting that as your criterion for selecting people, the way candidates appear should change.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. What is AI talent?

It is a general term for people who use AI to solve business and operational challenges and produce results. It includes not only engineers who develop models, but also promoters who redesign operations with AI in mind and establish them in the field. In recent years, demand for the latter has in fact been growing.

Q. How do AI talent differ from IT talent and DX talent?

IT talent focuses on running systems and infrastructure stably, while DX talent focuses on transforming operations and business models with digital technology. AI talent overlaps with both, but differ in that they redraw the very procedures of operations starting from what AI can do.

Q. Why is there a shortage of AI talent?

Because demand has expanded rapidly with the spread of generative AI, while development takes time and the workforce itself is shrinking. In IPA's "DX Trends 2026," 69.6% of companies answered that they lack employees who can drive AI adoption in the field, far exceeding the 39.1% for AI researchers developing cutting-edge algorithms *7.

Q. Should AI talent be secured through hiring or development?

If you need immediately productive talent right away, hiring or using external professional talent is suitable; if you want to increase people who deeply understand your company over the medium to long term, in-house development is suitable. In practice, combining both and proceeding while transferring know-how from external professionals to in-house talent is realistic.

Q. Can AI be implemented even without engineers in-house?

Yes. Many of today's generative AI tools and business automation tools can be incorporated into operations without writing code, so you can start by trying one task in your own department. It becomes difficult at the stage of linking multiple systems together, or the stage of rolling out company-wide and putting it into operation. For those stages, it is faster to bring in someone with implementation experience from outside and leave the approach behind in-house.
