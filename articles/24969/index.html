What is the difference between an FDE and a consultant?

Original title: FDE 和顾问的区别是什么

FDE Times · 2026-10-28 · 5 min read

Summary

Using a two-day invoice parser as its running example, this article argues that the difference between an FDE and a consultant lies not in job titles but in business model and feedback loops. A consultant's hours are a product sold to the client; an FDE's hours are a cost borne by the product company, so work done for one customer should flow back into the product as a generalized feature. It contrasts team sizes, delivery speed, and risk preferences, lists common mistakes that turn FDEs into consultants, and offers practical advice on structuring code, writing feedback notes, and presenting FDE work on resumes.

Key points

  • Who pays for your hours defines the role: clients pay consultants; the product company bears FDE costs.
  • The key design decision is a feedback loop that turns customer-specific work into product features.
  • FDE teams are small (usually six or fewer) and ship to production within weeks.
  • Common failure modes: never closing the loop, defaulting to safe tools, uncontrolled scope creep.
  • Resumes should show time-to-production and what the product team learned, not just deliverables.

The difference isn't the title—it's who pays for your hours, and whether the code makes it back into the product.

Key Takeaways

  • The core difference is the business model: a consultant's time is a commodity the client buys; an FDE's time is a cost the product company absorbs itself.
  • FDEs typically work in small teams and push products to production within weeks; consultants tend to choose proven solutions.
  • Only with a feedback loop does FDE work differ from consulting: work done for a single client must flow back to the product team and be generalized.

Picture this scenario. It's Tuesday of week three of a deployment, and the client makes a reasonable request: the AI agent you built also needs to read another type of shipping invoice, in a format the client defined themselves. You spend two days writing the parser, it runs fine, and the client is happy.

The real question comes after: two days later, where did that code go? If it lives in the client's repository and nobody at your company knows it exists, then whatever your title says, you spent that week doing consultant work.

The confusion between the two roles isn't just about names. Gergely Orosz, author of The Pragmatic Engineer, has observed that Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) roles at AI labs look a lot like the work of external consultants or system integrators.

He also warns that the role is becoming nearly indistinguishable from a Solutions Architect or consultant. If you're an FDE, or want to become one, understanding where this line sits helps you do the right things each day—and pick the right company to join.

Two questions that separate FDEs from consultants

The first question: who pays for your hours? According to a comparison by Webfuse, FDEs work for the product company, where the deployment exists to help the company sell software and retain customers. For consultants, time is usually the commodity being billed to the client. An FDE's time, by contrast, is a cost the product company absorbs itself.

This has real consequences. Consultants have a rational incentive to let work stretch longer, because every hour gets paid. A product company wants the same outcome with as few FDE hours as possible, so it wants the work done for one client today to become a reusable feature tomorrow.

The second question gets right at this: can the work you did for one client make it back into the product? A practitioner writing on Substack points out that the difference between FDE work and "expensive consulting with a nicer title" hinges on one design decision inside the company.

That decision is the feedback loop: the FDE team builds something for a client, then brings it back to the product team and turns it into a general capability.

Where does the difference show up on-site?

Two business models produce two ways of working. According to SD Times, strong FDEs deliver production-ready products within weeks and start creating value. FDE teams are also small—usually no more than six people—while consulting teams tend to be larger.

Risk appetite differs too. SD Times also notes that consultants tend to choose proven solutions over the newest options. The choice makes sense: when hours are a commodity sold to the client, the risk of a new tool breaking lands entirely inside the contract.

DimensionFDEConsultant
Who pays for the hoursThe product company, as a costThe client; hours are the commodity being sold

That said, don't treat this table as a ranking. SD Times argues that FDEs complement rather than replace traditional consultants, filling the gaps where consultants are typically weaker.

On the vendor side, the line is blurring too. OpenAI has established a separate entity, OpenAI Deployment Company, whose first acquisition is the UK AI company Tomoro—a step toward a services model.

Example: one invoice parser, two endings

Back to that shipping invoice parser. The consultant approach: you write the parser, write the handover docs, log the hours on your timesheet. The work is solid, the client gets what they paid for, and the story ends there.

The FDE approach: the code is identical, except you spend an extra 15 minutes afterward writing a short note to the product team, roughly like this:

客户：[客户名称]，物流
需求：Agent 读取自定义格式的运输发票
已完成：定制解析器，自 [日期] 起在生产环境运行
产品为何做不到：目前还无法声明自定义文档格式
通用方案：通过配置定义格式，而不是为每个客户写代码
问 PM：其他客户是否也遇到这个问题？

Now imagine a third deployment, where a colleague at another client writes a similar parser and sends a note too. The PM now has two independent signals instead of one vague complaint.

By the fourth client, it might take only configuration, no new code. Your hours have become a feature—which is exactly why the company is willing to absorb your cost.

What you can do yourself

Every time a custom request comes in, ask yourself three questions before opening the editor: Can this reach production within weeks, or will it stay a demo forever? Which parts hold only for this client, and which might hold for others? Who on the product team needs to know about this?

Then split the code into two layers from the start: the parts specific to this client, and the parts that can be generalized. Compared with one monolithic blob mixing the client's field names, paths, and logic, this layering makes it far easier for the product team to take over. Finally, send the feedback note the same week, while the details are still fresh.

Mistakes that turn an FDE into a consultant

The most common mistake is never closing the feedback loop. You're busy, the client is urgent, the note keeps getting postponed, and six months later you've accumulated a pile of custom parsers nobody at headquarters knows about. Your cost is an FDE's cost, but your output is a consultant's.

Another mistake is defaulting to the safe choice. Choosing proven solutions makes sense in a services model, but the advice here is not to make it your default. Every time you make a technology choice, ask whether it helps you ship within weeks and bring the lessons back to the product.

Also, don't let scope creep without re-confirming who's paying—because when you're sitting next to the client, every request looks reasonable.

Job postings and resumes answer the same two questions

When reading an FDE job description, check whether it mentions collaborating with the product team and feeding insights into the product roadmap, or whether it's all "deliverables," "contract scope," and "billable hours." The latter is usually consulting under a different name. That's not bad—but you should know which job you're taking.

When writing your resume, don't just say "deployed a solution for client X." Write each bullet in three parts: what you did for the client, how quickly it reached production, and what the product team learned from it. Put the most effort into the last part, because it proves you closed the feedback loop—and that's what separates FDE work from consulting.

The next time you finish writing code for a client, ask one question before committing: does this code have a chance of making it back into the product? The answer will tell you whether, that day, you were an FDE or a consultant.
