Should FDEs learn fine-tuning? Start with evals first

FDE Times · 2026-11-13 · 6 min read

Summary

Comparing FDE job ads and first-hand accounts, the article argues that deep fine-tuning skills (SFT, LoRA, DPO, GRPO/PPO) are demanded mainly by AI infrastructure companies like Together AI, and even there they come bundled with inference optimisation (vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, SGLang) and customer-facing project experience. A Baseten engineer says customers mostly ask for lower latency at the same cost, not tuning algorithms. On OpenAI's Morgan Stanley project, the FDE team tuned retrieval and treated evals as mandatory. The recommended learning order: evals and prompts first, then retrieval and data quality, then serving, and fine-tuning last.

Key points

  • Deep fine-tuning requirements appear mainly in infrastructure-company FDE ads like Together AI's.
  • At Together AI, fine-tuning is bundled with inference optimisation and customer project leadership.
  • Baseten's FDE work centres on cutting serving latency, not tuning algorithms.
  • OpenAI's Morgan Stanley work focused on retrieval tuning, with evals treated as mandatory.
  • Suggested order: evals and prompts first, fine-tuning only after other fixes fail.

In brief

  • Demands for deep fine-tuning skills (SFT, LoRA, DPO, GRPO/PPO) appear in the FDE ad from Together AI, an AI infrastructure company.
  • Even there, fine-tuning comes bundled with inference optimisation, vLLM/TensorRT-LLM/SGLang and experience leading projects directly with customers.
  • On the Morgan Stanley project, OpenAI's FDE team refined retrieval and treated evals as mandatory; OpenAI also advises that prompts plus an eval baseline are usually the place to start.

Together AI’s job ad for an FDE, Post-Training lists a string of acronyms that could send a reader straight to a course sign-up page: SFT, LoRA, DPO, GRPO, PPO. The company wants candidates with expert-level hands-on experience in fine-tuning or RL for LLMs.

Read in a hurry, the ad suggests that fine-tuning is the ticket into FDE work in the AI era. Put it next to a few other FDE job descriptions, however, and that conclusion wobbles. Across the ads and first-hand accounts below, the demand for deep fine-tuning expertise comes from only one AI infrastructure company, and even there it does not stand alone.

For an engineer with a few years of experience preparing to move into an FDE role, the practical question is not whether to learn fine-tuning. It is when to learn it, and what to learn first so as not to spend six months on a skill your customers may never ask about.

At Together AI, fine-tuning does not stand alone

Together AI has two FDE roles that touch fine-tuning, and both show it bundled with something else. The Post-Training ad, alongside its list of algorithms, also asks for experience leading complex technical projects directly with customers.

The second role, FDE Strategic Inference, describes the hire as a deep specialist in inference optimisation, fine-tuning pipelines and production deployment. The ad names the tools: inference engines such as vLLM, TensorRT-LLM and SGLang. The day-to-day work is choosing, configuring and optimising engines based on hardware, model architecture and workload characteristics.

The way both ads are written shows fine-tuning as one stage in a production line, not the destination. A model that has been fine-tuned still has to run on a specific GPU, under a specific traffic pattern, for a specific customer. The person who can handle the whole chain is the person Together AI needs.

Infrastructure customers ask about latency, not DPO

An account from an FDE at Baseten, an inference platform, makes the point sharper. The engineer describes the main goal as helping customers put open-source models, fine-tuned models or custom models behind an API endpoint.

Note where the word “fine-tuned” sits in that sentence. The fine-tuned model is what the customer brings; the FDE’s job is to make it run. The typical question cited is how to cut serving latency tenfold while keeping costs the same.

Treat that question as a problem to solve. If a request currently takes 2 seconds, the customer wants 200 milliseconds on the same GPU bill. DPO or GRPO will not easily solve that; the answer lies in choosing the engine, the configuration and hardware suited to the model architecture, exactly what the Strategic Inference ad describes.

An application-layer example: the real work is retrieval and evals

On the side of companies selling models to enterprises, one example deserves a close look. On its project with Morgan Stanley, OpenAI’s FDE team had to develop retrieval-tuning techniques so that research reports were found accurately and trusted by users. They tuned the search step, not the model.

The team also treated evals as mandatory: an LLM-based application is not complete without an evaluation set that proves it works.

OpenAI’s guide to optimising LLM accuracy makes the same point as a sequence: prompt engineering is usually the best place to start, and from there you need a solid eval set as a baseline before considering RAG or fine-tuning.

From these two sides, two kinds of FDE emerge, with different skill sets despite sharing a job title. The right-hand column rests on a single project, Morgan Stanley, plus OpenAI’s general guidance, so read it as an example for comparison, not a rule for the whole application layer.

What the two columns share is not fine-tuning. It is the ability to measure how good a system is and to take it into production for the customer.

When fine-tuning finally gets its turn

Imagine you are an FDE at a bank that wants an internal assistant to answer questions about analyst reports. You build 100 questions with reference answers, run them with prompts alone and, say, get 62 right. That is your baseline.

Now read the 38 wrong answers carefully. Suppose 25 are wrong because the system pulled the wrong report, 8 because the answer had the right substance but the wrong format, and 5 because the model did not understand internal terminology. The first 25 are a retrieval problem, exactly the kind of work OpenAI’s team did at Morgan Stanley; the next 8 can usually be fixed with prompts.

Only once those two groups are dealt with, and the last group of errors persists, does fine-tuning become a reasonable candidate. And at that point, according to OpenAI, the quality of training data matters more than its quantity. So the decisive skill is selecting and cleaning a few hundred good examples from the very questions the system got wrong, not memorising the DPO loss function.

A learning order for those aiming to get in

From these descriptions a sensible order emerges. Start with evals and prompts, because they are the baseline every later decision rests on. Next come retrieval and data skills: retrieval is what OpenAI’s team had to do at Morgan Stanley, and data quality is what OpenAI ranks above quantity when fine-tuning.

The third step is serving: run an open-source model on vLLM or SGLang, measure latency and cost, change the configuration and measure again.

This is the skill the Strategic Inference ad names, and the one the Baseten account revolves around. Only after that, if you are aiming at infrastructure companies, go deep on SFT and LoRA, then DPO and RL methods such as GRPO and PPO.

When reading job ads, use the table above as a rough filter. An ad that names inference engines and post-training algorithms is most likely an infrastructure-layer role; there, fine-tuning is worth investing in early.

An ad centred on accuracy and reliability for enterprise customers may sit closer to the Morgan Stanley example; there, a good eval project probably carries more weight than a fine-tuning certificate.

On your CV, do not write “experience with fine-tuning” as a bare skills line. Turn it into a story with numbers: what the baseline was, how many groups the errors fell into, how much fixing retrieval raised the score, and why you decided to fine-tune or not. If you are targeting the infrastructure layer, add a line on latency before and after you changed the serving configuration.

Do not forget the half of the requirement that Together AI places right beside its list of algorithms: leading technical projects with customers. The string of acronyms may be the most impressive line in the ad, but it is the eval set that tells you whether the customer needs any of them.
