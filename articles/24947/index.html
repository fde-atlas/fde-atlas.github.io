What to Do When the Project Sponsor Leaves: A Guide for FDEs

Original title: 项目发起人离职怎么办：FDE 应对指南

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide addresses what a forward deployed engineer (FDE) should do when a customer-side project sponsor leaves. Citing PMI research that 26% of organizations blame insufficient sponsor support for project failure, it argues FDEs are often the first to notice the risk and should act proactively: distinguish the nominal sponsor from the real decision-maker using three practical tests, log the risk formally and escalate through governance channels, re-sell the project to the new stakeholder as if it were a fresh deal, and deliver quick wins using the customer's own data. If no one steps up, proposing a structured pause is a professional outcome. Prevention starts on day one by cultivating multiple contacts within the customer organization.

Key points

  • PMI data: 26% of organizations cite insufficient sponsor support as a main cause of project failure.
  • Test a successor with three questions: can they unblock issues, allocate resources, and sign off?
  • Record the risk in a risk register and escalate via governance channels, not hallway talk.
  • Treat the new stakeholder as a new deal: re-run discovery and tie scope to their metrics.
  • If no sponsor emerges, propose pausing with handover docs and restart conditions.

Key Takeaways

  • When your champion leaves, the client relationship almost has to be rebuilt from scratch—treat it like a brand-new deal.
  • Write the risk down and escalate it through governance channels: surprises are the fastest way to destroy trust.
  • If you can't find a real sponsor, be willing to ask whether the project should continue at all.

Imagine you're deploying an invoice reconciliation AI agent for a distribution company, now in week six. On Friday afternoon, the finance director messages you to say she's leaving at the end of the month. She brought the project into the company and signed off on its scope.

Monday morning, the accounting team meets with you as usual. But no one can say who will approve the ERP access request still pending, or who will sign off on acceptance.

This is one of the hardest situations in forward deployed engineering, and it has nothing to do with code. As SaaStr puts it, once your champion leaves, you're essentially starting over with that customer.

A 2018 PMI survey found that one in four organizations (26%) cited insufficient sponsor support as a leading cause of project failure.

Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) work closely with client teams, so you're likely to be the first to realize the project has lost its backing. If so, don't wait for someone else to raise it: you already have enough information to act.

The nominal sponsor isn't necessarily the real sponsor

Ricardo Vargas, a longtime project management writer, advises that when a sponsor leaves, the first thing is not to confuse the sponsor on paper with the sponsor who actually does the work. After the finance director departs, someone will almost certainly be appointed "acting lead." A name on the org chart tells you nothing.

Test that person with three questions. Can they unblock what's currently stuck, like the ERP access? Can they commit resources, like having an accountant free up two time slots per week to work with you?

Will they stand up in front of the board and sign off on delivery? If the answer to all three is "let me check and get back to you," your project has no sponsor.

This distinction matters because PMI's data shows a clear correlation: organizations where more than 80% of projects have actively engaged sponsors have 40% more successful projects than those where fewer than 50% do.

That number only shows the two tend to co-occur; it doesn't prove causation. Even so, it's reason enough not to be too generous when evaluating the successor.

Try a quick self-check on your current project: list the last three blockers you resolved and note who resolved each one. If all three are the same name, your project hinges on one person—and you know where to start.

Write the risk down; don't just whisper about it

An engineer's instinct is to keep heads-down working and hope things sort themselves out. Vargas argues that declining sponsor engagement should be explicitly recorded as a risk, not discussed in the hallway. SaaStr also points out that surprises are the fastest way to lose trust, so transparency is essential.

Concretely, add an entry like this to the risk register or weekly meeting notes:

风险：失去项目发起人（财务总监月底离职）
影响：无人审批 ERP 权限，无人签署第一阶段验收
当前信号：2 项申请待审批，尚无正式接替者
建议：在 X 日前任命新的发起人；在此之前，将审批权限委托给指定人员
决策人：项目指导委员会 / 原发起人的直属上级

This template states only facts and impact, without judging anyone. It also turns a vague worry into a concrete item that someone must decide on.

Escalate through governance channels, not through emotion

Once the risk is documented, submit it through the right channel: to the steering committee if one exists, otherwise to the former sponsor's manager. Vargas stresses that this isn't about assigning blame, but about ensuring a strategic initiative still has leadership behind it.

You could open the meeting like this: "The project is technically on track, but next month it will lack a decision-maker. Two items are currently awaiting approval. From now until acceptance, who would you like to own this?" The final question is closed-ended, forcing the client to name a specific person.

One small rule worth keeping: before escalating, always give a heads-up to your own sales lead or account manager first. You're asking the client to be transparent; your own team shouldn't be caught off guard either.

Re-sell the project like a new deal

Once the new person is in place, the biggest trap is treating them as the project's natural inheritor. SaaStr considers this critical: treat it as a brand-new deal and re-sell the value to the new decision-maker. They may never have attended the requirements discovery, and their priorities are likely different.

So the first meeting should feel more like a mini discovery session than a status update. Ask what metrics they're evaluated on this quarter, then tie the project scope to those metrics. If the original scope no longer fits, renegotiate now; by the acceptance meeting it will be too late.

The Persimmon Group recommends that when facing a low-engagement sponsor, the top strategy is to deliver quick wins and make them visible. In the invoice reconciliation example, a quick win could be running the AI agent on a month of real data and presenting a set of before-and-after numbers. Numbers from the client's own data are more persuasive than any slide deck.

Persimmon also suggests not defaulting to relying solely on the assigned person. Other leaders who stand to benefit from the project, such as the head of procurement, can also serve as informal sponsors.

When no one steps up

Sometimes you do everything right and still no one is willing to take ownership. Vargas believes that if a real sponsor cannot be re-established, you must raise the harder question: should the project continue at all?

For an FDE, proposing a pause—with handover documentation, the system's current state, and restart conditions attached—is a professional outcome, not a failure. What damages your reputation far more is letting an unowned system keep running until one day it's completely forgotten.

Instincts to unlearn

Common reaction

  • Keep working and wait for the new person to reach out
  • Send the new person progress updates as if they were the original sponsor
  • Share concerns only with your own colleagues
  • Treat the acting lead as the sponsor

Better approach

  • Log the risk in the risk register within a week
  • Redo requirements discovery and tie the project to the new person's metrics
  • Escalate through the steering committee without assigning blame
  • Test them with a real blocker: can they resolve it?

Prevention from day one

Vargas and SaaStr offer the same advice: a project should never depend entirely on one person. That means building multiple contacts within the client organization from the very start.

CIO.com notes that project managers need to work with sponsors to ensure visible support throughout the project's life. For an FDE, a safe practice is to get at least two leaders attending demos while the project is still going well.

When interviewing for FDE roles or writing your resume, describe this capability with concrete actions rather than vague adjectives. A line like "Kept a project alive through a sponsor transition: mapped stakeholders, escalated via the steering committee, delivered quick wins within 2 weeks" carries far more weight than "strong communication skills."

Working code is only half of a deployment. The other half is making sure someone on the client side still wants it to keep running.
