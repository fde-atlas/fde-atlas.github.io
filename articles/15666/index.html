Biggest risks of the technology we build

FDE Interview Prep · 2026 · 2 min read

Summary

This is a practice interview prompt for FDE candidates on naming the biggest risks of the technology they build. It argues that strong answers start from what the product actually touches in a customer's systems, then rank specific risks with concrete mechanisms: task-scoped tool permissions, human approval for irreversible actions, evals built from real customer cases with a named owner, and a tested kill switch. Candidates should state a personal line—such as refusing to remove approval steps for throughput—and an internal escalation path. The article warns against generic societal-risk answers, deflection to policy teams, or flattery, and suggests engaging with published material like ElevenLabs' lessons from forward deployed engineering.

Key points

  • Anchor risk analysis in what the product touches in customer systems.
  • Pair each top risk with a mechanism, not vague labels like hallucination.
  • Propose week-one practices: scoped permissions, approval gates, evals, kill switch.
  • State a personal refusal line and an internal escalation contact.
  • Avoid generic societal risks, deflection, or praising the company's existing work.

How to answer

The interviewer is checking that you know what the product does inside a customer’s systems, and that your concern turns into engineering habits. An answer that fits any company in the category fails.

  • Name what the product touches. “Your agents read support tickets and can issue refunds through the customer’s billing API” sets up one risk list; “your platform joins an agency’s case records across departments” sets up another, where the risks are access beyond need and data used for a purpose nobody approved.
  • Pick the two you would rank highest for the deployments you’d work on, say why, and give each a mechanism. Not “hallucination” but “the states a refund policy that doesn’t exist, and the customer is held to it”. Not “security” but “text in an inbound email is read as an instruction by an agent with write access”. Say who gets hurt: the end user, the customer or someone outside both.
  • Give the field practice for each, as week-one work. Tool permissions scoped to the task, with irreversible actions behind human approval. An evaluation set built from the customer’s real cases before go-live, with a named owner after you leave. A tested way to switch the agent off.
  • Say where your line is, knowing the deal pressure lands on you. Name the request you would refuse, such as removing approval on irreversible actions to hit a throughput target, and the person inside your own company you would escalate to, rather than a stand taken alone in front of the customer.
  • Tie it to something they published about their own deployments. ElevenLabs’ post on lessons from its forward deployed engineering, for instance, names prompt ambiguity, tool misuse and escalation drift as recurring failure patterns in agent deployments. Follow-ups

What the interviewer may ask next, once your first answer is on the table.

  • Which of those risks have you seen go wrong in something you deployed, and what did you change afterwards?
  • A customer wants the human approval step removed to hit a throughput target. What do you do this week?
  • Where would you disagree with how we handle one of these today?

Where answers go wrong

  • Listing headline risks such as bias, misinformation or job loss with no mechanism, so nothing an engineer at a customer site would do differently follows from the answer.
  • Handing the risk to someone else (“the policy team owns that”), so the field practice becomes “follow the guidelines”.
  • Turning the question into praise by saying the company has already solved it.

Answer this in two minutes

Write the answer you would say out loud. The clock starts with your first word.
