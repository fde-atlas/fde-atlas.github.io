Insight 03

Driving AI in the field through implementation skills, business understanding, and adaptability

This section outlines the three essential competencies for succeeding as an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)—hard skills, domain knowledge, and soft skills. It reveals what is actually required on the front lines of AI implementation.

An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is an engineer role that is stationed at the customer's site and handles AI design, implementation, and operations end to end. As the name "engineer deployed to the front line" suggests, rather than staying confined to a development base, they deliver results on the very front lines of the business.

So what is needed to succeed as an FDE? It can be organized into three core competencies.

An FDE needs not only proficiency in specific languages and tools, but also the ability to work across data science, cloud infrastructure, API integration, and frontend development.

On AI projects, situations that don't go as planned are the norm—constraints of existing systems, inconsistent data structures, and so on. The assumption that "the data should all be in this format" almost never holds. Within such conditions, the ability to see implementation through to completion—connecting and making things work while leveraging the existing environment and delivering results—is the core value of an FDE.

Not just talking about designs and concepts, but actually turning them into working systems and refining them to a state where they can be used in the field—whether you can bridge this "last mile" is the deciding factor.

The success of AI adoption is directly tied to the depth of business understanding. For example, in manufacturing, you need to understand everything from production line processes and quality control standards to the judgment criteria that experienced operators prioritize. These prerequisites and constraints differ greatly from industry to industry.

An FDE is not merely a technician, but someone who bridges technology and business. They must understand specialized terminology, engage in dialogue using the language of the field, and take an approach of structurally grasping business processes.

Rather than imposing technology, an attitude of respecting the business is what earns trust from the field and is the key to making AI stick. Engineers who can "converse in the language of the field" and have "strong curiosity about the business" are a better fit than those who are merely "good at interviews."

On AI implementation projects, unexpected events such as changing requirements or insufficient data occur daily. Projects that start with fully finalized specifications may actually be rare.

An FDE must accept uncertainty as a given and find the essential issues within it. Even when facing chaotic situations, the flexibility and persistence to run hypothesis testing at high speed and iterate on improvements are indispensable.

A mindset that treats uncertainty not as a risk to avoid, but as an opportunity to create value. Rather than "build once it's decided," the stance of "decide while building" is what leads AI projects to success.

Each of these three skills could be mapped to a separate specialist role: technology to the SE, business understanding to the consultant, adaptability to the product manager. However, on AI implementation projects, the value lies in one person covering all three.

Gathering requirements in a meeting room, taking them back to implement at a development base, and showing a demo days later—in this cycle, the "details you only notice in the field" that are unique to AI projects get lost. Because an FDE can reflect what they see in the field directly into the implementation on the spot, the improvement cycle becomes extremely short.
