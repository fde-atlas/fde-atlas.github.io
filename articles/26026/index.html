What does a forward deployed engineer do?

Deployed Lab · 2026-11-15 · 10 min read

Summary

This free first chapter of Deployed Lab's course explains what a forward deployed engineer (FDE) does: a builder who works inside a customer's business, writes real code, and stays responsible until the software works in production. It distinguishes the FDE from software engineers, solutions engineers, and consultants using a time-allocation comparison. A fictional delivery-company case walks through five project stages—Discover, proof of concept, pilot, production, expand—showing that demos take days while permissions, security reviews, and ownership often take weeks, which is where projects stall. It also describes the "loop back," where one customer's fix becomes a product feature for everyone. Job-board data from 15 November 2026 shows OpenAI (95 roles), Palantir (91), and Databricks (86) hiring. A caveat: some FDE titles are actually sales roles.

Key points

  • An FDE codes at the customer site and owns production outcomes.
  • Projects follow five stages: Discover, POC, pilot, production, expand.
  • Demos take days; permissions and security reviews often take weeks.
  • The "loop back" turns one customer's fix into a product feature.
  • Nov 2026 job data: OpenAI 95, Palantir 91, Databricks 86 roles.

In 30 seconds

  • A forward deployed engineer (FDE) is a software builder who works inside a customer's business and stays until the software really works.
  • It is one of the fastest-growing jobs in AI, and it pays well (real numbers below).
  • You already have half the skills if you can code. This course teaches the other half.

The one picture to remember: the FDE is the chef who cooks in your kitchen.

The problemA delivery company tells a software vendor: "We need AI for our supply chain." Three engineers build a late-delivery predictor (a made-up, typical story). Six weeks later nobody uses it. The data sat in a system they could not reach, and nobody had asked the planners how their day works. The missing person is the FDE: the one who sits with the users and makes the software work in the messy real world.

Next: you will meet a few words again and again. Here they are in plain language, then the real numbers on why this job is worth learning.

Words you'll meet

  • FDE: a builder who works with the customer and stays until it really works.
  • SDE: a software engineer who builds the company's own product.
  • Production: the real thing people depend on, not a practice version.
  • Proof of concept (POC): a small build that shows an idea can work.
  • Pilot: a small real trial with real users and data.
  • Stakeholder: anyone who cares how the project turns out.
  • Champion: a person at the customer who helps you succeed.
  • Baseline: how things are today, so you can prove they got better.

Next: who does what. Four jobs touch software and customers, and people mix them up.

Four jobs, one kitchen

Four jobs touch software and customers. Picture a restaurant. Only one chef cooks in your kitchen.

Where the picture breaks down: a real FDE also carries what worked back to the company, so the product gets better for everyone.

Next: the title "FDE" is not used the same way everywhere.

Three jobs share one title

Companies use "FDE" in different ways. Always read the posting.

  • Builder FDE: writes and runs real code inside the customer's business. This course is about this one.
  • Sales-flavoured FDE: a solutions engineer with an FDE title. Shows the product to buyers.
  • Sales-tooling FDE: builds tools for the company's own sales team.

Next: how these jobs differ in practice: where does each person's week go?

Where does the time go?

Same week, four different people. Every job is a mix of five things. Click each role to see its mix.

  • Time writing code: hours spent building.
  • Time with customers: calls, visits, watching them work.
  • Responsibility if it breaks: who gets the phone call at 9 a.m. when it stops working.
  • Finding the real problem: asking questions until you know what to build.
  • Making things reusable: turning one fix into something others can use.

The bars are the author's rough picture, not measured data. Compare the shapes, not the numbers.

  • The SDE is tall on code and short on customers.
  • The consultant is the reverse: lots of customer time, little code.
  • The FDE is the only role tall on customers and on "responsibility if it breaks", with real code too.
  • The last bar, "reusable", is the loop back to the product. We come to it in Stage 5.

Next: how a real FDE project goes, start to finish. We follow one made-up delivery company through five stages.

How an FDE project goes: the map

Every project has five stages, like moving house. Press next to see the whole map first.

The example we follow: a delivery company with 6 planners, who keep goods moving. Their VP of operations (VP means vice president) has just said "We need AI for our supply chain." The supply chain is the path goods take from makers to buyers.

  • Watch how the work really happens. The first request ("AI for our supply chain") is rarely the real problem. You leave with a clear problem, a champion at the customer, and a way to measure success.
  • Build the smallest thing that shows the idea working, fast. Example: a screen built from 500 sample shipments.
  • Five planners try it on real, messy data. Many projects stall here, because nobody owns the move to production.
  • Everyone depends on it. You add alarms, access rules, a security review and a plan to undo changes.
  • A system that works earns the next team and the next use case. You reuse what you built.
  • What you learn here is handed to the product team, so the next customer starts further ahead. We explain this loop after Stage 5.

Now we walk the stages one by one. Stage 1 starts with a phone call.

Stage 1: Discover, the first call

In Discover you do not build anything. You talk and you watch, to find the real problem.

Below is the first call with the VP. You are the FDE. Choose how you respond and watch two meters: customer trust, and scope risk (the chance the project grows beyond what you can deliver).

Notice: the best path never said yes or no to the six weeks. It asked what goes wrong today. Trust rose and risk fell at the same time.

The VP's answer gave you a clue: planners spend hours phoning carriers (trucking firms) to find late shipments. Next, turn that clue into numbers.

Stage 1: Discover, turn the clue into numbers

A vague wish is hard to act on. A number is easy. In Discover, an FDE measures how big the problem is today (the baseline), so you can prove later that it got better.

Made-up example: the 6 planners spend about 4 hours a day phoning carriers to find late shipments.

  • One planner chases carriers 4 hours a day.
  • 6 planners: 6 x 4 = 24 hours a day.
  • 5 days a week: 24 x 5 = 120 hours a week. This is the baseline.
  • Goal: cut chasing in half. 120 / 2 = 60 hours saved (a guess to agree with the VP).
  • One full-time person is about 40 hours, so 60 / 40 = 1.5 people's time.

So you leave Discover with four things: a clear problem (late shipments found too slowly), a user (the planners), a baseline (120 hours a week) and a champion (a person at the customer who wants this to work).

Next: Stages 2 and 3. You have a clear problem. Now you build something and put it in front of real users.

Stages 2 and 3: Proof of concept, then pilot

  • Proof of concept (POC): the smallest build that shows the idea works. Fast and cheap on purpose.
  • Pilot: a small real trial with real users and real data.

Three weeks into the project, you have built a POC. It shows late shipments on a screen, using a sample file of 500 shipments. Here comes the moment that tests every FDE.

ScenarioThe VP loves the demo and says: "Can we switch it on for all planners on Monday?" The real system has about 20,000 shipments a week, and the security team has not seen it. What do you say?

The demo is real, but it only proves the idea works on 500 clean rows. A pilot with a small group tests it on the real 20,000 shipments, with security and an owner in place, before everyone depends on it. Saying yes risks the tool failing in front of all the planners on Monday. Saying no throws away goodwill. Agreeing what "ready" means keeps both the trust and the safety.

A sample reply: "I would love that. First, let us run it with 5 planners for two weeks on real data, and ask security to review it. Then everyone gets it the Monday after."

The gap between a working demo and a system people trust is where projects commonly stall. Demos run on clean sample data and skip access rules, alarms and ownership. Closing that gap is a large part of what customers pay an FDE for.

A pattern you will see on almost every project: the demo takes days, and everything around it takes weeks. A demo like the one above can be built in about 3 days. Getting data access approved, the security review done and an owner named commonly takes about 5 weeks. The code is rarely the slow part. The permissions are.

Next: Stage 4. A pilot that works needs to become something everyone can depend on.

Stage 4: Production

Production is the real thing real people depend on. If it breaks at 9 a.m. Monday and someone cannot work, it is production.

Think of a school play. Rehearsal in an empty hall is practice. Opening night with parents in the seats is production.

On opening night you need working lights and a plan for when things go wrong. For software, that means:

  • Alarms that tell you when it breaks.
  • Access rules about who may see which data.
  • A security review, a check that private information is safe.
  • A plan to undo a change that goes wrong.

For the delivery company, this is the week all 6 planners start relying on the screen. This is where an FDE earns their pay.

Next: Stage 5, and the part that makes an FDE different from everyone else.

Stage 5: Expand, and the loop back to the product

Expand. The screen works. Now the warehouse team asks for one too. You reuse what you built, so the second project is faster than the first.

Then one more thing happens. It is called the loop back.

What is it?

Something you built for one customer becomes a feature of the product, for every customer.

Example: the planners asked "alert me when a shipment is more than 2 hours late". You built it for them. A month later two other customers ask for the same thing. The product team makes it a built-in setting.

Why does it matter?

  • The company: every customer makes the product better, so the next customer is easier to win and to set up.
  • The next customer: starts with the alert already built. Days, not weeks.
  • You: your work lasts beyond one customer.

How does it happen?

  • Notice: you build the same fix a second time.
  • Write it down: what the customer asked for, and the hours it saved (your baseline numbers).
  • Hand it over: give the product team that evidence.
  • Build it once, in a general way: as a setting, not hard-coded for one customer.
  • Next customer: starts from the feature, so their Discover is shorter.

Why don't regular software engineers do this?

It is not that they cannot. It is where they sit. This is a pattern in many companies, not a rule for every one.

  • An SDE usually hears about customers second-hand (support tickets, product managers). They rarely see one customer's messy day.
  • They build for many imagined users, so one customer's details are hard to see.
  • A consultant leaves when the report is delivered, so nothing flows back.

The FDE sees the real mess and can build the fix. That makes the FDE the link between customers and the product.

Next: you have now seen the whole job. Here is what you can practise in the rest of the course.

Could you already do this?

The start of this chapter claimed you already have half the skills if you can code. Check that claim against yourself:

  • Pull data out of a messy CSV with a script and clean it up. You likely have this. It is the raw material of almost every FDE project.
  • Work out what a manual process costs per week, in hours and money. You did a first version above, with the planners' baseline. The habit matters more than the arithmetic.
  • Sit in a customer call and find the real problem behind the first request. Chapters 4 and 49 practise this in depth.
  • Explain a trade-off to a non-engineer, in plain words, without hiding behind jargon. Part of the discovery practice in Chapters 4 and 49.
  • Say "not yet" to a demo request without losing the customer's trust. You just practised it in the call simulation above, and demos get their own practice later in the course.
  • Take a working prototype and make it safe for real users: access rules, a security review, alarms, a named owner. The production and security chapters later in the course teach this.

Whatever you could not tick is not a gap in you. It is your syllabus. The rest of the course is built to close exactly those gaps.

Mix-ups to avoid

  • "An FDE is a salesperson with a laptop." Some titles are sales jobs. The builder FDE writes and runs real code.
  • "A great demo means we are nearly done." Real data, permissions and ownership usually take longer than the demo.
  • "The first request is the problem." "We need AI" is a wish. Ask and watch to find the real problem.

Recap

  • The FDE cooks in the customer's kitchen and owns the result in production.
  • Five stages: Discover, Proof of concept, Pilot, Production, Expand. Most projects stall between demo and production.
  • In Discover you turn a vague wish into a problem, a user and a baseline number.
  • The loop back: what you build for one customer can become a product feature for all of them.
  • Hiring is strong and pay is high, but read the posting: some FDE titles are sales jobs.

In the fieldA team demoed a document-search assistant on sample PDFs. On the pilot they connected the real file share. Half the files were scans, and the assistant happily quoted HR files to anyone who asked. Fixing that took longer than building the demo.
