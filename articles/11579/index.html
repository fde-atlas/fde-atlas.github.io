Full text

If a single number in a bond prospectus is wrong, what are the consequences?

It's not a rewrite—it's an incident.

Liao Wanli, founder of Jinzhiwei, has worked in the financial industry for so many years that he knows this bottom line all too well.

When large models arrived, everyone thought AI could write reports—and it indeed can. But can the reports it writes actually be used in real financial transactions? "Not a single number in there can be wrong," Liao Wanli said.

Financial customers demand 100% "accuracy" from AI.

01

Liao Wanli first noticed the term FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) in September 2024, when Jinzhiwei was preparing materials for its Hong Kong IPO.

While researching comparable companies, he came across Palantir. "My first reaction was, huh? What they do is remarkably similar to the way we've always worked."

Jinzhiwei started out in RPA, with over 1.8 million digital employees deployed in the systems of the six major banks and more than a hundred brokerages. RPA itself means a lot of on-site delivery—stationing staff, integration, tuning.

"I used to feel we hadn't mastered a complete methodology. We never truly formed a flywheel of knowledge and data the way Palantir did."

That remark reminded me of Li Jiarui, founder of Juzi Interactive, whom I interviewed last time. When she started doing Prompt Engineer work in 2023, she didn't know it was called FDE either.

Two founders—one in online education marketing, one in financial RPA—arrived at the same answer by different paths.

FDE practice in China was forced out by business needs, not learned from others.

02

Liao Wanli is not someone easily swayed by concepts. He paid attention to FDE because three signals were growing stronger and stronger.

The first signal: customers are no longer satisfied with what they had.

In the past, once an RPA automation workflow was delivered, customers could use it for two or three years without a peep. Now that AI has arrived, customers feel the old model is "not intelligent enough" and demand rapid evolution.

The second signal: customers started asking "harder questions."

Customers who have been brainwashed by AI hype think large models can do anything. "They have higher expectations, but they themselves may not have figured out what's good and what's not."

The third signal: the competitive landscape has changed.

"In the past, RPA vendors competed on features, stability, and price. Now the competitors are AI companies—they move faster and can produce demos quickly." Even though the demos never land, that's enough to influence customers' decision-making psychology.

These three signals point to the same conclusion: the old organization and delivery model no longer work.

03

I asked Liao Wanli a question: of the three elements you summarized on customer sites—"accurate, stable, well done"—which one is most likely to break down?

He barely hesitated: "Accurate."

"Stability is a technical problem; a good architecture and operations system can basically guarantee it. Doing well is the outcome; if the first two are solved, it will most likely be done well. But accuracy—that's the hardest."

He gave an example. The same financial document is handled in completely different ways under different regulatory environments. General-purpose large models can't possibly understand this.

Every number in a bond prospectus has "cross-checking relationships"—data A and data B must conform to specific logical rules.

These rules aren't in any public document. They're hidden in the experience of business staff, in the approval habits of compliance departments, in the records of past rejected revisions.

"Without deep industry knowledge and accumulated business logic, it's very hard to be accurate."

This is the real value of FDE in financial scenarios—not the person stationed on-site writing code, but the person who can dig out these implicit rules, systematize them, and feed them to AI.

04

But Liao Wanli didn't stop at the "we should have a methodology" stage.

He is running an organizational experiment: he formed a small independent team that reports directly to him, consisting of himself, a newly appointed CKO (Chief Knowledge Officer), and the CTO.

In his words, "it's basically simulating the operations of a small company."

Using a new "knowledge ontology-driven" methodology, this team is rebuilding from scratch a product line that's already running. Goal: get it working in two months.

"If we can make this path work, it will be a revolutionary change," Liao Wanli said.

What does making it work mean? It means that for any customer's business need in the future, what you sort out is a knowledge ontology that links directly with the model—"rapidly building business capabilities while being precise and reliable."

An entrepreneur of over a decade, at a critical juncture of AI transformation, chose the dumbest approach: get hands-on himself, take a real customer's real need, and redo it with the new method.

This is not a technical experiment. This is an organizational experiment.

05

But the cost of the experiment is real.

"The first project will definitely lose money," Liao Wanli is clear-eyed about this. The Silicon Valley FDE model rests on the paying power of American customers. The reality in China is—customers are willing to spend money on AI, but the margins can't support a large number of on-site engineers.

Liao Wanli's confidence lies in the replicable network of financial customers. "In our financial document scenario, once we've done one brokerage, over a hundred brokerages have similar needs. The key is whether we can productize the scenario and scale the market."

This is not a technical question; it's an organizational design question: can the knowledge that FDEs dig out become replicable product capability? If yes, FDE is an investment; if no, FDE is a cost.

He made an appointment with me to talk again in two months. If the experiment works, I'll come back to fill in the answer.

Liao Wanli's definition of himself is interesting: "We're definitely not the company with the strongest AI technology, and probably not the one that has deployed the most AI capabilities—but we just want it to truly create value in business processes."

Not the flashiest, but the one that gets the work done.

I asked him what reputation he hopes Jinzhiwei will have in AI delivery three years from now. He said: "Truly helping customers get their work done, and creating real value. If you create value for them, you can naturally share in the fruits of that value."

This remark may come closer to the essence of FDE than any technical white paper.
