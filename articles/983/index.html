More Than an On-Site Engineer: Why the FDE Is Becoming the "Dragon-Slaying Skill" of the AI Era?

Original title: 不止是驻场工程师：为什么FDE正成为AI时代的“屠龙之术”？

CSDN · 2025-09-22 · 32 min read

Summary

The article argues that over 70% of AI projects stall at deployment because generic models cannot handle enterprises' non-standardized data, tool stacks, and workflows. It presents the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) model, pioneered by Palantir, as the solution: engineers embedded at customer sites act as "product pathfinders," using demo-driven development to surface hidden needs. Teams pair Echo analysts (who understand the business) with Delta engineers (who build prototypes fast). Commercially, FDE sells outcomes rather than seats, using value-based pricing and revenue sharing, following a J-curve of early losses followed by scalable profit as custom work is distilled into platform capabilities.

Key points

  • Over 70% of AI projects fail at deployment due to non-standard enterprise needs.
  • FDEs are product pathfinders who discover hidden needs via demo-driven development.
  • Echo analysts understand the business; Delta engineers rapidly build prototypes.
  • The model sells measurable outcomes with value-based pricing, not per-seat licenses.
  • A J-curve turns early custom work into scalable platform moats, as with Palantir.

On one side of the stage, model capabilities are advancing at a nearly frenzied pace—able to write poetry and code alike, seemingly omnipotent. On the other side, enterprise application deployment is stumbling along. Countless ambitious AI projects "fail to adapt" when they touch the real, complex, and tangled business processes of enterprises. According to statistics, over 70% of AI projects get stuck at the implementation stage, and the conversion rate from proof of concept to actual production is less than 30%. Between technology and application lies a huge chasm. This chasm, like a dragon that is hard to tame, prevents AI's enormous potential from being converted into real productivity. To slay this dragon, a seemingly "old-fashioned," heavy, and exhausting approach is being picked up again from the arsenal by Silicon Valley's AI pioneers and held up as a guiding principle. It is the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) model. This is not a new concept; it was pioneered by the mysterious data giant Palantir and was the basis of its success. But today, with the rise of new species like AI agents, it has been given a whole new significance, becoming the "dragon-slaying skill" for deciding the future in the eyes of many AI entrepreneurs. This article will deeply dissect the core of the FDE model, explore why it can be the key to solving the AI implementation dilemma, and how it achieves "scaling the things that were originally unscalable" through its unique team composition and business logic. ### I. 🐉 The Pain Point of the Era: The Huge Chasm Facing AI Implementation ![](https://i-blog.csdnimg.cn/img_convert/c76a69ddfbb7fbf0d26d859424443c88.jpeg) To understand why FDE has risen, we must first see clearly the predicament facing AI implementation. The core of this predicament is not that the technology itself is not powerful enough, but rather the huge gap between general-purpose technical capabilities and enterprises' individualized business scenarios. #### 1.1 The Curse of "Non-Standardization": The Fundamental Difference Between AI and SaaS The reason traditional SaaS (Software as a Service) has flourished is that it is rooted in the standardization of business processes. Whether CRM, ERP, or HR systems, the business processes they serve—such as customer management, financial reimbursement, and payroll—have a high degree of commonality across different companies. Enterprises can adjust their own processes to fit the software, thereby achieving rapid deployment and scaled application. But AI applications, especially the highly anticipated AI agents, are a completely different matter. What they aim to solve are often the most core, most unique, and most complex "non-standard" problems of an enterprise. * "Islands" of data formats: Every company's data is stored in different systems, with wildly varying formats—from structured databases to scattered PDFs and emails. AI first needs to learn to "read" these unique languages. * "Barriers" of tool stacks: The software, APIs, and workflow tools used within enterprises are diverse. AI agents need to deeply integrate with these existing tools to truly execute tasks. * "Labyrinth" of permission systems: In highly sensitive industries such as finance and healthcare, data and operational permission management is extremely strict, and AI's behavior must be precisely constrained within security boundaries. * "Deep waters" of business scenarios: A bank's intelligent risk control model needs to understand its unique credit policies and customer profiles; a manufacturing plant's supply chain optimization must grasp the subtle differences in its material flow and production rhythm. These highly individualized elements make it nearly impossible for a "standard version" AI product to be "plug-and-play." Forcing the promotion of a generic product often results in the project running aground and ultimately becoming an expensive "technical toy." | Comparison Dimension | Traditional SaaS Software | AI Applications (Especially Agents) | Core Foundation | Standardized business processes | Solving non-standardized, complex problems | Deployment Model | Plug-and-play, quick configuration | Deep integration, extensive customization | Customer Adaptation | Customers adjust processes to fit the software | Software deeply adapts to customer processes | Value Manifestation | Improving efficiency, standardizing processes | Empowering core decisions, creating new value | Key to Success | Product universality and ease of use | Deep understanding of and adaptation to customer business | #### 1.2 The "Last Mile" Dilemma It is precisely this curse of "non-standardization" that leads to the "last mile" dilemma of AI implementation. The powerful models trained in AI companies' labs are like a supercar with an F1 racing engine. But the customer's site is a country road full of potholes and forks. No matter how powerful the supercar, it cannot perform on such roads. The FDE model was born precisely to solve this problem. It does not try to drive one car on all roads; instead, it directly dispatches an engineering crew to the site to build the roads. This crew is the Forward Deployed Engineers. They go deep into the customer's business heartland, and through on-site observation, communication, and rapid development, they precisely identify and solve the real needs enterprises encounter in AI implementation, inch by inch connecting technology and business. ### II. 🧭 Role Positioning: "Product Pathfinders," Not Mere Executors If you merely understand FDE as on-site technical support or outsourced engineers stationed at the customer's site, you completely miss its essence. The true value of FDE lies in the fact that they play a role far beyond that of executors—"Product Pathfinders". #### 2.1 Getting Out of the Building to Discover Implicit Needs The concept of the "Product Pathfinder" was formally established by Palantir's key figure Shyam Sankar. Its core idea is that the most real and valuable needs often cannot be obtained in the office through surveys or interviews. They are hidden in the minutiae of customers' daily work, and even the customers themselves may not be able to articulate them clearly. The way FDEs work is to "get out of the building" and immerse themselves in the customer's business environment. * They work side by side with the customer's business staff, observing how they process data, how they make decisions, and where they feel pain and inefficiency. * They do not just passively receive requirements; instead, through "Demo-Driven Development," they quickly build a rough but usable prototype and show it directly to the customer. "When you see a tool that can solve part of your problem, you truly start thinking about what you need." This approach can greatly stimulate and clarify real needs. * They possess a kind of "rebellious spirit," daring to challenge the surface-level requirements proposed by customers and digging into the deeper motivations and goals behind them, thereby pushing the product from simple feature stacking toward genuine problem orientation. In this way, FDEs can capture the most valuable "tacit knowledge," finding the most accurate direction for product evolution. #### 2.2 The Dual Cycle of "Gravel Roads" and "Highways" Bob McGrew, former Chief Research Officer at OpenAI, used an excellent metaphor to explain the collaboration between FDEs and the headquarters product team. > > What FDEs lay at the customer frontline are "gravel roads." These roads are built quickly, not aiming for perfection, but they can immediately solve the customer's urgent needs and get the business "running." > > The product team at headquarters observes all the "gravel roads" laid by FDEs, identifying the sections that are most frequently used and most valuable. Then, they abstract the design principles and functionality of these sections, invest resources to build them into standard, stable, scalable "highways," and integrate them into the core product. > This is an elegant and powerful dual-engine model. ![](https://i-blog.csdnimg.cn/img_convert/b874424605837a1bb84ab862e3742a87.jpeg) The value of this cycle lies in: 1. Agile response: FDEs ensure the company can quickly respond to the urgent needs of major customers, winning trust and orders. 2. Product evolution: The frontline "gravel roads" provide a continuous stream of battle-tested nourishment for product evolution, avoiding working behind closed doors. 3. Foundation for scaling: The construction of "highways" allows what was originally one-off customized work to be consolidated into reusable product capabilities, thereby reducing the deployment cost of future projects and achieving scale. FDEs are not doing one-off projects; they are exploring and mapping the entire product landscape. Every "battlefield report" they bring back is a valuable blueprint for building the future "product highway network." ### III. 👨‍💻 Team Composition: A "Special Forces" Squad Stationed at the Customer's Site ![](https://i-blog.csdnimg.cn/img_convert/efe83c1f0ad2b59df98ecd9d81141be7.jpeg) To successfully execute the FDE model, what is needed is not a lone hero fighting alone, but a well-structured, complementary composite team. They are like a "small startup team" or "special forces squad" stationed at the customer's site, with extremely high autonomy and combat effectiveness. Palantir, the pioneer of this model, provides the best template with the classic configuration of its FDE team. This team usually consists of two key roles: Echo and Delta. #### 3.1 The Seamless Collaboration of Echo and Delta "One listens and understands, the other implements"—this vivid description precisely summarizes the collaboration model of the Echo and Delta teams. | Role | Echo Team (Embedded Analysts) | Delta Team (Deployment Engineers) | Core Responsibility | "Understanding the jargon", deeply understanding the business and identifying key problems. | "Coding overnight", rapidly developing prototypes and deploying and iterating on-site. | Capability Profile | - Deep domain knowledge (e.g., finance, intelligence, manufacturing) - Excellent communication and interpersonal skills - Keen business insight - Customer relationship management | - Solid full-stack engineering skills - Fast learning and problem-solving abilities - Deep understanding of product architecture - Strong hands-on ability and execution | Work Focus | Communicating closely with the customer's business side, mining requirements, defining problems, and translating "business language" into "technical language." | Quickly converting the problems defined by the Echo team into interactive software prototypes and iterating agilely based on on-site feedback. | Measure of Success | Whether the customer's core pain points were accurately identified and customer trust was won. | Whether a solution to the problem can be built quickly and effectively, and whether it runs stably. | The power of this combination is that it forms a complete closed loop of "requirement discovery - solution design - development implementation - feedback iteration" right at the customer's site. * Echo is like the team's "brain" and "ears"; they ensure the team is always solving a correct and valuable problem. Without them, even the most technically skilled Delta might fall into the awkward position of "using a cannon to shoot mosquitoes," or deliver something the customer never uses. * Delta is the team's "hands"; their powerful engineering capabilities ensure the team's ideas can be quickly materialized. Without them, no matter how many pain points Echo discovers, they can only remain on a PowerPoint slide. Taking the financial anti-fraud scenario as an example, Castle's FDE team practiced this model. Echo team members may come from the bank's risk control department, so they can quickly understand transaction flows, risk models, and regulatory requirements. When they discover a new fraud pattern, they immediately communicate with the Delta team. Delta engineers, within hours or days, use the company's platform capabilities to develop a new monitoring rule or analytical model, deploy it, and immediately produce results. This kind of rapid response is unimaginable in traditional software development processes. #### 3.2 The Culture and Empowerment of a "Small Startup Team" To maximize the effectiveness of this "special forces squad," the company must grant full empowerment and trust. * High autonomy: FDE teams have great decision-making power on-site; they can decide on technical solutions and development priorities based on actual circumstances, without layers of reporting and approval. * Outcome orientation: The company's assessment focuses on whether the team created value for the customer and solved core problems, not on how many lines of code they wrote or how many meetings they held. * Resource support: Company headquarters is the strong backing of the FDE team, providing powerful platform tools, technical support, and knowledge bases, so they are "well-armed" at the frontline. This model is essentially incubating small, problem-solving-oriented startups at the customer's site. They have the agility and focus of a startup, plus the resource support of a large company's platform, thus unleashing astonishing combat effectiveness. ### IV. 💰 Business Value and Pricing: Selling "Outcomes," Not "Seats" The FDE model is not only unique in technology and organization; its business logic completely overturns the traditional software industry's playbook. It does not sell software seats, nor does it sell services by person-day; it sells the "outcomes" that customers can directly perceive. #### 4.1 From "Selling Shovels" to "Helping You Find Gold" The traditional SaaS business model is "selling shovels." The software company provides standardized tools, and customers pay per seat. As for whether the customer digs up dirt or gold with that shovel, the software company is usually not directly responsible. The consulting firm's model is "time-based services." They provide experts' wisdom and time; revenue is linearly related to the labor cost invested, making it hard to scale. The FDE model is completely different. Its core promise is "helping you find gold." Through the deep involvement of the FDE team, the AI company directly participates in the process of creating value for the customer and shares in the value created. | Business Model | Traditional SaaS | Traditional Consulting | FDE Model | What Is Sold | Standardized software tools (shovels) | Experts' time and wisdom | Measurable business outcomes (gold) | Pricing Strategy | Pay per seat/feature module | Pay per person-day/project | Pay based on value/outcomes | Revenue Growth | Depends on user growth | Linearly related to labor cost | Exponential growth as platform capabilities strengthen | Customer Relationship | Vendor relationship | Service provider relationship | Deep strategic partnership | Core Goal | Sell more software licenses | Complete project delivery | Solve the customer's core problems, create value | This "outcome-oriented" model dictates that its pricing strategy must be Value-Based Pricing. For example: * Fixed high-value contracts: For a clear business goal (e.g., reducing fraud rates by 5%), sign a contract worth millions or even tens of millions of dollars. * Outcome-sharing model: Take a percentage of the costs saved or additional revenue generated for the customer by the AI system. For example, for every fraudulent transaction identified and blocked through the AI system, the AI company receives a certain percentage of the commission. This pricing method deeply binds the interests of the AI company with those of the customer, fundamentally changing the game between the two parties and making them a true "community of shared destiny." #### 4.2 The "J-Curve" of Losing First, Earning Later The business curve of the FDE model does not rise all the way; it presents a fascinating "J-curve." 1. Initial investment (loss stage): In the early stage of a project, the AI company needs to invest a large amount of FDE manpower for on-site exploration, requirement mining, and custom development. At this point, labor costs are high, and the contract value may not yet be fully realized, so the company is often in a loss-making state. This is a necessary "tuition fee," paid to deeply understand the customer's business and build trust. 2. Value realization (turning point): When the FDE team, through prototypes and initial results, truly solves one of the customer's core pain points and proves its value, the turning point arrives. The customer is willing to sign a high-value long-term contract, because what they are buying is no longer an uncertain technology, but a validated solution. 3. Scaled profitability (growth stage): As the experience FDEs bring back from the frontline is continuously consolidated into more powerful platform-based products (the "highways" are built), the deployment efficiency of subsequent new projects improves dramatically, and customization costs drop significantly. And because platform capabilities have strengthened, the company can solve more complex and more valuable problems, and contract amounts actually continue to rise. The rising right side of this curve is precisely the charm of the FDE model. Through the early "non-scalable" investment, it ultimately yields exponential improvement in product capabilities and scaled business profitability. Palantir grew from a mysterious software company serving US intelligence agencies into a giant whose market value once exceeded 50 billion USD (about 400 billion RMB), and what underpins it is precisely this solid "J-curve." ### V. ✨ The Essential Difference and Future Outlook: "Scaling the Unscalable" ![](https://i-blog.csdnimg.cn/img_convert/c31353657565ff73cfc06cc53d87182c.jpeg) At this point, the outline of the FDE model is clear. It is not consulting, not outsourcing, and certainly not simple technical support. Its essence is a philosophy and methodology of "scaling the things that were originally unscalable." #### 5.1 The Moat of FDE Traditional consulting or project outsourcing delivers one-time, non-reusable solutions. When the project ends and the team leaves, the knowledge and experience are lost with it. This is a linear business model that cannot accumulate. The fundamental difference of the FDE model is that each of its projects contributes to building a more powerful, scalable product platform. The customized work FDE teams do at the customer's site is the "frontline antenna" of product R&D. These seemingly "non-scalable" efforts, through the transformation mechanism from "gravel roads" to "highways," ultimately consolidate into the company's core assets and an insurmountable moat. While competitors are still selling standardized "shovels," companies with a strong FDE system have, through project after project, built a business empire integrating deep industry knowledge, powerful platform capabilities, and deep customer trust. Palantir, precisely by means of this playbook, has established a near-monopolistic position in the most complex domains such as government, finance, and energy. #### 5.2 The Evolution of FDE in the AI Agent Era Entering the AI agent era, the importance of the FDE model will only become more prominent. Because the ultimate goal of agents is to become "digital employees" capable of autonomously understanding, planning, and executing complex tasks. To achieve this goal, agents must integrate with the enterprise's complex internal systems and processes to an unprecedented degree. This integration process is precisely the field where FDEs excel. In the future, the FDE model itself will also be changed and enhanced by AI. * Partial process automation: Some standardized tasks such as data cleaning, API integration, and code generation can be completed by AI agents assisting FDEs, thereby improving their work efficiency. * The core of human wisdom remains unchanged: However, the core of the FDE model—understanding complex business, mining implicit needs, building interpersonal trust, and performing creative problem-solving—these parts that rely on deep human wisdom and empathy still cannot be fully replaced by AI in the foreseeable future. The role of FDEs may evolve from engineers who "build roads with their own hands" to "chief engineers" directing fleets of AI agents in automated deployment. But their core value as the "bridge" and "translator" connecting technology and business will remain unchanged forever. #### Conclusion Today, as the wave of AI technology sweeps the globe, we do not lack powerful algorithms and models. What we lack is a method to inject these powerful capabilities into the bloodstream of enterprises precisely, efficiently, and reliably. The FDE model is precisely the "dragon-slaying skill" for meeting this challenge. Through the deep embedding of "product pathfinders," it solves the "last mile" problem of AI implementation. With the "Echo+Delta" special forces squad, it achieves seamless collaboration between technology and business. With the business logic of "selling outcomes" and the growth model of the "J-curve," it sublimates one-off customized services into a powerful engine driving product evolution and building business moats. The FDE model reminds us that in an era of runaway technology, the scarcest resource may still be those people willing to bend down, go deep into the frontline, and use their hands and wisdom to fill the gap between technology and reality. They are more than on-site engineers; they are the true value creators and dragon slayers of the AI era. ### 📢💻 [Sharp Commentary] > *> FDE is not a stopgap measure, but a business model leap for AI companies from "selling technology" to "selling value."
