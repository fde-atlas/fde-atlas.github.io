Explain your current role · FDE hiring manager question

FDE Interview Prep · 2026 · 2 min read

Summary

This prep guide addresses the "explain your current role" question reported by a candidate interviewing for a Forward Deployed Engineer position at Cognition, based on a September 2026 write-up on Aced (formerly Exponent). It argues the question is not small talk: interviewers want you to unpack your responsibilities as a system diagram with yourself inside it. The recommended answer has five ordered parts: a one-sentence role definition, the surrounding org and reporting lines, ownership sorted into owned/shared/touched, a traced customer-facing change ending in a measured number, and a one-sentence gap explaining why you're looking. Keep the first pass to about three minutes and prepare one layer deeper per part. It also lists likely follow-ups and two common failure modes: reciting a job description and describing an org chart without placing yourself in it.

Key points

  • The question tests system thinking, not small talk.
  • Five-part structure: role, org, ownership, customer path, gap.
  • First pass about three minutes, with deeper layers prepared.
  • Common failures: reciting duties or org charts without yourself in them.
  • Includes follow-up questions and a two-minute sample answer.

How to answer

This question sounds like small talk and is not. One candidate’s report on Aced (formerly Exponent) for a Cognition role, dated September 2026, says interviewers expect you to deeply unpack your responsibilities in current or former roles, including how your organization functions and how different teams interact. Source 1Cognition AI Forward Deployed Engineer Interview ExperiencePublisherAced (formerly Exponent)Source typecandidate’s personal write-up Answer it like a system diagram with you inside it.

Give it in five parts, in this order.

  • The role in one sentence, in plain words, not your title: “I’m the engineer who builds and runs integrations for our five largest customers.”
  • The org around you. Who you report to, who sits beside you, and the teams you depend on or hand work to. Name roles, not people. Say who decides your priorities.
  • What you own, sorted. What you own outright, meaning you decide and you get paged; what you share, and with whom; and what you touch but don’t own. That last category is what makes the first believable.
  • How your work reaches a customer. Trace one real change from the request to the customer’s outcome, naming each hand-off: who asked, who scoped it, what you built, who shipped it, how the customer knew. End with a number measured on their side.
  • The gap, in one sentence. What this role doesn’t give you that the one you’re interviewing for would. It answers “why are you looking” before they ask.

Keep the first pass to about three minutes, since they asked for depth, then let them pick where to dig. Prepare one layer deeper on each part: the decision you made that someone else would have made differently, and the hand-off that most often goes wrong.

The trap is the job description: all duties, no decisions, no customer.

Forward deployed engineerA software engineer who builds and ships production systems inside a customer’s problem and environment, accountable to that customer’s outcome.

Follow-ups

What the interviewer may ask next, once your first answer is on the table.

  • Who decides what you work on next, and when did you last change their mind?
  • What would break in the first month if you left?
  • Which team do you depend on most, and what happens when they say no?

Where answers go wrong

  • Reciting a job description (“I’m responsible for backend services”) with no system, no decision and no customer anywhere in it.
  • Describing the org chart without placing yourself in it, so the interviewer can’t tell what you own from what your team owns.

Answer this in two minutes

Write the answer you would say out loud. The clock starts with your first word.

Illustrative answer about a fictional project

“I’m the engineer on the integrations team at a company that sells inventory planning software to mid-sized grocery chains. My job is getting each new customer’s data flowing into our product, and keeping it flowing after they go live.
