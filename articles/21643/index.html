A security consultant is a professional who comprehensively evaluates a company's IT systems, business processes, and organizational structure, and designs, recommends, and supports the implementation of countermeasures against information security risks. Their role goes beyond simple system vulnerability assessments, encompassing upstream consulting that considers corporate strategy, regulatory compliance, organizational design, and talent development.

The main roles security consultants play in companies are as follows.

  • Developing security strategies and policies: Designing medium- to long-term information security policies and advising executive management
  • Risk assessment: Asset inventory, threat analysis, vulnerability evaluation, and defining risk acceptance levels
  • Supporting regulatory and standards compliance: Assisting with obtaining and maintaining certifications such as ISMS (ISO/IEC 27001), Privacy Mark, NIST CSF, SOC 2, and PCI DSS
  • Building incident response frameworks: Designing CSIRTs/SOCs, developing incident response plans, and conducting training
  • Security education and awareness building: Designing and delivering security training for executives and employees
  • Vendor and procurement management: Evaluating and selecting security products and services, and managing supply chain risks

In particular, freelance security consultants can support companies from a neutral standpoint unbound by specific products or vendors, making them a good fit for companies that want "an objective, third-party evaluation that in-house staff cannot provide."

The position of a security consultant is characterized by the balance between "technical depth" and "proximity to management." While implementation knowledge as deep as an engineer's is not required, both the logical ability to explain to executives "why a given countermeasure is necessary" and the foundational knowledge to discuss technical matters with field engineers are essential.

Demand for security consultants is rising structurally due to the following three drivers.

Driver 1: Intensifying cyberattacks and growing damage scale In IPA's "Information Security 10 Major Threats 2026," ransomware damage, supply chain attacks, targeted email attacks, and exploitation of zero-day vulnerabilities continue to rank at the top. With system outages occurring one after another at major manufacturers, medical institutions, and local governments, the awareness that "reacting after being attacked is too late" has spread among executives. As a result, requests for risk assessments and countermeasure planning by external experts are surging.

Driver 2: Strengthened laws, regulations, and guidelines Domestic and international regulations are becoming increasingly complex, including the amended Act on the Protection of Personal Information (enacted in 2022), enhanced trade secret protection under the Unfair Competition Prevention Act, the Economic Security Promotion Act, compliance with the EU GDPR, and the US NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0. Only professionals who understand both law and technology can develop appropriate countermeasures, driving up demand for specialized consultants.

Driver 3: New Risks Emerging from DX Promotion and Cloud Migration The more companies promote DX using cloud services, IoT, and AI, the more new security risks arise. For new types of risks such as "cloud misconfigurations," "Shadow IT," and "risks of entering data into generative AI," which cannot be handled by the knowledge of traditional security staff, the advice of consultants who stay up to date with the latest trends is in demand.

As of 2026, the market value of security consultants is at an all-time high. Behind this lies the following supply-demand gap.

Demand side: Due to the increase in cyberattack damage, tighter regulations, and DX promotion, demand for corporate security experts is expanding at an annual rate of 15-20%.

Supply side: Training advanced security consultants takes 5-10 years or more, and professionals who can handle both "technology and business communication" are chronically in short supply. Universities and vocational schools have also been unable to keep up with security talent development.

Going independent as a freelance security consultant offers the following clear advantages.

① Achieve Exceptionally High Rates You receive direct compensation for skills that would not be rewarded as a salaried employee. When a company hires one employee, it costs 1.5-2 times the salary (social insurance, benefits, office costs, etc.). Since freelancers involve fewer intermediary costs, companies more readily accept high-rate engagements, and as a result, a freelancer's take-home income becomes significantly higher than that of a salaried employee.

② Continuous Exposure to the Latest Technologies and Threats Unlike salaried employees tied to a single company, freelancers move across multiple clients, industries, and projects, gaining hands-on experience with a wide range of the latest technologies, attack methods, and defenses. Accumulating diverse experience such as "zero trust design in finance," "OT security measures in manufacturing," and "ransomware countermeasures at medical institutions" exponentially increases your market value as an expert.

③ Remote Work and Flexible Time Management Security consulting centers on document creation, report submission, and online meetings, making it one of the occupations with a high proportion of fully remote engagements. Working from home or coworking spaces is the norm, and it is even possible to handle multiple projects in parallel with zero commute time.

④ Tax Benefits of Incorporation Once your annual income exceeds 10 million yen, incorporating allows you to significantly reduce your effective tax rate through salary income deductions, executive compensation arrangements, and broader expense deductions.

When considering a move to freelancing, it is important to understand the following risks and countermeasures in advance.

① High Liability Risk Security consulting inherently carries the risk that "errors in recommendations or advice cause real harm to clients." For example, if a large-scale data breach occurs the month after advising that "security measures are adequate," the risk of facing a damages claim is not zero. Before going independent, be sure to consider purchasing liability insurance (E&O insurance and PL insurance). It is also essential to thoroughly draft contract clauses covering "disclaimers," "liability caps," and "confidentiality (NDA)."

② Continuous Self-Improvement Is an Absolute Requirement Cyber threats and security technologies change significantly every 6-12 months. Salaried employees can get the latest information through organizational knowledge sharing and in-house training, but freelancers must keep up on their own. Participation in communities such as SANS, Black Hat, and DEF CON, renewal of security certifications, and continuous intake of English-language sources (NVD, CVE, security blogs) are indispensable.

③ Income Interruption Risk During Gaps Between Projects When a gap occurs between projects, income drops to zero during that period. Especially in the early stages of independence, since you have not yet established channels for acquiring projects, it is recommended that you secure 6 to 12 months' worth of working capital before going independent.

④ Responsibility for Managing Client and Confidential Information Falls on the Individual You will handle highly sensitive information such as clients' system configurations, vulnerability information, and incident records. Once you become a freelancer, responsibility for managing this information falls directly on you as an individual (or corporation), so establishing an information management framework (encryption, NDAs, document disposal rules, etc.) is essential.

The ability to "translate" technical security risks into language that enables executive decision-making is the greatest added value that only security consultants can provide.

Many security engineers report "there is a vulnerability with a CVSS score of 9.8," but executives understand in terms of business impact: "What will happen as a result?" "What happens if we ignore it?" "How much will countermeasures cost?" Consultants who can perform this translation can step up from being mere engineers to becoming "professionals who provide value to management."

A Practical Example of Risk Translation

  • ❌ "An SQL injection vulnerability was detected."
  • ✅ "If the current vulnerability is exploited, there is a risk that the personal data of 1 million customers could be leaked externally. Taking into account reporting obligations under the Personal Information Protection Act, the costs of notifying affected parties, stock price declines, and brand damage, the estimated damage could reach the scale of tens of millions to hundreds of millions of yen. Countermeasures would cost X million yen and can be implemented within this month."

This skill of "financial quantification of risk" cannot be acquired overnight, but mastering it will reliably increase your rates. Continuously strengthening your presentation, writing, and facilitation skills directly contributes to your growth as a freelance security consultant.

Security consultant certifications are not just useful for "gaining trust"—they have practical value as direct grounds for rate negotiations.

Most Important Certification #1: CISSP (Certified Information Systems Security Professional)

  • Administered by: ISC² (International Information System Security Certification Consortium)
  • Pass rate: Not disclosed (estimated around 20–30%)
  • Prerequisites: 5+ years of professional experience in 8 security domains
  • Impact on rates: Holders tend to be able to command monthly rates 20–30% higher on average
  • Why it matters: This is the most globally recognized and authoritative security certification, and it is often a de facto requirement at foreign-affiliated companies, financial institutions, and for global projects.

Most Important Certification #2: Registered Information Security Specialist (Gyosei Shien Shi / Registered Security Specialist)

  • Administered by: IPA (Information-technology Promotion Agency)
  • Pass rate: Around 20%
  • Impact on rates: Significantly increases credibility for domestic corporate and government-related projects
  • Why it matters: This is the only national information security certification in Japan, and through the registration system, holders are continuously certified as "active security professionals." Some government and public-sector projects, as well as procurement requirements, explicitly state that "a Registered Security Specialist must be involved."

Important Certification #3: CISA (Certified Information Systems Auditor)

  • Administered by: ISACA
  • Pass rate: Around 40–50%
  • Impact on rates: Improves the success rate of winning audit and compliance-related projects
  • Why it matters: This is an international certification in information systems auditing, security management, and IT governance, and it is highly valued in ISMS audits and internal audit support projects.

As a primary channel for freelance security consultants to win projects, using freelance agents specializing in high-class talent is the most effective approach. Especially in the early stages of going independent, agents support you with project quality, contract procedures, and rate negotiations, allowing you to focus on your work.

Key Checkpoints When Using Agents

  • Number of security/consulting projects available: Agents specializing in consultant and high-class projects tend to have higher rate levels than general engineering agents
  • Transparency of margin rates: Margin rates of 10–20% are common. Whether an agent discloses its own margin rate is an indicator of reliability
  • Flexibility of contract types: Ideally, choose an agent that offers contract types suited to the project, such as quasi-mandate contracts, service contracts, or advisory contracts
  • Interviews with agents: We recommend confirming in a preliminary interview "what types of clients they are strong with" and "how many security projects they have running per month"

Registering with multiple agents simultaneously to compare project quality, volume, and rates is an effective strategy for increasing your chances of success in the early stages of independence.

Many freelance security consultants come from backgrounds such as infrastructure engineer, security engineer, or in-house CISO roles. The following track record-building process is effective for stepping up to consultant roles.

Step 1: Actively participate in "upstream work" at your current job Even while working as an infrastructure or security engineer, consciously get actively involved in upstream work such as "participating in risk assessments," "creating security reports for management," and "serving on vendor selection committees." This experience will later be evaluated as "practical experience" when you become a consultant.

Step 2: Articulate "consulting-like achievements" within your company Make a habit of quantitatively summarizing "what risks you identified, what countermeasures you proposed, and what results were achieved" at the end of projects. Track records with numbers, such as "achieved ISMS certification two months ahead of schedule" or "reduced incident response time from an average of 4 hours to 30 minutes," dramatically increase the persuasiveness of your skill sheet.

Step 3: Build a track record through side jobs and small projects While still a company employee, you can build a track record as a freelancer by taking on small consulting projects (security assessments, ISMS certification support, training instructor work, etc.) as side jobs. If your workplace permits side jobs, ideally you should accumulate 1–3 project results before going independent.

Step 4: Build a portfolio after going independent After going independent, it is important to quickly build a portfolio with an awareness of "industry specialization." By intensively accumulating results in a specific industry, such as "ISMS certification support for 3 financial companies" or "zero trust design for 2 manufacturing companies," you will establish a brand as "the security consultant for that industry."

To gradually raise your rates as a freelance security consultant, the most effective strategy is to elevate your services to a "higher-level concept."

Start with spot assessments and build a track record Starting with spot projects such as "a security assessment for 300,000 yen per engagement" or "a vulnerability evaluation report for 500,000 yen per item" allows you to build connections with a wide range of clients. If the quality of your spot work is high, it often develops into an ongoing advisory contract with that client.

Elevating to fractional CISO or technical advisor The highest-paying position for a freelance security consultant is "fractional CISO (part-time CISO)." In this arrangement, you take on overall responsibility for security strategy while working one to two days per week, and demand is rapidly increasing among small to mid-sized companies that cannot maintain a dedicated IT security team. A single contract can reach 1 to 2 million yen per month, and with two or three clients, exceeding 3 million yen per month is a realistic goal.

For freelance security consultants to earn a stable, high income, the most effective approach is to increase long-term advisory contracts rather than spot projects. Below are the key points for building trust with executives and winning long-term contracts.

① Always "speak in terms of business issues" When discussing security, always frame it from a management perspective: "What risk does this pose to the business?" Instead of saying "the firewall settings should be changed," say something like "with the current configuration, we are defenseless against the ransomware attacks that competitors suffered last year, and if a similar incident occurred, the average business downtime is said to be 21 days." This is the kind of communication that reaches executives.

② Provide regular "security health reports" By submitting monthly or quarterly security health reports to management, you can establish your position as "the expert who visualizes risks that were previously invisible." Once these reports become an agenda item at management meetings, you will be recognized as "indispensable," and long-term contract renewals will follow naturally.

③ Rapid initial response when incidents occur By putting in place a system that allows you to "provide initial response advice within 24 hours" of an incident and communicating this to clients in advance, your trust as a reliable presence in emergencies will rise dramatically. Accurate handling during a crisis is the greatest opportunity leading to contract renewals and rate increases.

④ Raise your visibility within the industry Build your brand as "a leading expert in the field" by speaking at security conferences (ISACA, Information Security Symposium, etc.), contributing to industry media, and sharing information on LinkedIn. Once your brand is established, referrals and direct requests increase, and the proportion of direct contracts without going through agents rises.

  • When going freelance, articulating your track record, obtaining liability insurance, and building a system for continuous information gathering are essential preparations

For security engineers and IT consultants with technical skills and specialized knowledge, going freelance is a highly promising option in terms of income, flexibility, and professional growth.
