FDE is not a job title, but a closed loop from the field to production

Summary

This article argues that FDE is not a job title but a closed loop from customer site to production: understanding real workflows, building production-ready systems, gating launches with evidence, tracking adoption, and turning experience into reusable assets. Using a replenishment agent as an example, it shows how business boundaries change engineering scope. It proposes four acceptance gates (task, system, governance, business) and rejects the lone-hero FDE model in favor of a small collaborative unit. FDEr.ai suggests evaluating partners by asking who owns business outcomes, who can fix issues in production, and what verifiable assets remain.

Key points

  • FDE is a closed loop from site to production, not a title.
  • Different business boundaries drastically change scope and risk for the same model.
  • Launches should pass four gates: task, system, governance, business.
  • A single hero FDE is unreliable; use a small collaborative unit.
  • Projects should leave sanitized, reusable, verifiable assets behind.

The conclusion first: FDE is a closed loop, not a title on a business card

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is often understood as an engineering role close to customers, but the job title itself does not guarantee delivery quality. The core of FDE is to turn real problems on the customer's site into deployable workflows, verifiable business outcomes, and reusable engineering assets for the next engagement.

Therefore, to judge whether a team is actually doing FDE, you cannot just look at whether it is on-site, whether it can build demos, or whether it calls itself an "AI solutions team." You need to see whether it connects context, production, adoption, and feedback into a reviewable chain.

Pushing a vague requirement into production: a replenishment agent example

"Build an intelligent replenishment agent" sounds like a product requirement, but it actually answers none of the key questions. The FDE must first work with the business to ground the problem in observable work: How many stores experienced stockouts in the past month? How long does a manual judgment take? Where do sales, inventory, weather, holiday, and promotion data live? Does the agent provide suggestions, or can it generate purchase orders? Above what amount must someone approve? When data is delayed or systems are unavailable, how does the business continue operating?

The value of these questions is that they directly change the solution's boundaries. A system that only provides "replenishment suggestions" might first integrate inventory and sales data, produce recommendations with supporting evidence, and keep a human in the approval loop; a system that automatically generates purchase orders must additionally handle permissions, quotas, auditing, duplicate submissions, failure retries, and rollback. The same model, placed within different business boundaries, carries completely different risks and engineering workloads.

What a complete FDE closed loop includes

1. Understand the work first, not showcase the model first

Delivery starts with business goals, existing processes, involved roles, system boundaries, and failure consequences. Good questions include: Who completes which action in which system? Which inputs are trustworthy? Which actions require human confirmation? If the AI is unavailable, how does the business continue?

2. Turn the problem into a production workflow

The FDE needs to translate business language into data interfaces, permissions, prompts and tool calls, exception handling, logging, and deployment constraints. The model is just one component; what is truly delivered is a system that can be used within real processes.

3. Judge launch with evidence, not judge success with a demo

Production admission requires looking at task quality, system reliability, business owner buy-in, and user adoption simultaneously. For agents that modify data or trigger external actions, you must also verify least privilege, approvals, auditing, and rollback. Technically runnable does not mean the business can bear the consequences.

Acceptance can be broken into four gates: the task gate confirms whether outputs solve real tasks; the system gate confirms predictable behavior for latency, failure retries, and dependency outages; the governance gate confirms permissions, auditing, human confirmation, and data boundaries; the business gate confirms users are willing to put it back into their daily workflow and there is a baseline for comparison. If any gate lacks evidence, the system should remain in pilot or observation, rather than using "launch first, figure it out later" to mask uncertainty.

4. Include adoption and operations in delivery

After launch, observe whether real users are willing to use it, whether they bypass the new process, who handles exceptions, and how feedback flows back to the engineering queue. The FDE's exit condition is not "code merged," but the business being able to continue operating within agreed boundaries.

5. Distill experience into reusable assets

At the end of a project, distinguish customer-specific configurations from reusable connectors, eval samples, policies, runbooks, and failure modes. Only content that has been anonymized, tested, and version-managed counts as an organizational asset; personal memory or a non-reproducible script does not.

Why "one heroic FDE" is not a reliable delivery model

Customer understanding, engineering implementation, compliance review, production operations, and productization usually require different responsible parties. One person can span multiple roles, but the project still needs clarity on who owns business outcomes, who maintains the code, who approves risks, and who receives product feedback. Without these boundaries, the FDE easily degrades into a "senior firefighter on the customer's site."

A more robust approach is to organize the work as a small collaborative unit: the business-facing FDE understands scenarios, drives adoption, and surfaces problems; delivery engineers handle connectors, workflows, testing, and deployment; core product or platform engineers turn recurring differences into general capabilities and decide which requirements do not enter the product. This preserves on-site speed while avoiding locking critical knowledge in one person's memory.

FDEr.ai's take

FDEr.ai focuses not on replicating a particular company's roles or platform, but on turning the "field to product" feedback loop into a verifiable delivery capability. We break every project into five categories of outcomes: context map, production workflow, admission evidence, adoption observation, and asset review, and clearly identify which are currently deliverable capabilities and which remain product roadmap items.

When enterprises evaluate FDE partners, we recommend asking three questions first: Who is accountable for business outcomes? Who can fix problems in production? What reusable, verifiable assets remain after the project ends? These three questions get closer to the essence of FDE than any job title.
