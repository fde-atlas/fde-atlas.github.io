Will AI Replace Forward Deployed Engineers?

Fourth Age's Substack · 2026-03-05 · 10 min read

Summary

The author argues that Forward Deployed Engineers remain necessary despite increasingly capable frontier models like Claude Opus 4.6 and GPT-5.2. The FDE role combines design, consulting, development, and data engineering, but its core is relationship engineering: embedding with employees, building trust, reading subtle cues, and navigating organizational politics when exposing inefficiencies. AI agents lack physical embodiment, persistent trustworthy personas, and the ability to keep pace with rapidly degrading business context. The author expects technical challenges to be solved before social ones, so the hardest business problems will still need human collaboration for years.

Key points

  • FDEs are still needed; their core value is human trust and rapport, not just technical skill.
  • AI agents lack physical embodiment and personas people interact with candidly.
  • Business context degrades over time, requiring constant on-the-ground verification.
  • Outsider perspective and in-person observation reveal problems invisible in system data.
  • Technical hurdles will likely be solved before social acceptance of AI in these roles.

Full text

Do we need humans to be Forward Deployed Engineers in a world increasingly saturated with AI? And if the answer is “yes” today, will we continue to need them as the AI models become more capable and the infrastructure around them improves? What is it about humans that makes them good at finding and solving business problems using data, anyway?

These are critical questions, as the answers will dictate the future for AI software companies, IT service providers, Forward Deployed Engineering companies, and the end-customer businesses they interface with around the world. Below I’ll attempt to lay out the current state of the FDE role with AI newly in the mix, as well as speculate on where the paradigm is headed and what the resulting future might look like.

The current state of the world

The short answer to the question posed is yes: FDEs are still required for the time being. Most of what an FDE does involves digging into the granular details of key business processes in collaboration with a business’s expert employees, and that collaboration is still best done face-to-face, human-to-human. The full landscape of human interaction with all its nuance, uncertainty and imperfection is still a domain that AI agents haven’t mastered.

Where AI has been inserted into the human domain, people have started to develop a sixth sense (and accompanying distaste) for AI-authored content and its imposition on their lives. Whether this continues to be a problem as models improve and context engineering matures remains to be seen, but we currently live in a time where AI is both mind-blowingly capable and decidedly off-putting at the same time. This “icky”-ness is one of the reasons that AI struggles to conquer the human-centric side of white-collar work, while simultaneously carving out deep footholds in the more deterministic worlds of software engineering and data analytics.

We can therefore acknowledge on the one hand the LLM’s supremacy when it comes to tasks we categorize as “calculator-like” (like coding and data processing) and simultaneously consider it unable to fully interact with emotion, nuance and creativity. Something about the quasi-conscious outputs and musings of LLMs unsettles us and holds us back from sharing what we would consider “too much” with these models. Unfortunately for our AI overlords, precisely this kind of human-to-human sharing and trust is core to the FDE role.

So what does an FDE actually do that an AI cannot? This blog post provides a detailed answer to that question, but in short, an FDE is part designer, part therapist, part management consultant, part solution architect, part software developer, and part data engineer. The art of Forward Deployed Engineering lies in combining these facets in such a way as to be effective at finding opportunities for business improvement, developing solutions to the underlying problems and scaling these solutions to production. Each step involves embedding deeply within the organization and developing an understanding of its workings that spans the high-level strategy of C-Suite, the people-oriented maneuvering of middle management, and the operational realities of the teams in the workshop, salesroom, bedside or control center.

Part of what makes an FDE’s job challenging is that a business’s operational cohort tends to have a long list of pressing concerns in any given moment, and is therefore not typically excited about spending hours explaining their workflows and pain points to an outsider who is trying to grok what they already deeply understand. Navigating this tension is critical to maintaining a positive relationship with business users who generally “have better things to do,” and requires balancing interview-style questioning with fly-on-the-wall observation. The relationship aspect simply must be managed successfully until you’ve alleviated someone’s pain or helped them achieve greater results. Until then, you’re a burden rather than a benefit, and getting from one to the other before your customer’s patience runs out is more of an art than a science. Consider the nuance, compassion and delicacy required to be successful here and now picture a frustrated, overloaded operations manager sitting in front of a chat interface, typing out what their typical day looks like to an AI agent who cheerfully cajoles them along with platitudes and sycophantic encouragement — the laptop is likely in danger of going out the window!

It’s important to realize that while the FDE aims to embed deeply within an organization, a lot of their value comes from the fact that they sit outside of the day-to-day operation. This outsider perspective allows them to quickly identify anti-patterns and inefficiencies that are hard to see from within, especially among the typical day’s urgent tasks and duct-taped processes holding things together. An FDE finds opportunities by asking targeted questions and comparing the answers to real-world events. They are constantly and intuitively reading between the lines of every interaction, statement and happening, trying to see the bigger picture and isolate where in the chain of decisions and actions a process is bottlenecked or breaking down. Even something as subtle as a filler “umm” or a lengthy pause can suggest that there might be an opportunity for improvement if the FDE keeps digging into the source of the uncertainty.

An FDE’s ultimate role is to help an organization and its employees solve their most business-critical problems, but it takes real sleuthing to peel away the layers and uncover the ground truth of a situation before a solution can be developed. While it’s certainly feasible for multi-modal AI models to identify and pursue nuances in a user’s speech, the real question is whether the human interfacing with that AI will act naturally enough for those subtleties to present themselves in the way they do when two humans interact. If you’re anything like me, thrusting a microphone in my face with an AI agent on the other end feels akin to being shoved onstage in front of a crowd and asked to sing. For some reason it generates mostly dumbfounded stuttering and incoherence (or maybe that’s just me!).

Another critical point is that an FDE’s work is never neutral. You may very well be riling up at least one stakeholder with every action you take. Whether that’s because you’re exposing inefficiency, making some illogical bias apparent or replacing someone’s pet tool or workflow, you will bother people. This means that shaking the tree in the wrong way can derail an entire initiative, or even have you thrown out of the building entirely. Businesses are after all collections of humans acting in human ways. Implementing change within such a minefield requires delicate navigation and you can’t always assume everyone is a rational actor who only wants what’s best for the bottom line of the business. While it’s possible for AI agents to conceive of these complexities and devise strategies to operate within them, their ability to do so is severely limited by the simple fact that they’re not “one of us.” Often, avoiding catastrophe requires embedding with and sharing the pain of an organization’s employees. You work side-by-side, toiling with them and building rapport in a sincere and genuine way. This rapport is what opens up access to their challenges and collaboration and for the time being, I don’t see AI models being capable of doing this. As a result, I expect they will always be at least partially excluded from the necessary conversations and resulting opportunities.

Suffice it to say, the FDE role is as much about relationship engineering as it is about software and data engineering. This is where AI agents currently fall short, partly due to insufficient harnesses and partly due to their underlying “differentness”. While the latest frontier models from the major labs (Claude Opus 4.6, GPT-5.2 etc. as of this writing) are all extraordinarily capable and already surpass humans in many areas, they lack two critical features:

  • Physical embodiment to go where they please, watch processes unfold and cross-reference what they see with their internal model of the organization.
  • Persistent, natural personas that human’s are comfortable interacting with.

On the point of physical embodiment, this expands on the idea that human operators can’t possibly communicate everything they do and everything wrong with their existing systems and processes in one sitting and solely via voice and text. Some of the signal comes from an FDE witnessing the inefficiencies and gaps. While models are multi-modal these days, we have some way to go before they’ll truly be able to “watch” a complex, physical process and pattern-match to enough accompanying data to critique it in real time. Perhaps a return of the Google Glass project would be able to change this assessment, as fundamentally this is a structural issue that might one day be solved with advanced contextual retrieval and smart conceptual compression to allow an LLM to draw valuable conclusions from seemingly disconnected data points, but we’re not there yet.

Consider the example of a warehouse team who proactively pick high-velocity parts from the shelves and store them by the loading dock for faster shipping because they know they’re likely to get regular orders for these. The chosen parts are added to an Excel sheet that the inventory manager maintains, but from the point of view of the inventory management system, those parts exited the warehouse days ago. An FDE walking around the warehouse and asking questions would see this and immediately identify that the “hidden” parts need to be factored into any full ontological representation of the warehouse, but it would be hard to recognize this fact purely by looking at the warehouse’s exhaust data (the parts coming in and out of storage). There are innumerable similar examples happening every day in businesses all over the world.

On the second point, while LLMs are great communicators and have captured the hearts and minds of millions of users, there’s still a difference between how humans interact with LLMs via chat interfaces and how they interact with humans face to face. There’s something that changes the dynamic in the fact that the model won’t truly spar with you, won’t get upset, and won’t care if you’re just going through the motions in an important meeting. This is where humans have a communication edge that continues to warrant the involvement of highly skilled, human FDEs.

The final point to make relates to the transient nature of business context and therefore the associated ontological context. Even if an organization allowed an AI agent to interview every relevant team member and compile a detailed account of their current operations (and ignoring the possible issues described above), the reality is that the business’s state information degrades over time. The process a team was using to prioritize high-value customers today might be replaced by a business-wide mandate to normalize prioritization across all customers tomorrow. The excel workbook used to estimate shipment due dates today could be replaced by a more sophisticated algorithm from the logistics software supplier tomorrow. Identifying and adapting to these kinds of changes requires near-constant cross-referencing between the current ontological representation of the business with the reality on the ground, and is a key challenge for anyone attempting to devise a software alternative to human FDEs. It’s not to say that such signal could never be provided to an AI agent, but rather that the process of having the agent pull this signal with high fidelity is a major technical challenge to overcome.

What could the future look like?

The natural next question is whether we are we moving towards a future where the technical challenges of providing LLMs with sufficient business context are solved, the “icky”-ness we feel when interacting with the models is alleviated, and we’ve managed to develop tooling that allows LLMs to maintain a persistent “watch” over real-world operations. I think we are in fact on this path and therefore believe the technical challenges will be solved long before the social hurdles are overcome. I think we’re a handful of years away from someone developing a business-approved harness around a capable frontier model that provides that model with unfettered access to the business’s inner workings. Whether employees will interact sufficiently candidly with that model such that it can isolate and solve the business’s critical problems remains to be seen, but I’m skeptical in the short to medium term. I’m instead a big believer in Fourth Age’s value proposition, and our own internal experiments and internal deployments with LLMs only leave us feeling more empowered by the GenAI revolution. We’re building faster, more robustly and more completely, but with just as much of the human element as before.

In a subsequent essay I’ll document more specifically how AI is helping us and how others can replicate that success within their own organizations. For now, remain confident that the hardest business problems are still best solved by humans working together.
