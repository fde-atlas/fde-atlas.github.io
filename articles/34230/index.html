AI Evals for Forward Deployed Engineers: Field Guide

Falkster.AI · 2026-11-25 · 16 min read

Summary

This Falkster.AI field guide argues that for a forward deployed engineer, the eval is the definition of done. Citing Colin Jarvis, who leads forward deployed engineering at OpenAI, it says a project counts as delivered only when the system runs in production and keeps passing agreed evals. The guide walks through eight stages: score a baseline with 30-50 real customer cases during scoping; build the test set with customer experts in labeling workshops; use messy data as-is per NVIDIA's guidance; measure the customer's own human baseline before setting targets; write eval-based acceptance criteria into contracts (like Thrive Holdings' Tax AI, which reached 90% precision and recall on rental-property returns after processing 7,000 returns); treat eval sets as customer data with privacy and retention rules; run a visible iteration loop; and hand the eval back to both the customer and the product team. Templates are included throughout.

Key points

  • The eval, not the demo, is the FDE's key deliverable.
  • OpenAI treats delivery as production plus sustained eval performance.
  • Score a baseline in week one before promising anything.
  • Customer experts define correctness; never clean the data.
  • Write eval targets into contracts and hand evals back twice.

When is an AI deployment done? Ask a sales team and you'll hear a date. Ask the customer and you'll hear "when it works." Neither one can be checked.

A forward deployed engineer (FDE) works on site with a customer until the software actually runs in their world. The FDE is the person best placed to turn "when it works" into something you can check. That something is the customer's eval. It's the most important artifact an FDE produces, more than the demo, the integration, or the slide deck.

This is the field guide for that work. If evals are new to you, read Evals From Zero: The Complete Guide to Testing AI Products, for Everyone first. It covers the vocabulary and the core method. This part is about doing it inside someone else's company, with their data and their experts.

The short version

  • For a forward deployed engineer, the eval is the definition of done.
  • OpenAI's forward deployed team treats a project as delivered when it runs in production and keeps passing its evals.
  • Build the eval during scoping, before you build the product. Use the customer's real cases, messy as they are. Have their experts decide what's correct.
  • Measure how their own people score today, and set the target from that. Write the pass rate into the contract. Treat the test set as customer data. And when you leave, hand the eval back to two places. The customer gets it, so they can run it without you.
  • Your product team gets it, so the next deployment starts ahead.

Why the eval is the FDE's job

Colin Jarvis leads forward deployed engineering at OpenAI. He told Gergely Orosz of The Pragmatic Engineer: "We usually consider a project delivered when the application is in production and maintaining performance on our evals."

That's a definition of done you can check. Not "the customer is happy." Not "we went live." The system is in production, and it keeps passing a test both sides agreed on.

Jarvis's October 22 post on OpenAI's site shows what that looks like in the field. At a semiconductor customer, some of the FDEs had joined OpenAI only days earlier. They sat with the customer's chip verification team and studied chip design in their spare time. And they "built evals with the engineers to test whether the AI could generate useful tests and correctly diagnose chip failures," Jarvis writes. The goal, in Jarvis's words, is to "solve the customer's problem to a standard we can stand behind in production."

I made the same argument in The FDE Writes the Eval: Almost Right Has a Number. The eval does four jobs at once. It's the statement of work, because done means the rows pass. It's the spine of the handback to product. It's the independence test, because the customer can add a row and rerun it without you. And it's what the renewal gets priced against.

Stage 1: Score before you build

Most deployments start with a kickoff deck. Start with an eval instead.

In week one, before anyone writes integration code, collect 30 to 50 of the customer's real cases. Run them through the product as it is today. Score the results with the customer's expert in the room. Now you know how far you are from good enough, in a number, before you've promised anything.

Jarvis describes this for a telecom customer that wanted an AI voice agent. "During scoping, our model scored around 60% on the key evals we used to assess whether it could do the job in production." That number did real work. It told the team the gap was in the model itself, so OpenAI's research team got involved. Over six months, the score rose to around 80%. Then it passed 90%, which Jarvis describes as "reaching the levels needed for production."

60% to 90%+

Imagine that project without the scoping score. Six months of effort, with no way to show progress or to know when to stop.

A scoping eval also protects you. If the customer's experts won't sit for 40 cases in week one, you've learned something that would have killed the project in month two. Take it to your sales lead now, while the scope can still change.

## Scoping eval: [customer], [use case], week of [date]
FDE: [name]   Customer expert: [name, role]   Product version tested: [version]

### The job
[One sentence: who does what today, and what the AI should take over.]

### Cases (30 to 50, real, not cleaned)
Source: [system / export / tickets], date range [from] to [to]
Mix: [N] common, [N] hard, [N] that the team says "always go wrong"

### First score
| Check | Grader | Today's product | Customer's team today | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Correct answer | customer expert | 58% | 91% | Misses multi-account cases |
| Follows policy | customer expert | 70% | 97% | |
| Time per case | stopwatch | 40 sec | 6 min | |

### Read
Gap type: configuration / data access / product feature / model capability
What closes it: [plan]   Realistic target by [date]: [number]
Go / adjust scope / no-go: [decision], agreed with [sales lead] on [date]

Note the "gap type" line. It's the most useful sentence in the sheet. A configuration gap is your problem this week. A model capability gap, like Jarvis's voice case, is a research problem that takes months. Mixing them up is how deployments miss their dates.

Stage 2: Build the set with the customer's experts

You can't decide what's correct in a domain you joined last week. The customer's experts can.

Sierra's founders, Bret Taylor and Clay Bavor, said this well when they described how their agents are built. "Evaluating a conversation is rarely a technical task," they wrote. In their view, judging whether an agent behaved correctly takes "a subject matter expert that knows the rules of the road of your business, like a customer experience or sales manager." Your job is to make that expert's time count.

Run a labeling workshop. Two hours, the customer's expert or two, and a spreadsheet you prepared in advance.

## Labeling workshop: [customer], [date] (2 hours)
People: FDE (runs it), 1 to 2 customer experts (decide), customer project lead (observes)
Prepared: 60 real cases with the product's output next to each, in a shared sheet

0:00  Why we're here (10 min)
      - "You're writing the test this system has to pass. Your call is final."
      - Show 3 examples: one clearly right, one clearly wrong, one arguable.

0:10  Label (60 min)
      - For each case: correct / not correct, and one sentence on why.
      - FDE records the "why" verbatim. Those sentences become the checks.
      - Arguable cases go in a separate pile. Don't debate them yet.

1:10  Rules (30 min)
      - Group the "why" sentences into rules. Read them back.
      - For each rule: is it ever OK to break it? If never, it's a hard rule.

1:40  The arguable pile (15 min)
      - Expert rules on each. If they can't decide, the case is removed for now.

1:55  Next steps (5 min)
      - Who adds 20 more cases by [date]. When we score the next version.

The verbatim "why" is gold. When the expert says "it should have checked whether the customer has a second account," that's a check you'd never have thought of. Write it down in their words.

Anthropic's test for a good case applies directly here: "A good task is one where two domain experts would independently reach the same pass/fail verdict." If the customer's two experts split on a case, that case isn't ready. Either the rule needs sharpening or the case comes out.

Stage 3: Use their data as it is

The temptation in the field is to tidy up. Remove the garbled inputs. Fix the typos. Generate some clean examples to fill the gaps. Don't.

NVIDIA's guide to designing customer evaluations is blunt about it. In its words, "Customer eval design is not a research problem." The guide says not to clean up the data or remove noisy inputs. It also warns against generating synthetic examples to build "something that looks nice and tidy," as the guide puts it. The goal is to "reflect the real downstream use case," mess included. If customers send garbled requests, the eval should contain garbled requests.

The same guide warns about sets the customer hands you: "If the customer hands you an eval set, do not assume it is high quality." Ask how it was made. Their example: if 10 samples all share the same failure pattern, is that really how the system fails? Or did someone over-sample a known issue? A set built to prove a point to a vendor is not the same as a sample of real work.

Here's a quick check I run on any set before trusting it.

## Eval set check: [customer set name], received [date]

| Question | Answer | OK? |
|---|---|---|
| Where did the cases come from? (system, date range) | | |
| Were they sampled at random, or picked? By whom? | | |
| Does the mix match real volume? (top 5 request types: set % vs real %) | | |
| Who labeled them? Was any label checked by a second person? | | |
| Were inputs edited or cleaned? | | |
| How many failures does the current process have on this set? | | |
| Any personal data? Covered by the agreement? (see Stage 6) | | |

Stage 4: Measure the human baseline

Here's the question that saves more deployments than any other: how well do the customer's own people do this job today?

Customers often ask for 100%. Nobody does real work at 100%, including their own team. If their best people agree with each other on 91% of cases, a target of 99% for the AI isn't a quality bar. It's a way to never go live.

So measure it. Take 30 cases from the set. Have two of their people do the task the normal way, separately. Score both against the expert's labels. Now you have a baseline, and the target conversation gets much easier.

What you measure Why it matters
How often their people get it right Sets a fair target for the AI
How often two of their people agree Shows which cases are really ambiguous
How long it takes them The speed gain is half the business case
Which mistakes they make Their mistakes and the AI's are usually different

That last row matters. The AI might match the team's overall accuracy and still fail on cases the team never misses. That's why the eval should carry hard rules too: mistakes that are forbidden at any rate, like a wrong payment amount or a missed safety flag.

Stage 5: Write the eval into the contract

A statement of work usually lists tasks. "Integrate with the ticketing system. Train the team. Go live by March 1." All of those can be done while the AI doesn't work.

Put the eval in it instead. Done means the agreed cases pass at the agreed rate, in production, for an agreed period.

Jarvis gives an example of what a precise target looks like. With Thrive Holdings, OpenAI's FDEs built Tax AI for accounting firms. In its pilot it processed 7,000 tax returns. The hardest part was rental-property returns, which took "six weeks of engineering and practitioner review to reach 90% precision and recall." Precision means how often the AI is right when it acts. Recall means how many of the cases it should handle it actually catches. A target with both numbers is far harder to argue about than "works well."

## Statement of work: acceptance section (eval-based)

Acceptance test
- Test set: "[name]", version [N], [N] cases drawn from [system] between [dates], labeled by [customer experts]. Owned by [customer].
- New cases: either party may propose cases. The customer's expert approves additions. Version changes are logged.

Acceptance criteria (all must hold for [14] consecutive days in production)
| Check | Target | Basis for the target |
|---|---|---|
| Correct outcome | >= [90]% | Customer team baseline: [91]% |
| Hard rule: [never pays wrong amount] | 100% | Forbidden at any rate |
| Hand-off to a person when unsure | >= [95]% of unsure cases | |
| Median time per case | <= [X] | Customer team today: [Y] |

Measurement
- Run weekly on the full set, and daily on a [5]% sample of live cases graded by [customer reviewer / validated judge].
- Results shared in [place]. Any hard-rule failure is reported within [24] hours.

If targets aren't met by [date]
- [Adjust scope / extend with plan / exit]. The test set stays with the customer in every case.

The last line matters for trust. The customer keeps the test set no matter what. It's theirs. It also lets them check whether any future vendor, including you, is better or worse.

Stage 6: Handle the data like it's theirs, because it is

An eval set built from a customer's real work is a collection of their real requests. It often holds names, account numbers, health details, or contract terms. And unlike most logs, it's meant to be kept and reused for a long time.

Tian Pan names the problem in a post titled "Eval Datasets Are Customer Data With a Right Answer Attached": "Your golden eval set is a privacy boundary your security team didn't know existed." His point about contracts is the one to raise early. A standard data processing agreement covers using data to provide the service. Building test sets may not fit that purpose. And if the agreement says traces are kept for 90 days, the eval cases built from those traces can't live forever either.

I'm not a lawyer, and you should involve the customer's privacy team. But here's the checklist I'd bring to that conversation.

## Eval data checklist: [customer]

Purpose
[ ] Building and running test sets is named as a purpose in the data processing agreement, or a signed addendum
[ ] The customer knows the set will be kept for regression testing, and for how long

Location and access
[ ] The set lives in the customer's environment where possible
[ ] A named list of who can see it, on both sides
[ ] No case is copied into vendor-wide test sets without separate written approval

Content
[ ] Personal details removed or replaced with placeholders that keep the meaning
[ ] Free-text fields checked by a person, not just a script
[ ] Cases that can't be safely de-identified are kept out

Lifecycle
[ ] Retention matches the customer's rules for the source data
[ ] Deletion requests reach the test set too
[ ] At contract end: set returned or deleted, in writing

The vendor-wide line is the subtle one. It's tempting to add a great customer case to your company's own regression suite. The pattern can travel to other customers. The customer's data can't, unless they say so in writing.

Stage 7: Run the field loop

Once the set exists, the daily rhythm is simple. Change one thing. Rerun the set. Compare every check. Log it.

Two things are different in the field.

First, the customer should see the log. When they can watch the score climb from 58% to 74% over three weeks, they stop asking "is it working?" and start asking "what's next?" That's a different relationship.

Second, keep adding cases from live traffic. Every time the customer's team flags a bad answer, it should become a row, labeled by their expert. Sierra builds this into its platform: "Every annotated conversation in the Experience Manager can become a conversation test." The set grows from real failures, which is exactly where its value comes from.

## Field log: [customer]

| Date | Change (one) | Set version | Correct | Hard rules | Hand-off | Note for the customer |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12-01 | Baseline | v1 (50) | 58% | 100% | 80% | Starting point |
| 12-03 | Connected billing history | v1 | 69% | 100% | 84% | Multi-account cases now pass |
| 12-08 | Added 12 flagged cases | v2 (62) | 66% | 100% | 84% | Score dipped: new cases are harder. Expected. |

That last row is worth explaining to the customer before it happens. Adding hard cases lowers the score. That's the set getting more honest, not the product getting worse.

Stage 8: Hand the eval back, twice

When the deployment is done, the eval goes to two places.

To the customer. They should be able to run it without you. That's the independence test. Before you leave, a named person on their side adds a new case, changes a threshold, reruns the set, and reads the result. If they can't, you haven't finished. You've created a dependency.

To your product team. This is where an FDE creates value beyond one account. The customer's rows show exactly where the product fell short in the field. Part 18 of the FDE Transition series argued that the field is the product's sensor network, in Workday's Deployment Agent Took 2 Million Questions. The Field Is the Product's Sensor Network.. The best form for a field finding is a test with a fix attached. The pattern goes back to product. The customer's data stays home.

Jarvis describes the payoff. The eval work became reusable. In one example, the first deployment took two months, and extending it to a second team took one day. By then, writing evals was itself a skill in their tooling.

## Handback: [customer], [date]

To the customer
- Test set "[name]" v[N] ([N] cases), location: [link], owner: [name]
- How to run it: [steps]. Last run: [date], result: [scores]
- Independence test: [customer person] added case [ID], reran, read results on [date]. [Pass / not yet]

To product (patterns only, no customer data)
| Pattern | How often in this customer's set | Product change that would fix it | Proposed test (synthetic or approved) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Multi-account customers get one account's history | 14% of cases | Account linking in the context step | [test description] |

Open risks
- [What could break after we leave, and the check that would catch it]

Mistakes I see in the field

Building first, testing later. The eval written in month three describes what was built, not what was needed.

Your labels, not theirs. If your team decided what's correct, the customer will dispute the score the first time it matters.

A target of 100%. Nobody hits it, including the customer's own team. Measure the baseline first.

A tidy set. Clean data makes a beautiful score that collapses in production.

One score for everything. Average accuracy hides the forbidden mistakes. Keep hard rules separate.

The eval leaves with you. If the customer can't run it, they can't trust the system after you're gone.

Customer data in the vendor's test suite. It feels efficient. Without written permission, it's a breach of trust.

This week

Open your live deployment's definition of done. If it's a date, a list of tasks, or "customer sign-off," write the first five rows of a real one. Five of the customer's actual cases, what a correct outcome is for each, who decided, and the pass rate that would make you both call it done. Send it to the customer's expert and ask what you got wrong.

Frequently asked

Why does a forward deployed engineer own the customer's eval?+

Because they're the one person who sits with the customer's real work and the vendor's product at the same time. The eval turns the customer's idea of 'working' into a set of their real cases with a pass rate. OpenAI's forward deployed team considers a project delivered when it is in production and maintaining performance on its evals.

When should the eval be built?+

Before building, during scoping. A first score on the customer's real cases tells you how far the product is from good enough. In one OpenAI example, the model scored around 60% during scoping and climbed past 90% over six months.

Who should label the customer's test cases?+

The customer's own experts, like a support lead, a lawyer, or an accountant. Sierra's founders note that knowing whether an agent behaved correctly takes someone who knows the rules of the road of the business. The engineer facilitates and records.

Should I clean up the customer's data for the eval?+

No. NVIDIA's guidance for customer evals says not to clean the data, remove noisy inputs, or generate tidy synthetic examples. The eval should reflect how people actually use the system, mess included. Also check any set the customer hands you, since it may over-sample one known issue.

How do I handle customer data privacy in an eval set?+

Treat the eval set as customer data. Agree in writing that building test cases is a named purpose, keep it inside the customer's environment where possible, remove personal details, and match the customer's retention rules. Tian Pan points out that a data processing agreement for providing the service may not cover building eval sets.

What happens to the eval after the deployment?+

It goes in three directions. Into the contract, as the exit test. Into the customer's hands, so they can add a row and rerun it without you. And back to your product team, so the next customer starts with a test instead of a demo.
