Understanding the FDE (Part 1): The Stronger the Models Get, the More Enterprises Need Forward Deployed Engineers on the Ground

Original title: 读懂 FDE（一）：模型越强，为什么企业越需要 FDE 下场？

Full text

I have long followed and participated in enterprise AI transformation and implementation. In several business scenarios, I have gone through the complete practice from PoC, data and permission integration, to launch validation and operational review. Some ran smoothly; others were halfway done before we realized the problem had been defined wrong and had to be reworked.

So when I write about enterprise AI, I don't just want to discuss how powerful the models are. I care more about three things: Has AI entered real workflows? Who is accountable for the results? After one delivery, does it leave behind reusable capability for the next one?

The earlier "Understanding Palantir Ontology" series covered how enterprises connect data, business semantics, logic, actions, and permissions. But by the end of it, a more practical question remains:

Who exactly goes on-site, builds it together with users, and then makes it actually run?

That's why I'm preparing to continue with the FDE series.

Next, I plan to use 18 main-line posts + 3 extras (maybe a bit too many) to break down FDE from a hot job title into a methodology for taking enterprise AI from Demo to results: how to find the right problems, build minimal viable deployments, handle data and permissions, design evals, enter real workflows, prove ROI, and avoid turning a software company into high-end outsourcing.

This is the first post, and we'll start with a phenomenon that seems somewhat counterintuitive.

In 2026, FDE began to shift from Palantir's distinctive playbook to a formal organizational choice for a group of AI and cloud computing companies.

  • On May 11, OpenAI announced the creation of a Deployment Company, and planned to bring in roughly 150 FDEs and deployment experts from day one through the acquisition of Tomoro.
  • On May 21, Microsoft and EY announced a joint investment of over $1 billion over five years, forming a combined team of Microsoft FDEs and EY industry and transformation teams.
  • On June 11, Anthropic and DXC announced a partnership, with DXC planning to train tens of thousands of Claude-certified FDEs to enter customer organizations.
  • On June 30, AWS announced a $1 billion-scale FDE investment, aiming to embed thousands of engineers directly with customers to co-build agent systems under real data, governance, and environmental constraints.

These figures come from company announcements, and some are still investment and training plans, not proof that the capabilities are fully built. But several companies making similar moves within a short period points to at least one thing:

The scarce resource in enterprise AI is shifting from "model capability" to "deployment capability."

The stronger the models, the larger the scope of what enterprises can imagine. Where they once only dared to use AI to polish emails, they now want it to handle customer complaints, review contracts, adjust inventory, schedule maintenance, and even change a core process.

But the more important the task, the higher the cost of errors, and the harder it becomes to sidestep the problems beyond the model.

Imagine a procurement agent.

In a demo, you give it quotes from three suppliers, and it quickly extracts prices, lead times, and terms, then produces a well-reasoned recommendation. This step is indeed far better than a few years ago.

But once it enters production, the questions immediately become:

  • Which supplier entity is the correct one in the ERP?
  • When a quote conflicts with a framework agreement, which one should be trusted?
  • Does this purchase exceed the requester's authorization scope?
  • Which steps can be skipped for expedited purchases, and who can approve them?
  • Can the agent only make recommendations, or can it write back to the ERP and generate orders?
  • After a recommendation is rejected, was it the model's mistake, stale data, or a business exception that exists nowhere in the system?

A demo cares about "can it produce a good answer."

Production cares about "in what context is this answer valid, who has the authority to adopt it, what actions it can trigger, who takes over when it fails, and how the results flow back."

The gap between the two is not a prompt, but an entire set of business, engineering, and organizational capabilities.

FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer, often translated as Forward Deployed Engineer.

But if you only understand it as "an engineer who goes to the customer site to install systems," you'll miss the most important part.

OpenAI's current FDE job description is quite representative: the FDE is responsible from problem discovery, technical scoping, system design, and development all the way to production release. Success is not measured by "the project was accepted," but by production adoption, measurable impact on workflows, and whether field feedback based on evals feeds back into product and model roadmaps.

I prefer to understand FDE through three "own it end to end" responsibilities:

Accountable for business outcomes.

He doesn't just take a requirements ticket; he works with the business side to confirm: what problem is worth solving, what the current baseline is, who will actually use it, and which outcome it changes.

Accountable for production systems.

He needs to connect the model to real data, tools, permissions, auditing, and business processes, write maintainable code, handle exceptions and rollbacks, until it can be used in daily operations.

Accountable for product learning.

He cannot leave every customer's problem as a piece of private code. The integration patterns, exception types, eval samples, permission templates, and product gaps learned in the field must be distilled into capabilities that the next project can reuse.

Missing any one of these three responsibilities, the FDE easily becomes distorted.

Without business outcomes, it becomes a tech demo; without production engineering, it becomes consulting that writes proposals; without product learning, it becomes custom outsourcing that grows heavier with every project.

Long before FDE gained widespread attention, Palantir had been working this way for many years.

In its Architecture Center, Palantir calls the FDE method "backpropagation for humans": engineering teams get as close to the problem as possible, collaborate with the core engineering team, and continuously synthesize field feedback to ship new capabilities.

The point of this metaphor is not "being on-site," but "backpropagation."

The ideal division of labor in traditional software is that product teams build general capabilities at headquarters, and sales, implementation, and customer success then deliver them to customers. But in complex enterprise environments, customers often cannot write complete specifications in advance. Much of the critical information only surfaces in real usage:

  • The process users describe verbally differs from the process they actually follow;
  • Business staff simply don't trust states in the system that look clean;
  • The documentation contains general rules, but what really determines outcomes are the exceptions only veteran employees know;
  • A recommendation looks complete in the interface, but users refuse to use it because it would require re-entering data into three systems.

So the field is not just the endpoint of delivery; it is also the input end of product development.

First, the more general the model capability, the more local the value realization.

The same large model can enter manufacturing, insurance, retail, and government, but the data it must read, the actions it can take, and the responsibility lines it cannot cross are local in every enterprise. Models can be reused across industries; business context does not grow automatically.

Second, AI is a non-deterministic system that still requires operations after launch.

In traditional software, an API call with the same input usually produces stable output. An agent may give different results depending on context, model version, tool responses, and conversation history. This means teams need real samples, evals, upgrade mechanisms, human takeover, and continuous review; "release" cannot be treated as the end of the project.

Third, many enterprise AI products are still at the stage where "problems and products are discovered together."

A customer can tell you "I want a procurement agent," but it's hard for them to say on day one exactly which data it should access, when it must refuse to answer, which decisions should be escalated to a human, and how success will ultimately be measured. These aren't things you can solve by waiting for a complete requirements document; you have to build and learn as you deploy.

First, a word of caution.

Sending engineers to work alongside customers is an expensive organizational choice. If the problem is standard, the implementation path is already clear, and a configurable product plus normal customer success can get the job done, then you shouldn't pile on FDEs.

In one public retrospective, Decagon noted that early forward deployment tended to funnel every customer's edge cases straight to the engineering team, and delivery quickly became a bottleneck. What they later emphasized wasn't "everyone heroically firefighting harder," but turning the repetitive work in each customization into self-service capabilities and shared systems.

So a truly healthy FDE role should accomplish two things at once:

  • Make this zero-to-one succeed, until the customer is using it in production to solve real problems;
  • Make the next zero-to-one cheaper, by leaving behind reusable data patterns, evals, connectors, permission templates, and delivery methods.

With only the first, FDEs eventually become high-end outsourcing. With only the second, the team drifts away from the field and returns to "guessing what customers need from the office."

For most enterprises, the first step is not to immediately create a new FDE position.

A more practical approach is to first check whether anyone on the project fully covers the following functions:

  • Defining the problem in terms of business outcomes, not model capabilities;
  • Following real users through a task end to end, seeing the workarounds and exceptions outside the system;
  • Being able to build a production system hands-on, and to make trade-offs under permission, compliance, and operational constraints;
  • Turning rejections, failures, and exceptions into evals and product inputs;
  • Designing the handover and exit from the start of the project, so the field team doesn't permanently become a human interface.

This person can be called an FDE, or it can be a small team made up of an internal AI product owner, a technical lead, and a business expert. The name doesn't matter most; what matters is that end-to-end ownership isn't left empty.

The FDE is trending not because models aren't strong enough.

Quite the opposite: precisely because what models can do is becoming more important, enterprises need a class of people who step into real environments, connect business context, production engineering, and product learning, and take responsibility for the final outcome.

Models solve for the ceiling of capability; FDEs solve for whether that capability can make it through enterprise reality.
