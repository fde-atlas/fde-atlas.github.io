Palantir's Reflection: The Journey from Controversy to Recognition

Original title: Palantir的反思：从争议到认可的历程

安全内参 · 2024-10-17 · 21 min read

Summary

Former Palantir engineer Nabeel S. Qureshi reflects on his eight years at the company, now near a $100B market cap and in the S&P 500. He explains the forward deployed engineer model: FDEs embed at client sites (e.g., Airbus, where software helped quadruple A350 production speed) while a product team generalizes their solutions into Foundry, enabling a rare services-to-product transition with 80% gross margins in 2023. He also covers data integration as the core business, the flat no-titles culture, distinctive recruiting, the ethics of working with government agencies, and why a decade of data integration positions Palantir well for enterprise AI.

Key points

  • Qureshi worked eight years as a forward deployed engineer
  • FDEs solved client problems on-site; PD turned them into Foundry
  • Airbus work helped quadruple A350 manufacturing speed
  • 2023 gross margins hit 80% versus Accenture's 32%
  • He argues engaging morally gray institutions beats abstaining

Full text

Compiled from: Reflections on Palantir (by Nabeel S. Qureshi)

Palantir recently joined the S&P 500, its stock price has soared, and its market cap is approaching $100 billion, attracting the investment interest of venture capitalists. In the past, Palantir was controversial due to its associations with spy technology and NSA surveillance; the company had a poor image and was even questioned on moral grounds. The author worked at Palantir for eight years, and after leaving, decided to share his experiences and understanding of the company, because there are many misconceptions about Palantir from the outside.

1. Why I joined

I joined the company in the summer of 2015, initially at the newly opened London office, later moving to Silicon Valley, and finally to Washington, D.C. — as a Forward Deployed Engineer. For context, the company had about 1,500 people at the time; it had offices in Palo Alto (headquarters), New York, London, and a few other places. (The company now has about 4,000 people, with headquarters in Denver.)

Why join?

First, I wanted to work in "hard" industries and solve genuinely meaningful problems. For personal reasons, the fields I was interested in were healthcare and biotechnology, and the company was just getting started in these areas. The company talked about working in healthcare, aerospace, manufacturing, cybersecurity, and other industries I considered very important, but at the time most people weren't working in these industries. What was hot then was social networks (Facebook, LinkedIn, Quora, etc.) and various consumer apps (Dropbox, Uber, Airbnb), but few companies were solving the truly hard parts of the economy. If you wanted to work in these "harder" sectors of the economy while also having Silicon Valley work culture, Palantir was at one point almost your only option.

My goal was to start a company, but I wanted to (1) first work deeply in an industry for a while and truly understand it; and (2) work for an American company and obtain a green card that way. Palantir offered both. This made the choice easy.

Second, talent density. I spoke with some of the early people working in the healthcare business and was very impressed. Afterwards, I talked with some of the early business operations and strategy people and was even more impressed. These people were extremely focused, fiercely competitive, hungry to win, true believers; they were quirky, fascinating, read philosophy books in their spare time, tried strange diets, and rode 100 miles for fun. It turned out this was inherited from the PayPal mafia. Yishan Wong, an early PayPal member, wrote about the importance of intensity:

Overall, as I began researching more startups, I found that PayPal's talent level was not unusual among Silicon Valley startups, but the differentiating factor may have been the intensity from the top: Peter Thiel and Max Levchin were both very intense people — extremely competitive, hardworking, and unwilling to accept failure. I believe this kind of leadership is what drove a "standard" talent team to accomplish great things, and subsequently enabled a steady stream of later achievements.

Palantir was a very peculiar place. I remember the first time I spoke with Stephen Cohen, the air conditioning in his office was set to 60 degrees, there were several oddly shaped devices to reduce the carbon dioxide level in the room, and a large cup filled with ice. Throughout the entire conversation, he kept chewing on ice cubes. (It's said this is good for cognition.)

I also interacted with CEO Alex Karp and spoke with other members of the leadership team. I probably don't need to convince you that Karp is quirky — just watch one of his interviews. I can't say what Karp and I talked about, but a 2012 interview captured his style well:

I like to meet candidates without any data: no resume, no prior discussion or job description, just the candidate and me in a room. I ask a fairly random question that has nothing to do with anything they might do at Palantir. Then I observe how they break down the problem, and whether they realize there can be many different ways of looking at the same thing. I like to keep interviews short, about 10 minutes. Otherwise, people slip into the answering patterns they've learned, and you can't really get to know who they are.

My interviews often had nothing to do with the job or software — in one interview, we spent an hour discussing Wittgenstein. Note that both Peter Thiel and Alex Karp were philosophy majors. Thiel's lecture notes (CS183: Startup - Peter Thiel Class Notes) were published publicly a while ago, and they discussed Shakespeare, Tolstoy, Girard (little known at the time, now a cliché), and more.

This intellectual grandeur and fierce competitiveness was a perfect fit for me. In fact, it's still hard to find today — many have imitated the "hardcore" work culture and the "this is the Marines" atmosphere, but few have the intellectual atmosphere, that feeling of engaging with rich ideas. This is hard to imitate — your founders and early employees must be genuinely interesting intellectual thinkers. The major companies that come to mind today doing this well are OpenAI and Anthropic. It's no surprise that these companies attract a huge amount of talent.

2. Forward deployed

When I joined, Palantir was divided into two types of engineers:

1. Engineers who worked with customers, sometimes called FDEs, i.e., Forward Deployed Engineers.

2. Engineers who worked on the core product teams (Product Development - PD), who rarely visited customers.

FDEs were generally expected to "go on-site" to work at customers' offices 3-4 days a week, which meant frequent travel. This was very unusual for a Silicon Valley company.

There is a lot to unpack about this model, but the core idea is that you would gain a deep understanding of the business processes of challenging industries (manufacturing, healthcare, intelligence, aerospace, etc.), and then use that knowledge to design software that actually solves problems. The job of PD engineers was to turn the custom solutions built by forward deployed engineers into standardized products, so that these products could be applied more broadly and improve the productivity of the whole company.

This is how the Foundry product originally took shape: FDEs went to customer sites and had to manually do a lot of tedious work, while PD engineers built tools to automate that tedious work. Need to import data from SAP or AWS? Here's Magritte (a data import tool). Need to visualize data? Here's Contour (a point-and-click visualization tool). Need to quickly spin up a web app? Here's Workshop (a Retool-like UI for building web apps). Eventually, around the loose theme of "integrate data and make it useful somehow," you had a truly excellent toolset.

At the time, having customers use these tools was seen as a radical move — they weren't ready — but now this drives over 50% of the company's revenue, and it's called Foundry. To put this in perspective, Palantir pulled off a rare services company → product company transformation: in 2016, describing it as a Silicon Valley services company wasn't entirely off the mark, but by 2024, that description is completely off the mark, because the company successfully leveraged the experience from those early years to build an enterprise data platform, which shows in the gross margin — 80% in 2023. Those are software gross margins. By comparison, Accenture's gross margin is 32%.

Tyler Cowen has a great line, "context is that which is scarce," and you could call it the fundamental insight of this model. Going to the customer site in person—startup guru Steve Blank calls this "getting out of the building"—means you can capture the tacit knowledge of how they actually work, not just the "requirements checklist" model that enterprise software typically relies on. The company believed in this deeply: people would often call in, and we'd have to book a flight the next morning to some very random place; "get on the plane first, ask questions later" was a cultural bias. This led to uncontrollable travel spending for a while—many of us ended up with United Airlines 1K status or the like—but it also meant an intense decade-long learning cycle that ultimately paid off.

My first real customer engagement was with the French aircraft manufacturer Airbus, where I moved to Toulouse for a year, working four days a week at the factory with the manufacturing staff, helping to build a version of our software there.

The CEO told us his biggest problem was scaling up manufacturing of the A350 aircraft. So we ended up building software to directly address that problem. I've sometimes described it as "Asana for manufacturing airplanes." You bring together different data sources—work orders, missing parts, quality issues ("non-conformances")—into one nice interface where users can check off work, see what other teams are doing, where parts are, what the schedule is, and so on. It lets them search (including fuzzy/semantic search) previous quality issues and see how those issues were resolved. These are basic software features, but you've also seen how bad enterprise software can be—just deploying these "best practice" UIs into the real world was extremely powerful. This ultimately helped drive the surge in A350 aircraft manufacturing, successfully increasing the manufacturing rate 4x while maintaining Airbus's high quality standards.

This makes the software hard to describe concisely—it's not just a database or spreadsheet, it's an end-to-end solution for that specific problem, with generality taking a back seat. Your job is to solve the problem, not to worry about overfitting; PD's job is to take whatever you built and generalize it, with the goal of selling it elsewhere.

FDEs tend to write code that gets the job done quickly, which often means—politely speaking—technical debt and hacky workarounds. PD engineers write software that scales cleanly, works for multiple use cases, and doesn't crash. A key "secret" of the company is that creating deep, lasting enterprise value requires both. BD engineers typically have high tolerance and need the social and political skills to embed deeply in an unfamiliar company and win customer trust, plus high efficiency—you need to quickly build something of value so the customer realizes you're the real deal. Customers have extremely low expectations of most software contractors, who are usually implementers of SAP or similar software working on multi-year "waterfall" timelines. So when a ragtag team of twenty-somethings shows up on site and builds real software that people can use within a week or two, people notice.

This two-pronged model forms a powerful engine. Customer teams are usually small (4-5 people) and operate quickly and autonomously; there are many of them, all learning fast, while the core product team's job is to leverage those learnings to build the main platform.

This model tends to work well when we're allowed to work within an organization. The obstacles are mostly political. Whenever you see the government giving Deloitte another $110 million contract to build a website that doesn't work, or disasters like http://healthcare.gov, or the San Francisco Unified School District spending $40 million on a payroll system that doesn't quite work, you're seeing politics triumph over substance. The SpaceX versus NASA comparison is another example.

The world needs more companies like SpaceX and Palantir that differentiate themselves through execution—delivering results—rather than playing political games or building narrow point solutions that fall short of the goal.

3. Secrets

Another key thing FDEs do is data integration, a term that puts most people to sleep. This was (and still is) the company's core business, and for years its importance was underestimated by most observers. In fact, it wasn't until the advent of AI that people began to appreciate how important it is for enterprises to have clean, curated, easily accessible data.

Simply put, "data integration" means: (a) obtaining enterprise data, which usually means negotiating with the "data owners" in the organization, (b) cleaning the data, and sometimes transforming it, to make it usable, and (c) putting it somewhere everyone can access. Much of the foundational software in Palantir's main software platform (Foundry) consists of nothing more than tools to make this task easier and faster.

Why is data integration so hard? Data often exists in formats that aren't easily analyzed by computers—PDF files, notebooks, Excel files, and so on. But often what really blocks us is organizational politics: a team or group controls a critical data source, and their reason for existing is that they hold the keys to that data source; they typically justify their existence in the company by acting as gatekeepers of that data source (and, often, providing analysis of that data). This politics can be a difficult obstacle to overcome, and in some cases led to laughable outcomes—you'd see a company purchase an 8–12 week pilot, and we'd spend the entire 8–12 weeks just getting data access, scrambling to prepare the demo in the final week.

Another "secret" Palantir discovered early on is that the fight over data access stems partly from genuine data security concerns, which can be mitigated by building security controls into the platform's data integration layer—at all levels. This means role-based access controls, row-level policies, security markings, audit trails, and many other data security features that other companies are still catching up on. Because of these features, implementing Palantir typically makes a company's data more secure, not less.

4. A Note on Culture

The company as a whole feels more like a messianic cult than a normal software company. But importantly, criticism appeared to be highly tolerated and welcomed—someone showed me an email in which a junior software engineer engaged in a public and contentious argument with a company director with the entire company (roughly a thousand people) cc'd. As a philosophy graduate with rationalist thinking, this mattered a lot to me—I had no interest in joining an uncritical cult. But a group of skeptics who deeply care and want to debate where the world is heading and how software fits into it—from an existential perspective—that was interesting to me.

I'm not sure if they still do this, but when you joined, they would give you a copy of Impro, The Looming Tower: Al-Qaeda and the Road to 9/11, The Mom Test, and Getting Things Done. I also received an early PDF version of what later became Ray Dalio's Principles. This set the tone. The Looming Tower is obvious—the company was founded partly in reaction to 9/11 and what Peter saw as the inevitable civil liberties infringements that followed, and that context is valuable. But why Impro?

Becoming a successful FDE requires an unusual sensitivity to social dynamics — what you really need to do is collaborate with your counterparts at the customer company (or government) at the highest level and win their trust, which often requires playing political games. Improv is popular among nerds partly because it mechanically breaks down social behavior. Corporate vocabulary is full of improv terms — "casting" is one example. Johnstone discussed how the same actor can play a "high status" or "low status" role just by changing one part of their physical behavior — for example, keeping your head still while talking is high status, while frequently shaking your head side to side is low status. Standing upright with hands visible is high status; hunching over with hands in your pockets is low status. And so on. If you don't know all of this, your odds of succeeding in a customer environment are slim. Which means you're unlikely to integrate customer data or get people to use your software. Which means failure.

This is one reason former FDEs tend to make excellent founders. (Even though Google has roughly 50 times as many employees as Palantir, in every YC batch, former Palantir employees usually outnumber former Google employees.) Great founders have an intuition for reading the room, group dynamics, and power. This rarely gets mentioned, but it's crucial: founding a successful company is a matter of negotiating, one encounter after another, and ultimately winning in aggregate. Recruiting, sales, and fundraising are all essentially negotiations. Without these intuitions about human behavior, it's hard to excel at negotiation. This is something Palantir taught its FDEs that's hard to learn anywhere else in Silicon Valley.

The other is that FDEs must be good at understanding things. Your effectiveness is directly proportional to how quickly you learn the customer's language and develop a deep understanding of how their business operates. If you're working with a hospital, you'll quickly learn to talk about capacity management and patient throughput, not just say "helping you improve your healthcare." The same goes for drug discovery, health insurance, informatics, cancer immunotherapy, and so on; they all have specialized vocabularies, and those who do well tend to be good at learning these vocabularies quickly.

One of my favorite insights from Tyler Cowen's book *Talent* is that the most talented people tend to develop their own vocabulary and memes, which become entry points into an entire intellectual world built by that person. Tyler himself is of course a great example of this. Any MR reader can immediately name more than ten Tylerisms — "model this," "the environment is the thing that's scarce," "solve for the equilibrium," "the Great Stagnation" are all examples. You can find others who are good at this. Thiel is one. Elon is too ("multiplanetary species," "the light of consciousness," etc. are all memes). Trump, Yudkowsky, gwern, SSC, Paul Graham — they all frequently create memes. It turns out this is a good indicator of influence.

The same insight applies to companies, and Palantir has its own vast set of jargon, some of it so obscure that "what does Palantir actually do?" became a meme on the internet. "Ontology" is an old term, but there's also "impl," "artist's colony," "composite," "Room 36," "points," "metabolic pain," "gamma radiation," and more. The point isn't to explain all these terms, each of which compresses a whole set of rich insights; rather, when you're looking for a company to join, it's not a bad heuristic to look for a rich internal language or vocabulary that helps you think about problems in more interesting ways.

When the name Palantir comes up, most people think of Peter Thiel. But many of these terms actually came from early employees, especially Shyam Sankar, the company's current president. Nevertheless, Peter's influence on the company culture remains profound, even though he wasn't involved in running the company during my time there. This document written by Joe Lonsdale (Lessons from Peter Thiel), originally an internal document that was made public at some point, shows the kind of cultural principles the company had.

One point I attribute to Peter is not giving people titles. When I was there, almost everyone had the title "forward deployed engineer," plus five or six supervisors and the CEO. Occasionally someone would come up with a different title (someone I knew called himself "Head of Special Situations," which I found amusing), but these titles never really caught on. This traces easily back to Peter's Girardian beliefs: if you create titles, people start to covet them, which eventually creates competitive politics within the company and undermines internal cohesion. It's better to give everyone the same title and let them focus on the mission.

There are many critiques of the "flat management" stance — The Tyranny of Structurelessness is a good example — and it seems to have fallen out of fashion in modern startups, where you quickly see CEOs, COOs, VPs, founding engineers, and so on. But in my experience, this management style worked well at Palantir. Some people were more influential than others, but that influence was usually based on some impressive accomplishment, and most importantly, no one could tell anyone else what to do. So if someone had influence or thought your idea was stupid, it didn't matter — you could ignore them and do what you believed was right. Beyond that, the culture celebrated people with stories like an engineer who ignored a supervisor and built something that ended up becoming critical infrastructure, which was held up as a role model to emulate.

The price the company paid for this was that it often felt like there was no clear strategy or direction — more like a bunch of smart people building their own little territories in a petri dish and then evolving at will. But its creativity was remarkable. People underestimate how many novel UI concepts and ideas this company produced. Only some of them now have non-Palantir equivalents; for example, Hex, Retool, and Airflow all contain parts originally developed at Palantir. Now the company is doing the same thing in AI — its tools for deploying large language models at large enterprises are very powerful.

The "no titles" approach also meant that people's popularity within the company could change quickly. Because everyone had the same title, you had to measure influence by other means, like "who is currently close with this director" or "who is leading this seemingly important product project," rather than "this person is the VP of such-and-such division." The result was a large-scale hero-and-asshole roller coaster — someone would be very influential for a while, then mysteriously disappear, no longer doing any visible work for months, and you could never quite figure out what happened.

5. The Bat Signal

Another idea I attribute to Peter is the talent signal. Recruiting great talent is hard; you need a distinctive talent source. If you're just competing with Facebook/Google every year for the same batch of Stanford CS graduates, you will fail. This means you need a set of people who (a) are particularly interested in joining your company rather than others, and (b) a way to reach them at scale. Palantir had several differentiated recruiting sources.

First, at the time there were many people who supported defense/intelligence work, which was not popular back then, which meant there were more than usual smart engineers from, say, the Midwest or red states, as well as many smart former military and former CIA/NSA personnel who wanted to serve their country while also seeing the appeal of working at a Silicon Valley company. On my first day at the company, I attended my team's internal onboarding, and there was another person who looked a bit older than me. I asked him what he did before coming to Palantir. He looked at me deadpan and said: "I worked at the Agency for 15 years." Then I was introduced to my first lead, who had been a SWAT officer (!) in Ohio and a veteran.

There are many such people, many extremely talented, and most of them did not join Google. Palantir was the only true "beacon" for this type of person—a company that openly supported the military, patriotism, and so on, which was very uncool at the time. This created an efficient, distinctive signal. (Now there's Anduril, along with a large number of defense and manufacturing startups).

Second, you had to be a bit quirky to be willing to join the company, at least after the initial hype faded (especially during the Trump administration, when the company became a lightning rod). This was partly because this kind of aggressive "mission-focused" branding was not yet common at the time, and the company made no secret of the fact that employees worked long hours, were paid below market rates, and frequently had to travel. Meanwhile, we were kicked out of Silicon Valley career fairs for working with the government. All of this filtered for a specific type of person: someone who could think independently and would not be overly swayed by negative news.

6. Ethics

Ethics is a fascinating topic. The company unapologetically supports the West, and even though I disagree with some things the military does, I have no problem supporting the company's work helping the military.

But doesn't the military sometimes do bad things? Of course—I opposed the Iraq War. This gets to the heart of the matter: working at the company is neither entirely morally good—because sometimes we might help institutions whose goals I disagree with—nor entirely bad: the government does a lot of good, and helping it do things more efficiently by providing non-terrible software is a noble thing. One way to clarify this is to break the company's work down into three categories—these categories are imperfect:

  • Morally neutral. Ordinary corporate work, like FedEx, CVS, financial companies, tech companies, etc. Some people might object to this, but overall people feel fine about these things.
  • Clearly good. For example, working with the CDC to fight a pandemic; working with the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children to combat child pornography; and so on. Most people would agree these are worthwhile endeavors.
  • Gray areas. I mean "work involving morally thorny, difficult decisions": for example, health insurance, immigration enforcement, oil companies, the military, intelligence agencies, police/crime, etc.

Every engineer faces a choice: you can work on things like Google Search or the Facebook News Feed, which look like slightly better things and basically fall into the first category. Or you can go work on things in the second category, like GiveDirectly or OpenPhilanthropy.

The key accusation against Palantir seems to be "you shouldn't work on third-category things, because sometimes that involves making morally wrong decisions." One example is immigration enforcement during 2016–2020, which many people felt uncomfortable about.

But in my view, completely ignoring the third category and disengaging from it is equally an abdication of responsibility. Third-category institutions need to exist. America is defended by people with guns. Police must carry out the task of fighting crime, and—in my experience—even people who are morally uncomfortable with certain aspects of policing will quickly call the police if their own home is burglarized. Oil companies must provide energy. Health insurance companies must constantly make difficult decisions. Yes, all of these things have unpleasant aspects. But should we completely disengage from these institutions and let them figure things out on their own?

I don't think there is a clear answer to whether one should work with all three categories of customers; it needs to be judged case by case. Palantir's answer is "we will work with most third-category organizations, unless they are clearly bad, and let them move in a good direction over time." Thus:

  • On the ICE issue, they decoupled from ERO (Enforcement and Removal Operations) during the Trump era while continuing to work with HSI (Homeland Security Investigations).
  • They work with most other third-category organizations, on the grounds that these organizations mostly do good in the world, even though it's easy to point out the bad things they do. I can't be specific, but Palantir software has partially prevented multiple terrorist attacks. I think this fact alone is enough to justify this position.

For many people, this is an uncomfortable position, precisely because you cannot guarantee you are always doing 100% good. To some extent, you are subject to history, and you are betting that (a) the good done will outweigh the bad, and (b) being in the room is better than not being in it.

That's good enough for me. Others prefer to go elsewhere.

Of course, the danger of this position is that it becomes a fully generic argument for doing whatever the power structure wants to do. You're just amplifying existing processes. This is where "case by case" comes in: there is no generic answer; you have to analyze each specific issue. As for me, I spent most of my time there working in healthcare and biology, and I am satisfied with my contributions. I'd bet the people who prevented terrorist attacks are also satisfied with their contributions. Or the people who distributed medicine during the pandemic.

Even though the tide has turned and it's now fashionable to work on these "thorny" areas, these questions remain relevant for technologists. AI is a good example—many people feel uneasy about some of the consequences of deploying AI. Maybe AI will be used for hacking; maybe deepfakes will make the world worse in various ways; maybe it will cause job losses. But AI also has major benefits.

Just like Palantir, working on AI research may not be 100% morally right, nor 100% evil. Not engaging with it—or calling for a pause/stop, which is mere fantasy—is unlikely to be the optimal position. Even if you don't work at OpenAI or Anthropic, if you're someone who might work on AI-related issues, you probably want to be involved in some way. Some cases are simple: building evals, working on alignment, improving societal resilience. But my point here is that the gray areas are also worth engaging with: working on government AI policy. Deploying AI into fields like healthcare. Of course, it will be difficult. Nevertheless, press forward.

When I think of the most influential people in AI today, almost all of them are in the room—whether at AI labs, in government, or at an influential think tank. I'd rather be one of them than one of the armchair critics. Of course, this will involve difficult decisions. But even if you later have to leave and sound the alarm, it's still better than not being in the room when things happen.

7. What's next?

Am I still bullish on the company? The huge productivity gains of this AI cycle will come when AI starts providing leverage for the large companies and enterprises of this era—in manufacturing, defense, logistics, healthcare, and other industries. Palantir has spent a decade working with these companies. AI agents will eventually drive many core business workflows, and these agents will depend on read-write access to critical business data. Spending a decade integrating enterprise data is a key foundation for deploying AI into enterprises. The opportunity is enormous.
