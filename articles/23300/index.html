Written by Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, this article argues that low engineer salaries in Japan stem from structural issues: multi-layer subcontracting that strips margins at each tier, SES firms taking 35-40% of monthly billing, engineers stuck in low-value tasks, seniority-based pay, and management treating IT as a cost. It outlines four paths to higher income—learning in-demand skills like generative AI and cloud architecture, moving to upstream roles, switching employers, or going freelance—and includes rate tables, case studies, risk mitigation, and a promotion of DeFactory's free rate diagnostic.

Key points

  • Japan's average IT engineer salary (~4.62M yen) barely exceeds the all-industry average.
  • Layered subcontracting and SES margins of 35-40% suppress take-home pay.
  • Freelance engineers average ~7.65M yen, about 65% above salaried peers.
  • Generative AI and cloud skills command monthly rates of 800K-1.3M yen.

The answer to the question "Why don't engineers' salaries rise, even though they are rare specialists?" lies in structural problems deeply rooted in Japan's IT industry. There are barriers that are difficult to overcome through individual skill and effort alone.

Limited to engineers with 5+ years of practical experience

High-rate, direct-contract project information

The biggest problem facing Japan's IT industry is the multi-layered subcontracting structure. In this structure, projects won by major SIers (system integrators) or prime contractors are subcontracted step by step to first-tier, second-tier, and third-tier subcontractors, with each layer taking a margin (intermediary fee).

クライアント企業 → 元請けSIer → 1次下請け → 2次下請け → 実務エンジニア

In a survey conducted by the Japan Fair Trade Commission, 33.5% of the lowest-tier subcontractors answered that they "receive orders from intermediary brokers that perform no actual work," revealing the reality that the payout rate to engineers is drastically reduced.

A concrete illustration of how margins stack up

LayerContract amount (example)Margin ratePayment to next layer

First-tier → Second-tier subcontractor5.6 million yen20%4.48 million yen
Second-tier → Working engineer4.48 million yen—4.48 million yen or less

Even for a project originally worth 10 million yen, it is not uncommon for less than half to actually reach the engineer doing the work.

An SES contract is a type of service agreement in which engineers are dispatched to client sites for a fixed period. Since engineers often work on-site at clients while remaining full-time employees of the SES company, they are bound by their employment relationship with the SES company.

The reality of the payout rate to engineers under SES contracts

  • Typical monthly rate for SES projects: 600,000–800,000 yen
  • Margin taken by SES companies: 35–40%
  • Amount paid to engineers: about 60% of the monthly rate (equivalent to 360,000–480,000 yen)

Even if you join a project with a monthly rate of 800,000 yen, if the SES company's margin is 40%, the engineer effectively receives 480,000 yen (in monthly salary terms). Converted to annual income, that is 5.76 million yen, but since many SES companies have limited bonus and raise systems, actual annual income tends to be lower than that.

The FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is the role attempting to break through the structural limitations of this SES-style man-month rate model at the job-type level.

FDEs base their value provision not on "compensation for working hours" but on "contribution to clients' business results." Because they directly commit to improving clients' KPIs, reducing churn rates, and increasing sales, there is structurally no ceiling in the form of a man-month rate cap.

In Japan as well, engineers are emerging who secure freelance FDE projects in the 1.2–1.5 million yen per month class, and this career path is attracting attention as a way to fundamentally surpass the SES-style rate barrier.

Engineers positioned at the bottom of the multi-layered subcontracting chain tend to be assigned work skewed toward testing, documentation, and maintenance/monitoring. They have few opportunities to engage in high-value upstream processes such as requirements definition, design, and architecture building, causing their skills to stagnate.

If skills do not grow, market value does not rise, and there are no grounds for rate negotiations — this vicious cycle is the typical pattern of engineers who cannot escape the low-wage structure.

At major Japanese SIers and traditional IT companies, "years of service" is often the primary factor determining salary rather than technical skills. It is a system in which salary does not increase unless your job grade rises, even if you have highly marketable skills.

In the United States, it is not uncommon for even young engineers to earn over 10 million yen annually based on their skills and achievements (Reference: Extreme — Differences in Annual Income Between Overseas and Japan), but in Japan, institutional barriers hinder income growth even for those with equivalent skills.

At many Japanese companies, IT and software development tends to be viewed as "a cost that supports the business." In contrast, in the US, GAFAM and startups position engineers as "core talent that creates business value," and this difference in perception leads to a large gap in salary levels (Reference: Diamond Zai: Comparison of Overseas Engineer Salaries).

In an environment where many executives view IT investment as "a means of cost reduction," engineer compensation is easily suppressed, which is one factor behind the outflow of talented engineers to overseas or independent work.
