What is a forward deployed engineer? The definition from 9 FDE interviews (2026)

Tandem · 2026-08-10 · 9 min read

Summary

Based on nine interviews with practicing FDEs in mid-2026, Tandem defines the role as an engineer who spends roughly half their time on customer discovery and half building solutions for that specific customer. The dividing line from implementation roles is custom code. Originating at Palantir, the title grew over 800% from January to September 2025, with AWS committing $1 billion and Microsoft $2.5 billion. Yet many postings are renamed implementation roles, and scoping—deciding what to build—turns out to be the hardest part.

Key points

  • An FDE splits time between customer discovery and building custom solutions.
  • Custom code, not the title, separates FDEs from implementation roles.
  • Postings grew 800%+ in nine months; AWS, Microsoft, and Salesforce invested heavily.
  • Scoping is harder than building and is where FDEs wash out.
  • Five signals distinguish real FDE functions from renamed services teams.

TL;DR: A forward deployed engineer is an engineer who spends roughly half their time with customers understanding the problem and half building the solution for that specific customer. The dividing line versus implementation roles is custom code. The title was coined at Palantir and job postings grew more than 800% from January to September 2025, but our interviews with nine practicing FDEs found the role is widely misapplied: plenty of companies have simply renamed their implementation managers. Here's the real definition, from the people doing the work.

"Forward deployed engineer" went from Palantir jargon to the most in-demand title in enterprise software in about eighteen months. AWS committed $1 billion to an organization built around it. Microsoft launched a $2.5 billion unit staffing 6,000 embedded experts. Adam Ward, Head of Talent at Cursor, named FDE the hardest role to hire right now, harder even than AI researchers, because researchers are a defined bracket and this role is still nebulous.

Nebulous is the right word. Between June and August 2026 we interviewed nine practicing FDEs and the leaders standing up FDE programs, across claims automation, process automation, fintech, healthcare, and data platforms. What follows is the definition that survived contact with all nine conversations, where the market definition gets it wrong, and how to tell a real FDE function from a renamed services team.

The one-line definition

A forward deployed engineer is an engineer who spends roughly half their time with customers understanding the problem and half building the solution for that specific customer.

The cleanest version came from an FDE at a company that turns natural language into deterministic code for Fortune 500 process automation: half his day is spent with customers understanding the problem and the process as it exists today, and the other half building it with his team. Not sales support. Not implementation with a better title. An engineer who does discovery and then writes the code.

The dividing line is custom code

Here is the test that separates FDEs from every adjacent role: does the job require building something that doesn't exist yet, for one customer, because that customer is worth building it for?

If the work is configuring the product or wiring up its API, that's implementation, regardless of what the title says. Implementation is essential work, and we cover those roles in depth elsewhere, but it operates within the product's existing surface. An FDE exists because the product's surface isn't enough for this customer, and an engineer has to extend it on site.

This matches the role's origin. Palantir pioneered the position for government customers whose problems couldn't be specified in advance or solved by generic software. Until 2016, Palantir had more FDEs (internally, "Deltas") than traditional software engineers. The model spread because AI deployments recreated the same condition everywhere: a capable core product and a messy customer environment it doesn't fit yet.

Where FDEs come from

Across our interviews the pattern held: FDEs are the full-stack and platform engineers of three to five years ago, plus former founders and robotics people. One described himself as "more of an engineer than forward deployed." They move out of the terminal and toward the customer, and for the right person that unlocks something. For the wrong one it doesn't.

Ward's public read matches ours on this point: he notes FDEs mostly come from full-stack backgrounds, "moving out of the terminal," and that the narrow, over-specialized IC is the waning profile. Where we diverge from Ward is the center of gravity. He frames the role around deploying a technical product and helping customers optimize their spend. Nine interviews put the weight somewhere else entirely: on scoping and building. Deployment is the visible part. Deciding what to build is the job.

Scoping is the part nobody prepares them for

The strongest finding across our research: deciding what to build turns out to be harder than building it. The people on the ground rarely have the full picture, and the stated requirement is rarely the real one.

A former professional services lead at a major data platform told us her organization was pushing FDEs to also act as solution architects, designing in a business context rather than just a technical one, and expected to lose roughly a third of them because they couldn't make that jump. That is the actual bar for the role, and it appears in no job description we've seen.

Three things eat the FDE's day

The day-to-day reality that came up in interview after interview has little to do with the glamorous version of the role:

  • Context switching across parallel accounts. FDEs typically carry 3 to 8 concurrent customers. One described talking to four clients a day and playing "broken telephone with outdated documentation." Another reported roughly three hours of prep per customer meeting, most of it reconstructing what was promised and decided. This is the problem a project brain exists to solve, and it's why we built Tandem's account brain to maintain that state automatically. More on this in the project brain for FDEs.
  • Access. Getting anything approved inside a client's environment takes weeks to months. It was described to us as the single biggest frustration of the job. Security reviews, credentials, VPNs, procurement: the engineering is often the fast part.
  • Change management. FDEs are frequently automating the job of the person sitting across the table. That person has heard the bad AI stories. You have to prove you're above human accuracy before anyone cooperates.

None of these three appeared in the popular "token maxing to cost optimization" narrative about FDE work. That angle appeared in zero of our nine interviews. Unless you're an AI infrastructure vendor, drop it.

The title inflation problem

FDE is trendy, and plenty of companies have simply renamed their implementation managers.

A senior professional services director at a billing platform, currently standing up an FDE program himself, put it plainly: everyone has their own model, and some companies are doing exactly what professional services always did and calling it forward deployed. His open question wasn't staffing but time-boxing, because an open-scope engagement model is very easy to misuse in both directions: the customer treats the FDE as free consulting, or the vendor uses "forward deployed" to justify unscoped services revenue.

We've seen the mismatch inside our own research CRM: a person doing textbook FDE work titled "Implementation lead," and a team lead titled FDE doing classic delivery management. The title tells you almost nothing. The custom-code test tells you everything. Five signals separate a real FDE function from a renamed services team:

  • Core output. Real FDE function: custom code and integrations shipped into customer environments. Renamed services team: configuration, training, project management.
  • Reporting line. Real: engineering or a dedicated FDE org. Renamed: services, CS, or sales.
  • Feedback loop. Real: builds feed the core product roadmap. Renamed: learnings stay in the account.
  • Scoping. Real: the FDE does discovery and defines what to build. Renamed: scope arrives from sales as an SOW.
  • Success metric. Real: customer outcome and product learnings. Renamed: utilization and billable hours.

How big is this, really

The demand is not hype. Job postings for the role grew more than 800% between January and September 2025 per Financial Times data, and Business Insider reported 700%+ year-over-year growth into 2026. Salesforce committed to 1,000 FDEs. AWS put $1 billion behind an embedded-engineer organization, and Microsoft's Frontier Company is a $2.5 billion bet on 6,000 of them. If you are on the hiring side of that curve, the manager's guide to hiring FDEs covers comp, screens, and sourcing.

Meanwhile the supply side is improvising. The role is being invented in real time: several of the people we interviewed were the first or third FDE at their company, actively defining what the job means. There is no established FDE community yet. They find each other on Luma. How companies organize these first hires is its own unsolved question, mapped in our guide to FDE team structure.

That gap between demand and definition is exactly why the title inflation matters. When a role grows 800% in nine months, most of the growth is relabeling.

What FDEs actually need (tooling honesty)

Ask FDEs what tooling they're missing and you will not hear "another project tracker." Their existing tools track the work without doing any of it, and every status field is a tax on the half of their day that should be spent building.

What came up instead: a current picture of each account they can trust (what was promised, what's blocking, who went quiet), and leverage on the repeated non-engineering work: follow-ups, status reports, CRM hygiene, meeting recaps. FAQs

What is a forward deployed engineer in one sentence?

An engineer who spends roughly half their time with customers understanding the problem and half building the solution for that specific customer.

How is an FDE different from a solutions engineer or implementation engineer?

Solutions engineers support the sales cycle with demos and architecture proposals. Implementation engineers configure the product and integrate its APIs. FDEs write custom code that doesn't exist yet for one customer's environment, and their builds feed back into the product roadmap. The dividing line is custom code. Full comparison here.

Where did the forward deployed engineer role come from?

Palantir pioneered it for government and enterprise customers whose problems couldn't be solved by generic software, with the earliest published use of the title appearing in 2010. The AI deployment wave took the model mainstream from 2025 onward.

Why is the role suddenly in such demand?

AI products created a permanent gap between demo and production inside messy customer environments. Someone technical has to close that gap on site. Postings grew more than 800% in 2025, and AWS, Microsoft, Salesforce, OpenAI, and Anthropic all built dedicated functions.

Is every FDE job posting a real FDE role?

No. A significant share are professional services or implementation roles relabeled for the market. Apply the custom-code test: if the job is configuring the product rather than building beyond it, it's implementation. Ask in the interview what the last thing the team built for a single customer was, and whether it fed the product roadmap.

What do FDEs struggle with most?

Three things came up repeatedly in our interviews: reconstructing account context across parallel customers, waiting weeks to months for access inside client environments, and change management when automating the job of the person across the table. Scoping in a business context is the skill that washes people out.

Key terms

  • Forward deployed engineer (FDE): An engineer embedded with customers who splits time between discovery and building custom solutions for that specific customer.
  • Scoping: Deciding what to build. In FDE work, harder than the building, because stated requirements rarely match real ones.
  • Time-boxing: Putting boundaries on an open-scope engagement so it serves both the customer outcome and the product.
  • Handoff: The transfer of account context between roles (sales to FDE, FDE to CS), where commitments are most often dropped.
  • Account brain: A continuously updated, sourced picture of a customer account. See what is a project brain.
  • Change management: The human side of deployment: earning cooperation from the people whose workflow the software changes.
  • SOW (statement of work): The contracted scope of a delivery project. FDE work regularly and legitimately outgrows it.
