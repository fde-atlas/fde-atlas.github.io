FDE Competency Model: A Four-Layer Breakdown of Engineering, AI Application, Business Communication, and On-Site Adaptability

Original title: FDE 能力模型：工程、AI 应用、业务沟通、现场应变四层拆解

FDE中国 · 2026-09-07 · 4 min read

Summary

FDEChina's editorial team breaks FDE competency into four interdependent layers: engineering fundamentals, applied AI skills, business communication, and on-site adaptability. It argues evals are the most underrated differentiator—defining quantitative metrics and regression cases is what moves features from demo to production—and that bidirectional translation between clients and engineering teams is equally overlooked. On-site adaptability can't be learned through exercises, only real projects, so newcomers should avoid unsupported solo deployments. The article ends with a radar self-assessment table, advising readers to fix their weakest layer first and sequence transition prep as engineering, evals, then communication.

Key points

  • Four layers are interdependent, not parallel labels; engineering is the foundation.
  • Evals separate strong candidates: define quantitative metrics and regression cases per business scenario.
  • Business communication means bidirectional translation that must satisfy both clients and engineers.
  • On-site adaptability only comes from real projects; avoid unsupported solo deployments as a beginner.
  • Score yourself on the radar table, fix the lowest layer first, retest in three months.

Introduction

Publicly visible job postings and frontline practice show that the skill requirements for FDEs are rarely single-point: you must be able to independently write production code while also understanding the business and driving things forward autonomously on-site with incomplete information. Section 7 of the Beginner's Guide summarizes FDE capabilities into four layers; this article breaks them down layer by layer—covering the components, self-test questions, and improvement paths for each, ending with a scoreable radar self-assessment chart.

How the Four Layers Relate

The four layers are not four parallel labels but a structure of dependencies: engineering fundamentals are the foundation, AI application capability is built on top of engineering ability, business communication determines whether a solution holds up, and on-site adaptability determines whether the other three layers can function properly in chaotic environments. A profile with one standout layer and the others collapsed is more likely to run into problems on-site than a balanced but unremarkable profile across all four.

Layer 1: Engineering Fundamentals

Components: Solid programming and debugging skills, basic system design ability, version control and testing habits, and the ability to independently deliver production-grade code. This layer determines your delivery floor—on-site there is no backup, and code quality directly becomes customer trust.

Self-test questions: Without an existing framework or support from colleagues, can you independently build a small system with a database and APIs, deploy it, and take it live? When something breaks in production, is your first reaction to check the logs and locate the issue, or to wait for someone else to handle it?

Improvement path: Fill gaps with delivery as the goal. Building a complete end-to-end small project (including deployment and monitoring) is closer to on-site requirements than grinding practice problems; keep writing even the most basic tests for your code—the habit will pay off in large projects.

Layer 2: AI Application Capability

Components: Understanding the capability boundaries and failure modes of large models, being familiar with the applicable scenarios of common paradigms such as prompt engineering, RAG, and agents, and knowing eval methods that turn "does it work well" into measurable metrics.

Self-test questions: Given a new requirement, can you judge whether it should be solved with prompts, RAG, or fine-tuning? Before an AI feature you own goes live, is there a fixed set of eval cases you can run for regression?

Improvement path: Evals are a widely underrated piece—first learn to define 3 quantitative metrics for a business scenario and build regression test cases, then talk about model selection. For reading material, prioritize first-hand retrospectives from model vendors' documentation and engineering blogs, especially the ones that write about failure cases.

Layer 3: Business Communication

Components: Bidirectional translation ability—converting vague business requests into technical solutions, and translating technical constraints back into business impact; it also includes plain, unglamorous actions like meeting preparation, minutes, and expectation management.

Self-test questions: When a business stakeholder says "we want to add intelligence," can you turn that sentence into a definable requirement with three follow-up questions? Can you explain to a customer, without jargon, why a certain solution can't be done and what it would cost?

Improvement path: Before every customer conversation, write down "the three questions to clarify this time," and send minutes within 24 hours after the meeting; practice describing the value of technical solutions using the customer's business metrics (cost, time, error rate).

Layer 4: On-Site Adaptability

Components: The judgment to keep moving forward autonomously when information is incomplete, expectation and risk management, and the ability to replan after your plan is disrupted by unexpected situations at the customer site.

Self-test questions: The customer's point of contact suddenly changes, key data can't be obtained, the system breaks the night before a demo—can you come up with a response plan without escalating for help, and keep the project moving?

Improvement path: Prepare backup plans for key milestones in advance (what if the data can't be obtained, what if the point of contact won't cooperate); after handling each surprise, write a two-line retrospective, gradually building your own library of contingency plans.

Four-Layer Radar Self-Assessment Chart

Score yourself 1-5 (1 = essentially missing, 5 = can independently lead a team); look at balance in the total score, and at priority in the lowest score:

Summary and Next Steps

The scarcity of the four-layer capabilities lies in their combination, not in any single item: people who are simultaneously strong on both the engineering and communication sides are a minority on any team.
