Why AI Companies Are Betting on Forward Deployed Engineers

The business case for forward deployed engineering in the AI era: services versus software margins, moats, deployment companies and the debate over whether the model scales.

AI companies hire forward deployed engineers (FDEs) because a capable model is not yet a working enterprise system: someone has to wire it into messy data and legacy tools, define what a correct answer looks like, and keep it reliable once real work depends on it. As frontier models converge in capability, that last mile is where vendors win accounts and build switching costs, so labs, cloud providers and startups are all building deployment teams.

The bet has a price. Embedded engineers look like services revenue, and services have never been valued like software. The open question is whether forward deployment compounds into product, as it did at Palantir, or rebuilds the consulting industry around better models.

Why LLM products need deployment work

Andreessen Horowitz's 2025 essay argues that startups trying to replace systems of record with thin, wrapper-like agents often watch them break, and that enterprise AI carries a heavier implementation burden than cloud software did, because models only deliver value with deep integrations and context: historical records, business logic, internal APIs. Its analogy for enterprise buyers is "your grandma getting an iPhone": keen to use it, unable to set it up alone.

In practice the deployment work falls into a few buckets:

  • Data and integration: connecting models to internal databases, document stores, permissions and the tools people already use.
  • Evals: turning a fuzzy business goal into a test set before anything ships. AY Automate's write-up describes the loop as audit the real workflow, build an evaluation set, then deploy with gradually increasing autonomy.
  • Production reliability: handling the failure modes that only appear at scale, with fallbacks and human review where errors are expensive.
  • Adoption: redesigning the workflow around the system and earning the trust of skeptical operators.

In its launch announcement, OpenAI describes its own engagements the same way: a diagnostic of where AI creates value, a short list of priority workflows, then designing, building, testing and deploying systems that connect its models to the customer's data, tools, controls and processes.

Trading margin for moat

The a16z essay frames the trade in its title. Product-led companies win on gross margin, but Salesforce, ServiceNow and Workday became dominant during the move to cloud by doing heavy implementation work that made them hard to remove. AI startups that send FDEs accept lower early margins for the same kind of integration depth.

What separates this from classic professional services is where the learning goes. Plank's comparison puts it simply: a services engineer implements a fixed statement of work and bills hours, while an FDE works open-ended against an outcome, and the patterns they discover flow back into the product so the next deployment is cheaper. A 2026 Goldman Sachs note, summarized by TechFlow, describes Palantir's version of this flywheel: FDEs build client-specific workflows, product engineers standardize what works, and the result ships across the platform. The Palantir model page covers how that loop produced Foundry.

The deployment company

In 2026 the idea became corporate structure. OpenAI launched the OpenAI Deployment Company on May 11, majority-owned by OpenAI with more than $4 billion in initial investment from 19 partners led by TPG, including McKinsey, Bain & Company and Capgemini. It agreed to acquire the applied AI consultancy Tomoro, bringing about 150 FDEs and deployment specialists from day one. FourWeekMBA's analysis reports, citing TechCrunch, that Anthropic set up its own deployment joint venture and that AWS announced a $1 billion internal FDE organization on June 30, 2026, keeping the work in-house rather than spinning it out. Its argument: when models return roughly equivalent outputs, advantage moves to whoever implements fastest and most deeply.

The skeptic case

The objections are mostly about scale:

  • Every new customer needs people, so revenue tracks headcount unless field work keeps turning into product.
  • Services-heavy revenue gets valued like consulting, not software.
  • The title is easy to borrow. A team that only implements statements of work is professional services under a new name.
  • Plank notes that fixed-scope services remain the right tool when the work is genuinely known in advance.

The other open question is automation. Palantir is already turning deployment know-how into agents: its AI FDE operates Foundry from natural-language requests, and the Goldman note describes Palantir's AIP Evolve coordinating such agents to test changes and evaluate models across clients. See FDEs and AI agents for that debate, and open FDE roles for who is hiring now.

FAQ

Why are forward deployed engineers in demand at AI companies?

Enterprise AI projects stall between demo and production. Models need access to internal data, integration with existing systems, evaluation against real cases and ongoing tuning before a business will rely on them. FDEs do that work inside the customer, and as model quality converges, deployment speed and depth become a main way AI vendors compete for and keep enterprise accounts.

What is the OpenAI Deployment Company?

It is a company OpenAI launched in May 2026 to embed forward deployed engineers in large organizations and build production AI systems with them. OpenAI holds majority ownership and control, and the venture launched with more than $4 billion from 19 investment firms, consultancies and system integrators. Its founding team came largely through the acquisition of Tomoro, an applied AI consultancy.

Is forward deployed engineering just professional services?

Not by design. Professional services delivers a fixed scope, bills by the hour and keeps what it learns inside the engagement. Forward deployed engineering is meant to work toward an outcome and feed recurring patterns back into the vendor's product. In practice the daily work can look similar, so the test is whether deployments get cheaper over time. See FDE vs solutions engineer for adjacent roles.

Do FDE-heavy companies have lower margins?

Usually yes, at least early. Engineers embedded with customers cost more than self-serve onboarding, which is why a16z describes the strategy as trading margin for moat. The bet is that integration depth makes the product hard to replace and that field work becomes reusable software, which pushes margins back toward software levels as the platform matures.

Will AI agents replace forward deployed engineers?

Some of the work is already being automated. Palantir ships an agent it calls AI FDE that performs Foundry operations from natural-language requests, and Goldman Sachs frames this as letting deployment knowledge scale beyond FDE headcount. Scoping problems, earning customer trust and handling unfamiliar systems remain human work, so the likelier change is each engineer covering more customers.
