FDE vs Solutions Engineer, SWE and Consultants

How the forward deployed engineer differs from solutions engineers, sales engineers, solutions architects, product engineers and consultants.

A forward deployed engineer (FDE) builds and ships production software inside one customer's environment, usually after the contract is signed, and is judged on whether that system gets adopted. A solutions engineer, often called a sales engineer, works before the contract is signed, proving through demos and proofs of concept that the product fits, and is judged on technical wins. Both are technical and customer-facing; they differ in when they engage, what they own, who they report to and how they are paid.

Companies reuse these titles loosely. Some startups call a deeply technical presales job FDE, and some call a genuine deployment job solutions engineering. The Forward Deployed's advice is to read the responsibilities, compensation plan and success metrics rather than the headline.

Where each role sits in the customer journey

Groovy Web's framing is the simplest: the roles are separated by the moment in the customer's journey they own.

  • Solutions or sales engineer: before signature. Discovery, demos, proofs of concept, security questionnaires. Measured on technical wins and deals influenced; sits in the sales org, usually on base plus commission.
  • Solutions architect: designs the target architecture, often pre-sale, sometimes post-sale. The output is a design or prototype someone else builds.
  • Forward deployed engineer: mostly after signature. Writes production code on the customer's systems, owns adoption and the business result, and feeds lessons into the product. Usually base plus equity, no quota.
  • Software engineer: builds the core product for all customers and rarely works with any single one.
  • Consultant or professional services: paid for delivery or time, accountable to the scope in a statement of work.
  • Customer engineer or technical account manager: supports a stable deployment and drives expansion.

FDE vs solutions engineer

Webfuse reduces it to one question: does the job end at signature, or continue through production? Median pay is close, per Webfuse's read of Levels.fyi data; the bigger difference is structure, with commission for the SE and equity for the FDE. Reporting lines follow: the FDE postings Webfuse reviewed mostly placed the role in a dedicated FDE team or in engineering, while SEs typically report into sales.

FDE Dispatch names three points where the jobs diverge: who owns the build, what the person is measured on and how deep they go after launch. Once a system is live, the FDE owns evals, monitoring, fallbacks and the next version. By then the solutions engineer has usually moved on to the next deal.

FDE vs solutions architect

This pair is the hardest to tell apart because both design systems with customers. In an analysis of job postings, Webfuse found the word "production" in 75% of FDE postings and 40% of solutions architect postings, with near-identical median pay. The question that sorts them: does the job end at the design, or at running code on the customer's systems? An architect can hand a design to a delivery team or partner; the FDE is the delivery. The Pragmatic Engineer, cited by The Forward Deployed, makes the same cut: solutions architects are normally advisory, while FDEs write code directly on customer infrastructure.

FDE vs software engineer and consultant

A product software engineer optimizes one codebase for the average customer. An FDE works for one customer at a time and builds what that customer needs, ideally pushing reusable parts back into the product. Palantir's shorthand for the FDE is "one customer, many capabilities".

The consultant comparison is closer than most FDE marketing admits. Webfuse argues the clearest test is the business model: an FDE works for a product vendor that needs the software adopted and renewed, while a consultant sells delivery priced by time or project, and the firm tracks utilization. Webfuse also quotes a16z's Tom Hollands, who argued that Palantir renamed low-status integration work: "The title gave the work prestige." Accenture, McKinsey and Deloitte now run or train FDE teams with the AI labs, so the employer alone no longer settles it.

When one role turns into another

Solutions engineers moving into FDE work already have discovery skills and customer credibility; according to FDE Dispatch, their gap is building, evaluating and deploying production systems. Software engineers have the opposite gap: turning a business problem into an architecture with non-technical users in the room. An FDE job without an outcome metric or a route back to the product slides toward consulting, and one tied to deal timelines slides toward presales. Before accepting the title, ask what share of the week is hands-on coding, who owns the code after launch, whether there is a quota and how many customers one engineer carries. FAQ

Is a forward deployed engineer a sales role?

Not in the usual sense. A genuine FDE role carries no sales quota and starts after the contract is signed; success is measured by adoption and the customer's business result. FDEs still affect revenue, since working deployments drive renewals and expansion, and some companies put the FDE title on technical presales jobs. Check the compensation plan: commission or on-target earnings usually means a sales engineering job under another name.

Which pays more, a forward deployed engineer or a solutions engineer?

Medians are close. Webfuse's October 2026 read of self-reported U.S. Levels.fyi data put sales engineers at $205,000 and FDEs at $216,250. Structure differs more than the total: SEs typically earn base plus commission, FDEs base plus equity. Webfuse also cites an OpenAI FDE posting at $185,000 to $300,000 plus equity. For ranges from current postings, see the salary data.

Can a solutions engineer become a forward deployed engineer?

Yes, and it is a common route in. Solutions engineers already handle discovery, customer communication and technical credibility. The missing piece is usually production engineering: owning code that runs on a customer's systems, writing evals and monitoring, and staying accountable after launch. The most direct way to close that gap is one real deployment you own end to end. The steps are in how to become an FDE.

Is an FDE just a consultant with a new title?

Partly. Day-to-day work can be nearly identical, and a16z's Tom Hollands has argued that the title mainly gave integration work prestige. The structural difference is who pays and where lessons go. FDEs work for a product vendor, the cost of deployment is tied to winning and keeping the software, and field learnings are supposed to reach the product. Consultants bill for delivery, and their lessons go into the firm's playbooks.

Should a startup hire an FDE or a solutions engineer first?

Webfuse's rule is to pick by where deals get stuck. If prospects stall during evaluation because they need demos, a proof of concept and security answers, a solutions engineer helps more. If contracts get signed but deployments stall before production because the product needs the customer's data, tools and permissions to work, hire an FDE. Team design is covered in building an FDE team.
